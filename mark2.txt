SS2 MARKETING LESSON NOTES
FIRST TERM
WEEK 1: TRANSPORTATION IN MARKETING
Topic: Transportation Duration: Double Period (80 Minutes) Instructional Objectives: At the
end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define transportation and explain its role in the supply chain and as a marketing utility.
2. Analyze the various modes of transportation with their specific merits, demerits, and
operational characteristics.
3. Identify and discuss the key documents involved in the transportation of goods.
4. Evaluate the contemporary problems facing the Nigerian transport sector and propose
potential solutions.
1. MEANING AND FUNDAMENTAL ROLE OF TRANSPORTATION
Transportation is the lifeblood of commerce and the engine of marketing. It is the physical
movement of goods and people from one location to another. In marketing, its primary
importance lies in the creation of Utilities.
 Place Utility: Transportation moves goods from regions of surplus (low value) to regions
of scarcity (high value). For example, moving oranges from Benue State (where they are
plentiful and cheap) to Lagos State (where demand is high) creates place utility, thereby
increasing their value and satisfying consumer needs.
 Time Utility: It ensures goods are available exactly when they are needed. For instance,
transporting grains from the harvest season to the dry season for consumption creates
time utility.
2
 Possession Utility: By bridging the gap between producer and consumer, transportation
facilitates the change in ownership.
Logistics vs. Transportation: While often used interchangeably, they are distinct.
Transportation is the actual movement. Logistics is the broader management of the entire
supply chain flow, which includes transportation, warehousing, inventory management,
packaging, and information systems.
2. MODES OF TRANSPORTATION (COMPREHENSIVE ANALYSIS)
A. LAND TRANSPORTATION
i. Road Transport This is the most pervasive mode in Nigeria, involving vehicles like trailers,
tankers, vans, and motorcycles.
 Operational Characteristics: Highly flexible, allowing for door-to-door services and
route adjustments in real-time.
 The Haulage System: This is the backbone of Nigerian logistics. Heavy-duty trucks
move over 90% of goods from Apapa and Tin Can Island ports to inland destinations.
 Advantages:
o Door-to-Door Service: Direct delivery from the seller's warehouse to the buyer's
premises.
3
o High Flexibility and Penetration: Can access remote rural areas via feeder roads
where other modes cannot.
o Relatively Low Start-up Cost: Compared to air or sea, acquiring a vehicle is
more affordable for small businesses.
 Disadvantages:
o High Risk: Prone to accidents, theft (hijacking), and damage to goods due to poor
road conditions.
o Limited Capacity: Cannot match the volume carried by a ship or a full train.
o Congestion and Delays: Traffic gridlock (especially in Lagos), numerous police
and military checkpoints cause significant delays and increase costs.
o High Maintenance Cost: Frequent vehicle repairs due to bad roads.
ii. Rail Transport This involves the movement of goods and passengers on fixed tracks. Nigeria
is transitioning from the old, inefficient narrow-gauge lines to modern standard-gauge lines.
 Operational Characteristics: Ideal for heavy, bulky, non-perishable goods over long
distances (e.g., coal, cement, containers, petroleum products).
 Advantages:
o Cost-Effective for Bulk: Lowest cost per ton/kilometer for large volumes.
o High Capacity: One train can carry the equivalent of 50 or more trucks.
o Reliability: Unaffected by road traffic congestion.
o Environmental Friendliness: More fuel-efficient per ton than road transport.
 Disadvantages:
o Inflexibility: Confined to fixed tracks, requiring "last-mile" delivery by road
(trans-shipment).
o High Capital Cost: Extremely expensive to lay and maintain railway lines.
o Limited Network: The rail network does not cover all parts of the country.
4
B. WATER TRANSPORTATION
i. Inland Waterways: Uses rivers (Niger, Benue), creeks, and lagoons with boats, ferries, and
barges. Crucial for moving agricultural produce and timber from riverine areas.
ii. Sea/Ocean Transport: The dominant mode for international trade.
 Ocean Liners: "Buses of the sea." They operate on fixed routes and published schedules.
 Tramps: "Taxis of the sea." They have no fixed schedule or route and are hired to carry
cargo wherever it is needed.
 Tankers: Specialized vessels for liquid bulk (crude oil, LNG).
 Container Ships: Carry goods in standardized containers, enabling efficient intermodal
transport.
 Advantages:
o Cheapest for International Trade: Moves massive volumes at a very low cost.
o High Capacity: Can carry very heavy and oversized cargo (e.g., machinery).
 Disadvantages:
o Slow Speed: Longest transit times.
5
o Port Dependency: Requires access to deep-sea ports with specialized handling
equipment.
o Perils: Vulnerable to piracy, storms, and accidents.
C. AIR TRANSPORTATION
 Operational Characteristics: Used for high-value, low-weight, and time-sensitive goods
(electronics, pharmaceuticals, samples, urgent documents, perishables like flowers and
fresh fish).
 Advantages:
o Speed: Fastest mode of transport.
o Reliability: Adheres to strict schedules.
o Security: High level of safety and security for goods.
 Disadvantages:
o High Cost: Most expensive mode.
o Limited Capacity: Cannot carry bulky, low-value goods economically.
o Weather Dependency: Susceptible to delays and cancellations due to bad
weather.
D. PIPELINE TRANSPORTATION A unique mode where the conduit is static, and the
product flows through it.
 Usage: Primarily for liquids and gases (crude oil, refined petroleum, natural gas, water).
 Advantages:
o Low Operating Cost: Once built, it is very cheap to operate.
o Continuous Flow: Provides a 24/7 supply.
o Reduces Road Congestion: Minimizes the number of fuel tankers on roads.
 Disadvantages:
o Immobility: Cannot be redirected; serves only fixed points.
o High Initial Investment: Very costly to construct.
o Vandalism and Theft: Prone to "bunkering" and sabotage, especially in the
Niger Delta.
6
o Environmental Hazard: Leaks can cause catastrophic environmental damage.
3. DOCUMENTS INVOLVED IN TRANSPORTATION
1. Waybill: A receipt issued by the carrier listing the goods and the terms of transport. It is
a contract of carriage but not a document of title.
2. Consignment Note: Similar to a waybill, used mainly in road and rail transport.
3. Bill of Lading (B/L): The most important document in sea freight. It serves three key
functions:
o A receipt for the goods received by the carrier.
o A contract of carriage between the shipper and the carrier.
o A document of title to the goods (ownership). The holder of the original B/L can
claim the goods.
4. Air Waybill (AWB): The equivalent of a Bill of Lading for air transport. However, it is
not a document of title, only a receipt and a contract of carriage.
4. PROBLEMS FACING THE NIGERIAN TRANSPORT SECTOR
1. Poor Infrastructure: Dilapidated roads, outdated rail lines, and shallow ports.
2. Insecurity: Piracy in the Gulf of Guinea, hijacking of trucks on highways, and pipeline
vandalism.
3. Administrative Bottlenecks: Corruption at ports and numerous checkpoints on roads.
4. Policy Inconsistency: Lack of a coherent and sustained national transport policy.
5. Funding Deficit: Inadequate investment in maintenance and development of transport
infrastructure.
Evaluation:
1. With the aid of an example, explain how transportation creates "Place Utility."
2. Differentiate between a 'Tramp' and an 'Ocean Liner'.
3. Why is a Bill of Lading considered more powerful than an Air Waybill?
4. Propose two solutions to the problem of insecurity on Nigerian highways.
7

2. Investigate the current state of the Lagos-Kano standard gauge rail project. What are its
potential impacts on marketing in Nigeria?

WEEK 2: WAREHOUSING
Topic: Warehousing Duration: Double Period (80 Minutes) Instructional Objectives: At the
end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define warehousing and distinguish it from mere storage.
2. Identify and explain the functions of different types of warehouses.
3. List and explain the documents used in warehousing.
4. Analyze the factors to consider when siting a warehouse.
1. CONCEPTUAL DEFINITION
Warehousing is the logistical function of holding and preserving goods from the time they are
produced until they are needed by consumers. It is not just storage; it involves systematic and
scientific management to maintain the quality and value of goods. It creates Time Utility by
9
making goods available when needed, thereby stabilizing prices and ensuring a continuous flow
of products.
Key Functions of a Warehouse:
 Storage: The basic function of holding goods.
 Price Stabilization: By releasing goods gradually, warehouses prevent market gluts
(which cause low prices) and shortages (which cause high prices).
 Risk Bearing: The warehouse bears risks like theft, fire, and spoilage (often mitigated by
insurance).
 Financing: A Warehouse Warrant can be used as collateral for a loan.
 Value-Added Services: Modern warehouses also offer branding, packaging, labelling,
and order assembly (breaking bulk).
2. TYPES OF WAREHOUSES
1. Bonded Warehouses:
o Ownership: Licensed by the government (Nigeria Customs Service).
o Function: Stores imported goods before customs duties and taxes are paid.
Goods are "in bond."
o Key Feature: The importer cannot access the goods until duties are paid. If not
paid within a stipulated time, the goods can be auctioned by Customs.
o Location: Seaports, airports, and land borders.
2. Public Warehouses:
o Ownership: Private businesses.
o Function: Rent out storage space to the general public for a fee.
o Advantage: Ideal for small businesses and seasonal sellers who cannot afford
their own storage facilities.
3. Private Warehouses:
10
o Ownership: Owned and operated by manufacturers, wholesalers, or retailers for
their exclusive use.
o Advantage: Maximum control over storage conditions and operations.
o Disadvantage: High capital investment and fixed cost.
4. Government Warehouses:
o Ownership: Federal or State governments.
o Function: Used to store seized goods (contraband), strategic grain reserves, and
other essential commodities to control inflation and prevent famine.
5. Cold Storage Warehouses:
o Function: Refrigerated facilities for perishable items like frozen foods, dairy
products, pharmaceuticals, and vaccines.
o Importance: Extends the shelf-life of products and allows for international trade
in perishables.
3. DOCUMENTS USED IN WAREHOUSING
1. Warehouse Warrant: A document of title issued by the warehouse keeper. It proves
ownership of the stored goods and can be traded or used as collateral for a bank loan.
2. Dock Warrant: Issued for goods stored at the docks, acknowledging receipt. It can be
converted into a warehouse warrant.
11
3. Gate Pass: An internal security document that authorizes the removal of specific goods
from the warehouse premises.
4. FACTORS TO CONSIDER IN SITING A WAREHOUSE
1. Proximity to Market/Factory: A distribution warehouse should be near customers; a
raw material warehouse should be near the factory.
2. Adequate Transportation Links: Easy access to major roads, rail sidings, or ports.
3. Cost and Availability of Land: Must be affordable and of sufficient size for future
expansion.
4. Utilities and Infrastructure: Reliable electricity (critical for cold storage), water, and
security systems.
5. Labour Availability: Access to a pool of skilled and unskilled workers.
6. Economic and Government Policies: Tax incentives and zoning laws can influence
location.

3. How does a Warehouse Warrant facilitate business financing?
4. A beverage company wants to build a warehouse to serve the entire South-East region.
List three factors they must consider before choosing a location.

WEEK 3: MARKET STRUCTURES AND UNIONS
Topic: Market Structure & Market Unions
Objectives: At the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define and differentiate between various market structures.
2. Analyze the characteristics of perfect competition, monopoly, oligopoly, and
monopolistic competition using real-world examples.
3. Explain the concept and functions of market unions in the Nigerian economy.
4. Critically evaluate the impact of market unions on both sellers and consumers.
13
1. MARKET STRUCTURE: THE COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE
A market structure describes the nature and degree of competition within a market. It is defined
by key characteristics such as the number of firms, the nature of the product, the ease of entry
and exit, and the level of control over price.
Market structures exist on a spectrum, from many competitors to no competition at all.
2. TYPES OF MARKET STRUCTURES (IN-DEPTH ANALYSIS)
A. Perfect Competition (Theoretical Ideal) This is a hypothetical, idealized market structure
used as a benchmark. It rarely exists in reality.
 Characteristics:
a. Very Many Buyers and Sellers: Each firm is so small that its actions cannot
influence the market price. They are "price takers."
b. Homogeneous Products: The goods sold by all firms are identical, e.g., salt, raw
agricultural commodities like cassava or yam from different farms. A buyer has
no reason to prefer one seller over another.
c. Free Entry and Exit: There are no barriers (legal, financial, or technological) to
prevent new firms from starting up or existing firms from leaving the market.
d. Perfect Knowledge: All buyers and sellers have complete information about
market conditions, including prices.
e. No Government Intervention: The market is left to regulate itself.
 Example: The market for tomatoes in a large rural market (e.g., Mile 12 Market in
Lagos). Thousands of sellers offer virtually identical products, and no single seller can
charge more than the prevailing market price.
B. Monopoly (The Single Seller) A monopoly exists when a single firm is the only producer of
a product with no close substitutes.
14
 Characteristics:
a. Single Seller: The firm is the entire industry.
b. No Close Substitutes: Consumers have no alternatives to the product.
c. High Barriers to Entry: Legal, economic, or technological factors make it
impossible for new competitors to enter (e.g., government license, control of a
key resource).
d. Price Maker: The monopolist has significant power to set the price.
 Examples in Nigeria:
o Transmission Company of Nigeria (TCN): It is the sole manager of the national
electricity grid.
o National Water Boards: In many states, they are the only legal provider of piped
water.
o Microsoft Windows (Historically): Held a near-monopoly on PC operating
systems.
C. Oligopoly (The Few Dominant Firms) This is a market dominated by a small number of
large, interdependent firms.
 Characteristics:
a. Few Dominant Firms: The market is controlled by a handful of large sellers (e.g.,
3-10 firms).
b. Interdependence: The actions of one firm (e.g., a price cut) directly affect the
others, who will likely react. This leads to strategic behavior and sometimes
collusion (illegal cooperation to fix prices).
c. Differentiated or Homogeneous Products: Can sell identical products (e.g.,
cement) or differentiated ones (e.g., mobile networks).
d. High Barriers to Entry: Significant capital investment, brand loyalty, and
economies of scale make entry difficult.
 Examples in Nigeria:
15
o Cement Industry: Dangote Cement, BUA Cement, and Lafarge Africa control
the market.
o Telecommunications: MTN, Airtel, Glo, and 9Mobile.
o Beverage Industry: Coca-Cola, PepsiCo, and 7UP.
o Banking Sector: A few top banks hold a large market share.
 Key Concept - Cartel: A formal type of oligopoly where firms explicitly agree to
coordinate production and pricing. OPEC is the world's most famous cartel.
D. Monopolistic Competition (Many Differentiated Sellers) This is a very common real-world
structure, featuring many firms competing by selling similar but not identical products.
 Characteristics:
a. Many Firms: A large number of sellers compete for the same customers.
b. Product Differentiation: This is the key feature. Products are perceived as
different by consumers due to:
 Branding: (e.g., Peak vs. Three Crowns milk)
 Quality: (e.g., different grades of furniture)
 Design: (e.g., different styles of clothing)
 Location: (e.g., a pharmacy on a main road vs. one inside a neighborhood)
c. Some Control Over Price: Because of differentiation, a firm can raise its price
slightly without losing all its customers (e.g., someone loyal to a specific hair
salon).
d. Low Barriers to Entry: It is relatively easy for new firms to enter the market.
 Examples: Restaurants, hair salons, fashion boutiques, bakeries, and supermarkets in a
city.
16
3. MARKET UNIONS: ASSOCIATIONS OF TRADERS
A Market Union is an association formed by traders or producers dealing in the same line of
goods or services. Their primary goal is to protect and promote the economic interests of their
members.
In-depth Functions of Market Unions:
1. Price Stabilization and Control: They often set minimum or uniform selling prices for
goods to prevent "price wars" that could hurt all members. For example, the Yam Sellers
Association may set a price floor for a heap of yams during the harvest season.
17
2. Regulation of Membership and Entry: They control who can sell in a particular market.
This protects members from excessive competition but can also create monopolistic
practices within the market.
3. Collective Bargaining: Unions act as a single voice for their members when negotiating
with government agencies over taxes, levies, market fees, and regulations. The National
Union of Road Transport Workers (NURTW) is a powerful example.
4. Provision of Welfare and Financial Aid: Many unions operate like cooperatives,
providing financial assistance, loans, or support to members during emergencies, sickness,
or bereavement.
5. Information Dissemination: They serve as a channel for communicating important
information about supply sources, government policies, or new regulations to their
members.
6. Maintenance of Standards: Some unions ensure that members adhere to certain quality
and ethical standards in their dealings.
7. Conflict Resolution: They provide a mechanism for settling disputes among members
internally.
Critical Analysis: The Double-Edged Sword of Market Unions
 Positive Impacts (Help):
o They provide a support system for small-scale traders.
o They bring order and predictability to the market.
o They can advocate for better market infrastructure from the government.
 Negative Impacts (Harm):
o They can act as Cartels: By fixing prices and restricting entry, they can keep
prices artificially high, harming consumers.
o Exploitation: They can impose numerous and sometimes arbitrary levies on their
own members and non-members.
o Violence and Intimidation: Some unions have been associated with the use of
force to enforce their rules and collect fees.
18

WEEK 4: MARKETING FACILITATORS – THE INVISIBLE
ENGINE OF COMMERCE
Topic: Marketing Facilitators Duration: Double Period (80 Minutes) Instructional Objectives:
At the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define marketing facilitators and distinguish them from merchant middlemen.
2. Categorize and explain the roles of different types of agent middlemen.
3. Identify and analyze the functions of ancillary service providers in the marketing chain.
4. Evaluate the critical importance of facilitators in modern business operations.
1. DEFINING MARKETING FACILITATORS: THE SUPPORTING CAST
In a movie, the stars cannot succeed without the directors, scriptwriters, and camera crew. In the
same way, the journey of a product from producer to consumer relies not just on the
manufacturers and sellers, but on a crucial group of supporting actors known as Marketing
Facilitators.
Marketing Facilitators are individuals or institutions that provide essential services to aid the
smooth flow of goods and the completion of transactions in the marketing process. Their
defining characteristic is that they do not take title to the goods—meaning they never own the
products. They are service providers, not owners.
20
2. CATEGORIES OF MARKETING FACILITATORS
Marketing facilitators can be broadly divided into two main categories: Agent Middlemen and
Ancillary Service Providers.
A. AGENT MIDDLEMEN: THE CONNECTORS AND NEGOTIATORS
These facilitators act as representatives for the producer (the principal) in negotiating sales
without taking ownership of the goods. They earn a commission on sales.
21
i. Brokers
 Role: Pure connectors. Their sole function is to bring buyers and sellers together.
 Operation: They do not handle the goods physically, store them, or get involved in
delivery. They operate on a one-time or short-term basis.
 Compensation: Earn a brokerage fee.
 Example: A real estate agent connecting a house owner to a potential tenant. A
stockbroker on the Nigerian Stock Exchange (NSE) connecting a share seller to a buyer.
ii. Factors
 Role: Have possession and significant authority. They take physical delivery of the goods
and sell them in their own name (not in the name of the principal).
 Operation: They have the power to set prices, offer credit terms to buyers, and are
responsible for collecting payment.
 Compensation: Earn a commission, which is usually higher than a broker's due to the
greater responsibility.
22
 Example: An agricultural factor who takes possession of a farmer's cocoa beans, stores
them, and finds buyers, handling all the sales details.
iii. Del Credere Agents
 Role: A specialized type of factor or agent who provides a credit guarantee.
 Operation: They guarantee to the principal that the buyer they found will pay for the
goods. If the buyer defaults (fails to pay), the Del Credere agent is liable and must pay
the principal.
 Compensation: Earn a higher Del Credere Commission for taking on this extra
financial risk.
 Example: An agent who sells machinery to a new construction company. If the
construction company cannot pay, the agent is responsible.
iv. Auctioneers
 Role: Licensed agents who sell goods publicly to the highest bidder.
 Operation: They take custody of the goods and conduct an auction, encouraging
competitive bidding to get the best possible price for the seller.
 Compensation: Earn a commission or a fixed fee.
 Example: An auctioneer selling off seized goods for the Nigeria Customs Service or
selling artwork and antiques.

B. ANCILLARY SERVICE PROVIDERS: THE INFRASTRUCTURE EXPERTS
These are institutions that provide specialized services that support the entire marketing process
without being directly involved in the negotiation of sales.
i. Financial Facilitators
 Banks: (e.g., FirstBank, Zenith Bank) Provide crucial working capital loans for
businesses to buy inventory, finance exports/imports, and manage cash flow.
 Insurance Companies: (e.g., Leadway Assurance, AIICO Insurance) Mitigate risk by
providing cover against fire, theft, damage in transit, and other losses. This makes
business possible despite uncertainties.
 FinTech Companies: (e.g., Paystack, Flutterwave) Facilitate seamless digital payments,
which is the backbone of e-commerce.
ii. Logistical & Physical Distribution Facilitators
 Transport Companies: (e.g., DHL, local haulage firms) Physically move goods from
one point to another.
 Warehousing Companies: Provide storage facilities, including public and cold storage
warehouses.
 Courier Services: (e.g., GIG Logistics, DHL Express) Handle small parcel deliveries
quickly and reliably.
iii. Informational & Promotional Facilitators
 Advertising Agencies: Create and place advertisements to build brand awareness and
persuade customers. They have expertise in media buying and creative design.
 Public Relations (PR) Firms: Manage the public image and reputation of a company.
 Market Research Agencies: Gather and analyze data about market trends, consumer
preferences, and competitor activity to guide business decisions.
iv. Regulatory & Standardization Bodies
24
 Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON): Ensures products meet quality and safety
standards, building consumer trust.
 Advertising Practitioners Council of Nigeria (APCON): Regulates advertising
practices to ensure they are ethical, truthful, and decent.
3. THE CRITICAL IMPORTANCE OF FACILITATORS
1. Specialization and Expertise: Facilitators are specialists. A company can focus on its
core strength (e.g., manufacturing) and outsource logistics to a transport specialist or
marketing to an ad agency.
2. Cost Efficiency (Economies of Scale): It is cheaper for a small business to pay for
warehouse space in a public warehouse than to build its own. A logistics company can
combine shipments from multiple clients to reduce costs for everyone.
3. Risk Reduction: Insurance companies absorb massive risks that would be catastrophic
for a single business. Del Credere agents absorb credit risk.
4. Market Expansion: Facilitators like transporters, banks, and online payment gateways
enable businesses to reach customers in geographically distant locations, both nationally
and internationally.
5. Enhanced Efficiency: The entire marketing system runs more smoothly, faster, and
more reliably because of the specialized services provided by facilitators.
The Facilitator Ecosystem in a Single Product's Journey

WEEK 6: MARKETING OF MINERAL PRODUCTS
Topic: Marketing of Mineral Products
Instructional Objectives: At the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define mineral products and analyze their unique characteristics.
2. Explain the concept of derived demand in the context of mineral products.
3. Apply the marketing mix (4Ps) to mineral products, with specific examples.
4. Evaluate the critical problems facing the marketing of mineral products in Nigeria.
5. Identify key government agencies involved in the sector.
1. NATURE AND CHARACTERISTICS OF MINERAL PRODUCTS
Mineral products are naturally occurring, inorganic substances extracted from the earth through
mining or drilling. Unlike agricultural products, they are extractive. Nigeria is endowed with a
wealth of these resources, which form the backbone of the national economy.
Categories of Mineral Products in Nigeria:
 Hydrocarbons (Energy Minerals): Crude Oil, Natural Gas, Bitumen.
 Metallic Minerals: Iron Ore, Gold, Tin, Lead, Zinc.
 Non-Metallic/Industrial Minerals: Limestone (for cement), Coal, Gypsum, Kaolin,
Barite.
Unique Characteristics of Mineral Products:
1. Non-Renewable (Depletable): Once extracted and used, they cannot be replaced within
a human timescale. This creates a need for sustainable management and revenue
diversification.
2. Location-Specific (Immobile): They are found only where geological conditions permit.
This influences infrastructure development and can lead to regional wealth disparities
(e.g., the Niger Delta).
27
3. Fluctuating and Variable Quality: The quality and grade can vary significantly from
one deposit to another. For example, Nigeria's Bonny Light crude oil is a high-grade,
"sweet" crude that is highly sought after, while other types are "heavier" and less valuable.
4. Bulkiness and Low Value-to-Weight Ratio (for some): Many minerals (like coal and
limestone) are heavy and costly to transport relative to their value, making proximity to
processing plants crucial.
5. Derived Demand: This is a fundamental concept. The demand for mineral products is
not direct; it is derived from the demand for the final products they help create.
o Example: The demand for crude oil is derived from the global demand for petrol,
diesel, plastics, and fertilizers. The demand for iron ore is derived from the
demand for steel, which is used in construction and car manufacturing.
2. THE MARKETING MIX (4PS) FOR MINERAL PRODUCTS
Marketing minerals is fundamentally different from marketing consumer goods like soap or soft
drinks. It is primarily Business-to-Business (B2B) and operates on a global scale.
A. PRODUCT
 Core Product: The raw or semi-processed mineral itself (e.g., crude oil, unrefined gold).
28
 Standardization and Grading: Products are strictly classified by internationally
accepted grades and specifications. For oil, this includes API gravity (light/heavy) and
sulphur content (sweet/sour). This standardization is crucial for pricing and trading on
global commodity exchanges.
 Limited Product Differentiation: It is difficult to differentiate a barrel of Bonny Light
crude from another barrel of the same grade. Competition is based more on reliability of

3. PROBLEMS IN THE MARKETING OF MINERAL PRODUCTS IN NIGERIA
1. The "Resource Curse" (Dutch Disease): Over-dependence on oil revenues has led to
the neglect of other sectors like agriculture and manufacturing, making the economy
vulnerable to global oil price shocks.
2. Infrastructure Decay and Vandalism:
o Oil Sector: Pipeline vandalism, illegal bunkering, and oil theft lead to massive
revenue losses and environmental damage. For example, the Forcados export
terminal is frequently shut down due to sabotage.
o Solid Minerals: Poor road networks and lack of rail links to mining sites increase
transport costs.
3. Price Volatility: Sudden crashes in global commodity prices (as seen in 2014-2016 and
2020) can cripple the national budget and lead to economic recessions.
4. Illegal Mining and Smuggling: Rampant illegal mining of gold, tin, and other solid
minerals leads to revenue loss for the government and funds criminal and insurgent
activities.
5. Environmental Degradation and Community Relations: Oil spills in the Niger Delta
and open-pit mining have destroyed farmland and aquatic life, leading to social unrest,
lawsuits, and a poor international image. Effective Corporate Social Responsibility
(CSR) is often lacking.
6. Policy Instability and Regulatory Overlap: Frequent changes in mining laws and
conflicts between federal, state, and local communities over mining rights create
uncertainty for investors.

promote, and direct the flow of a company's goods and services to consumers or users in more

2. Economies of Scale: Producing in larger volumes for a global market reduces the cost

3. Unique Product or Technological Advantage: A company with a superior product may

3. Excess Production Capacity: If a factory is not operating at full capacity, exporting can

3. THE CHALLENGES AND BARRIERS IN INTERNATIONAL MARKETING
Expanding internationally is fraught with obstacles that require careful planning to overcome.
These can be analyzed using the PESTEL Framework.
A. Political and Legal Barriers:
 Tariffs: Taxes imposed by a government on imported goods to make them more
expensive and protect local industries. (e.g., Nigeria's tariffs on imported rice to
encourage local production).
 Quotas: A physical limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported during a specific
period.
 Embargoes: A complete ban on the import or export of goods to or from a specific
country for political reasons (e.g., international embargoes on North Korea).
 Complex Regulations: Differing laws on product safety, labeling, packaging, and
environmental standards can be a major hurdle.
B. Economic Barriers:
 Currency Fluctuation (Exchange Rate Risk): The value of currencies changes daily. A
Nigerian exporter who agrees to a price in US Dollars may receive fewer Naira than
expected if the Naira depreciates before payment is made.
 Differences in Income Levels: Marketing strategies must adapt to the purchasing power
of the target market. Selling a luxury car in a low-income country requires a different
approach.
 Infrastructure Disparities: Lack of reliable roads, electricity, or banking systems in the
target country can disrupt distribution and operations.
C. Social and Cultural Barriers:
 Language: Brand names and slogans can have unintended meanings. Chevrolet's "Nova"
car famously translated to "no go" in Spanish.
 Values and Customs: Colors, symbols, and gestures have different meanings. The
"thumbs-up" sign is offensive in parts of the Middle East.
37
 Tastes and Preferences: Selling beef products in India (where the cow is sacred) or pork
in Saudi Arabia would be a failure. A food company must adapt its recipe to local palates.
 Religion: Religious holidays and dietary laws (e.g., Halal, Kosher) significantly impact
marketing timing and product formulation.
D. Technological and Environmental Barriers:
 Different Technical Standards: Electrical appliances must be compatible with local
voltage and plug types.
 Digital Divide: E-commerce strategies that work in a highly connected country may fail
in a region with low internet penetration.
 Environmental Regulations: Strict laws on packaging waste or carbon emissions can
affect product design and cost.
The PESTEL Framework for International Markets

1. MARKET ENTRY STRATEGIES: THE HIERARCHY OF COMMITMENT
Choosing how to enter a foreign market is one of the most critical strategic decisions a company
can make. The options represent a spectrum from low risk/low control to high risk/high control.
The Market Entry Strategy Spectrum
41
A. Exporting (The First Step) This is the simplest and most common way to begin international
marketing. The product is manufactured in the home country and then sold abroad.
 i. Indirect Exporting:
o How it works: The company uses intermediaries in its own country to handle the
export process. These can be Export Merchants (who buy your goods and sell
them abroad) or Export Agents (who find foreign buyers for a commission).
o Advantages: Least risky, requires no international expertise, low investment.
o Disadvantages: Least control over how the product is marketed and sold abroad;
lower profit margins.
o Example: A Nigerian shea butter producer sells a large batch to a trading
company in Lagos, which then exports it to Europe.
 ii. Direct Exporting:
o How it works: The company handles the export process itself, dealing directly
with buyers, distributors, or agents in the foreign market.
o Advantages: Greater control over the process, closer relationship with the foreign
market, higher profit potential.
o Disadvantages: Higher risk and resource requirement than indirect exporting;
requires understanding of export documentation and logistics.
o Example: The same shea butter producer partners directly with a cosmetic chain
in the UK, handling all shipping and customs paperwork.
B. Contractual Agreements (Partnerships without Equity) The firm enters a legal contract to
provide something of value to a foreign company in return for royalties or fees.
 i. Licensing:
o How it works: A company (the licensor) grants a foreign company (the licensee)
the right to use its intellectual property (e.g., patent, trademark, technology, brand
name) for a fee (royalty).
o Advantages: Low investment, quick market entry, circumvents trade barriers.
42
o Disadvantages: Risk of the licensee becoming a future competitor; less control
over product quality and marketing.
o Example: A Nigerian software company licenses its e-learning platform to a
South African university.
 ii. Franchising:
o How it works: A specialized form of licensing where the franchisor grants the
franchisee the right to do business under its name in a prescribed manner. The
franchisor provides a complete business system, marketing strategy, and ongoing
support.
o Advantages: Rapid expansion with minimal capital investment; franchisees are
highly motivated as owner-operators.
o Disadvantages: Requires strong, standardized brand; difficult to maintain quality
control across many franchisees.
o Example: A Nigerian restaurant chain like "Sweet Kiwi" franchising its brand
and operating model to entrepreneurs in Ghana.
C. Joint Venture (Shared Ownership)
 How it works: A company partners with a local firm in the foreign country to create a
new, jointly-owned entity. Both partners share investment, ownership, control, and
profits.
 Advantages: Access to the local partner's knowledge, distribution channels, and
government contacts; shared costs and risks.
 Disadvantages: Risk of conflicts with partners over goals, strategies, and control; less
profit than sole ownership.
 Example: Dangote Group forming a joint venture with a Chinese construction company
to build and operate a cement plant in Kenya.
D. Direct Investment (Full Ownership & Control)
43
 How it works: The company invests directly by establishing its own manufacturing
facilities, subsidiaries, or acquisitions in the foreign country. This is also known as
Foreign Direct Investment (FDI).
 Advantages: Maximum control over operations; protects technology and expertise;
qualifies for potential local government incentives.
 Disadvantages: Highest level of risk and financial commitment; most vulnerable to
political and economic instability in the host country.
 Example: Jumia, while pan-African, establishes its own fully-owned warehouses and
offices in each country it operates.

2. INTERNATIONAL MARKETING STRATEGIES: STANDARDIZATION VS.
ADAPTATION
Once a company has entered a market, it must decide on its strategic approach.
 Global Strategy (Standardization): Marketing a product in the same way everywhere in
the world. Assumes consumer needs are universal.
o Example: Coca-Cola, Apple iPhone.
 Multidomestic Strategy (Adaptation): Adjusting the marketing mix (product, price,
promotion, place) to suit each local market's unique needs.
o Example: McDonald's offering vegetarian burgers in India and rice dishes in
Nigeria.
 Transnational Strategy (Glocalization): A hybrid approach. The company seeks global
efficiency but remains responsive to local markets. The core brand is standardized, but
execution is localized.
o Example: A Nigerian fashion brand uses its signature Ankara prints (global
standard) but creates different clothing styles (e.g., abayas for the Middle East,
dresses for Europe) to suit local tastes (local adaptation).
3. ORGANIZATIONAL STRUCTURES FOR INTERNATIONAL OPERATIONS
As a company's international sales grow, its internal structure must evolve.
1. Export Department: A small unit within the domestic sales department. Suitable for
beginner exporters.
2. International Division: A separate division with its own staff (marketing, finance,
logistics) to handle all foreign operations. Common for companies with significant
international sales.
3. Global Organizational Structures:
o Geographical Structure: The world is divided into regions (e.g., VP of North
America, VP of Europe, VP of Africa). Ideal when consumer needs vary
significantly by region.
45
o Product Structure: Global managers are responsible for specific product lines
worldwide (e.g., Global Manager for Beverages, Global Manager for Snacks).
Best for companies with diverse, unrelated products.
o Matrix Structure: A complex structure where employees report to both a
product manager and a geographical manager. Balances global product focus with
local market responsiveness.

The Nigerian government provides crucial support to companies looking to "export Naija to the
world."
 Nigerian Export Promotion Council (NEPC):
o Primary Function: To promote the development and diversification of Nigeria's
non-oil exports.
o Services: Organizes trade fairs, provides market intelligence, and offers training
for exporters on standards and packaging.
 Nigerian Export-Import Bank (NEXIM Bank):
o Primary Function: To provide financing, risk-bearing, and trade information
services.
o Services: Offers loans to exporters, provides credit insurance against the risk of
non-payment by foreign buyers, and gives guarantees to commercial banks to
support export-oriented businesses.
 Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON):
o Role: Ensures that Nigerian exports meet international quality and safety
standards, which is critical for gaining acceptance in foreign markets.
 Nigeria Customs Service (NCS):
o Role: Regulates and facilitates the movement of goods across Nigerian borders,
handling export documentation and clearance.

 Physical Outlets: These have a tangible presence in a geographical location.

1. THE RETAIL TRIAD: THREE INTERCONNECTED FUNCTIONS
Purchasing, Supplying, and Merchandising are three distinct but deeply interconnected functions
that form the backbone of any successful retail operation. Understanding their unique roles is key
to managing a profitable marketing outlet.
A. Purchasing: The Strategic Art of Buying Purchasing is not merely "buying." It is the
strategic process of acquiring the right goods, in the right quantity, at the right price, from
the right source, at the right time.
 The 5 Rights of Purchasing:
a. Right Quality: Goods must meet the expected standards and specifications for
your target market.
b. Right Quantity: Buying enough to meet demand without overstocking and tying
up capital.
c. Right Price: Securing the best possible price to maintain profitability.
d. Right Source: Choosing reliable suppliers who are consistent and trustworthy.
e. Right Time: Ensuring goods are available when needed to avoid stock-outs.
 The Procurement Cycle: A structured process that guides purchasing decisions:
59
a. Need Identification: Realizing what goods are needed based on sales data or
inventory levels.
b. Supplier Sourcing & Evaluation: Finding and vetting potential suppliers.
c. Price & Term Negotiation: Agreeing on price, delivery schedules, and payment
terms.
d. Purchase Order Placement: Formally ordering the goods.
e. Order Expediting & Follow-up: Tracking the order to ensure timely delivery.
f. Receipt & Inspection: Accepting and checking the delivered goods against the
order.
g. Invoice Approval & Payment: Paying the supplier for the received goods.
B. Supplying: The Logistics of Availability Supplying is the operational process that follows
purchasing. It focuses on the logistics, storage, and delivery of goods from the supplier to the
buyer, ensuring they arrive in good condition and on schedule.
 Key Activities: Transportation management, warehousing, inventory control, and order
fulfillment.
 The Link: Purchasing decides what to buy and from whom; Supplying handles how and
when it gets to you.
C. Merchandising: The Psychology of Selling If purchasing is about buying right,
merchandising is about selling right. It is the planning, presenting, and promoting of products to
maximize sales and profitability. Merchandising is the "Silent Salesman" that works 24/7.
 Core Goal: To make products irresistible and easy to buy, thereby influencing consumer
purchasing decisions at the point of sale.
2. METHODS OF MERCHANDISING: TOOLS OF THE TRADE
Effective merchandising uses a blend of art and science to attract customers and drive sales.
1. Visual Merchandising (The Art of Display) This is about creating an appealing visual
experience.
60
 Window Displays: The "first impression" of the store. An attractive, thematic window
can stop passersby and draw them in.
 Interior Displays:
o The "Eye-Level is Buy-Level" Principle: The most profitable products are
placed at eye level (between waist and shoulders). Cheaper or less profitable
items are placed on lower or higher shelves.
o End-Cap Displays: The shelves at the end of aisles are prime real estate for
promotional items or high-margin goods.
o Thematic Arrangements: Grouping related products together (e.g., placing pasta,
pasta sauce, and cheese in one section).
The "Eye-Level is Buy-Level" Principle
2. Promotional Pricing & Offers Using price as a tool to create urgency and perceived value.
 Examples: "Buy One, Get One Free (BOGOF)," "50% Off," "Multi-buy offers" (3 for
the price of 2).
 Psychological Pricing: Setting prices just below a round number (e.g., N999 instead of
N1000) to make the product seem significantly cheaper.

3. Strategic Product Placement Placing products based on customer buying behavior.
 Impulse Buy Zones: Placing small, inexpensive, tempting items (chocolates, gums,
magazines) near the checkout counter where customers are likely to make unplanned
purchases while queueing.
 Destination Products: Staple items like milk or bread are often placed at the back of the

4. Stock Rotation & Freshness
 FIFO (First-In, First-Out): Ensuring that the oldest stock (first in) is sold first (first out)

5. Sensory Merchandising Engaging multiple senses to enhance the shopping experience.
 In-Store Music: Playing upbeat music to encourage a faster shopping pace or calm
music to make customers linger.
 Product Sampling: Allowing customers to taste or try a product (common in food stores
and cosmetics).
 Scent Marketing: Using a pleasant, subtle scent in the store to create a positive
atmosphere.
3. THE STRATEGIC IMPORTANCE OF MERCHANDISING
Effective merchandising is not just about looking good; it's a powerful business strategy.
2. Drives Sales and Increases Profit: Attractive displays and promotions directly lead to

3. Builds and Reinforces Brand Image: A clean, well-organized, and creatively

4. Enhances Customer Experience: It makes shopping easier, more enjoyable, and less
frustrating. Customers can find what they need and discover new products.

5. Optimizes Inventory Management: By highlighting slow-moving items through

6. Maximizes Space Productivity: Effective merchandising ensures that every square

profitability and sales velocity.

2. THE UNIQUE CHARACTERISTICS OF SERVICES (THE 8 I's)
Services possess unique characteristics that fundamentally shape how they are marketed. We can
expand beyond the classic 4 I's to an "8 I's" framework for a deeper understanding.
66
1. Intangibility Services cannot be seen, tasted, felt, heard, or smelled before they are purchased.
A customer cannot inspect a haircut before sitting in the barber's chair.
 Marketing Challenge: How do you sell a promise?
 Strategy: Use tangibles to represent the service. A bank uses a sturdy building and
branded debit cards to project security and reliability.
2. Inseparability Services are typically produced and consumed at the same time. The service
provider is often physically present when the service is delivered, and the customer participates
in the process.
 Marketing Challenge: The quality of the service is tied to the quality of the interaction
between the provider and the customer.
 Strategy: Focus on staff training and customer relationship management.
3. Inconsistency (Variability) The quality of a service can vary from day to day, from provider
to provider, and even with the same provider. A chef might have an off day; a teacher might
deliver a lesson differently to two separate classes.
 Marketing Challenge: How do you deliver a consistent, standardized experience?
 Strategy: Standardize the service process through checklists and scripts (e.g., the
greeting at a hotel). Invest in continuous training.
4. Inventory (Perishability) Services cannot be saved, stored, or inventoried for later use. An
empty hotel room for the night, an unfilled seat on a flight, or an unused hour of a lawyer's time
represents lost revenue that can never be recovered.
 Marketing Challenge: How do you manage fluctuating demand?
 Strategy: Use dynamic pricing (e.g., off-peak discounts for flights) and reservation
systems to manage demand.
5. Involvement (Customer Participation) The customer is often directly involved in the service
production. A student must attend and participate in class to get the full value of the education.
 Marketing Challenge: The customer's input affects the service outcome.
67
 Strategy: Educate the customer on their role. A gym provides an orientation session for
new members.
6. Inability to Own You pay for a service, but you don't own anything tangible at the end. You
pay for a Netflix subscription for a month of access, not for ownership of the movies.
 Marketing Challenge: The value is in the experience, not in a physical asset.
 Strategy: Emphasize the benefits and outcomes (e.g., relaxation, knowledge,
convenience) rather than the service itself.
7. Immediacy Many services are required instantly and cannot be delayed. You need a plumber
now when a pipe bursts, not tomorrow.
 Marketing Challenge: The service must be readily available.
 Strategy: Ensure accessibility and responsiveness (e.g., 24/7 customer service lines).
8. Information-Based The core of many services is information and expertise (e.g., legal advice,
software development, medical diagnosis).
 Marketing Challenge: Demonstrating expertise before the customer commits.
 Strategy: Showcase credentials, case studies, and testimonials to build credibility.

1. DECODING CONSUMER BEHAVIOR: WHY PEOPLE BUY
Consumer Behavior is the study of individuals, groups, or organizations and the processes they
use to select, secure, use, and dispose of products, services, experiences, or ideas to satisfy needs
and the impacts that these processes have on the consumer and society.
In simple terms, it answers the fundamental question: Why do people buy what they buy?
 It's More Than Just Buying: It encompasses the entire journey—from realizing a need,
to searching for information, to the final post-purchase feelings.
 A Blend of Disciplines: It draws from psychology (motivation, perception), sociology
(groups, family), anthropology (culture), and economics (income, value).
Why is Studying Consumer Behavior Crucial for Marketers?
1. To Develop Effective Products: By understanding needs and desires, companies can
create products that people actually want.
2. To Craft Compelling Messages: Marketing communication can be tailored to resonate
with the target audience's motivations and values.
3. To Predict Market Trends: Identifying shifts in consumer attitudes can help businesses
stay ahead of the curve.
4. To Build Customer Loyalty: Understanding post-purchase behavior helps in creating
experiences that turn one-time buyers into lifelong fans.
73
2. THE INFLUENCE MATRIX: FACTORS SHAPING CONSUMER DECISIONS
A consumer's decision is not made in a vacuum. It is influenced by a complex web of internal
and external factors.
A. Internal Influences (The Psychological World Inside the Consumer)
1. Motivation (The Driving Force):
o Concept: The internal need that drives a person to act. Psychologist Abraham
Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs explains that people are motivated to fulfill basic
needs (hunger, safety) before higher-level needs (esteem, self-actualization).
o Marketing Application: Advertisements for basic food items appeal to
physiological needs, while luxury car ads appeal to esteem and status needs.
2. Perception (Our Personal Reality):
o Concept: The process by which we select, organize, and interpret information to
create a meaningful picture of the world. Two people can see the same ad and
interpret it differently.
o Marketing Application: Brands use distinctive packaging (e.g., Coca-Cola's red
and white) to be easily perceived and recognized on a crowded shelf.
3. Learning and Experience (The Past Guides the Future):
o Concept: Changes in an individual's behavior arising from experience. If a
customer has a positive experience with a brand, they are likely to repeat the
purchase (positive reinforcement).
o Marketing Application: Free samples and trial periods are used to encourage
first-time purchases, leading to learned brand loyalty.
4. Attitudes and Beliefs (Mental Shortcuts):
o Concept: A person's enduring favorable or unfavorable evaluations, emotional
feelings, and action tendencies towards an object or idea. The belief that "German
cars are well-engineered" is a powerful attitude.
74
o Marketing Application: Marketers try to create positive brand attitudes through
quality products and emotional advertising.
B. External Influences (The Social World Around the Consumer)
1. Cultural Factors (The Invisible Hand):
o Culture: The set of basic values, perceptions, wants, and behaviors learned from
family and society. In Nigeria, the culture of respect for elders influences the
products bought for them.
o Subculture: A group of people with shared value systems based on common life
experiences. This includes ethnic groups (Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa), religions, or
geographic regions.
o Social Class: Relatively permanent divisions in a society with shared values,
interests, and behaviors. A person's social class influences their choice of car,
neighborhood, and leisure activities.
2. Social Factors (The People We Know):
o Reference Groups: Groups that serve as a direct or indirect point of comparison
in forming a person's attitudes or behavior.
 Membership Groups: Groups the person belongs to (family, friends,
coworkers).
 Aspirational Groups: Groups the person wishes to belong to (celebrities,
influencers).
o Family: The most important consumer-buying organization. Marketers study the
roles of the husband, wife, and children in purchasing different products.
o Roles and Status: A person's position in each group defines their role and status.
A university vice-chancellor is expected to dress and behave in a way that reflects
their status.

3. THE CONSUMER'S JOURNEY: THE DECISION-MAKING PROCESS
For significant purchases, consumers typically go through a five-stage process. However, for
routine buys, they may skip or reverse some stages.
Illustration: The Consumer Decision-Making Process
Stage 1: Need Recognition
76
 What Happens: The consumer recognizes a problem or need. The need can be triggered
by internal stimuli (e.g., feeling hungry) or external stimuli (e.g., seeing an ad for a new
phone).
 Marketing Task: Activate need recognition through advertising and promotions.
Stage 2: Information Search
 What Happens: The consumer seeks information. This can be:
o Internal Search: Recalling past experiences from memory.
o External Search: Seeking information from friends, family, online reviews, or
in-store visits.
 Marketing Task: Make information readily available through websites, brochures, and
sales staff. Ensure positive word-of-mouth.
Stage 3: Evaluation of Alternatives
 What Happens: The consumer uses the information to evaluate brands in the
consideration set. They compare products based on attributes like price, quality, and
features.
 Marketing Task: Understand the criteria consumers use and position your brand
favorably against competitors.
Stage 4: Purchase Decision
 What Happens: The consumer makes the actual purchase. However, this decision can
still be disrupted by factors like a negative salesperson interaction or a competitor's lastminute
discount.
 Marketing Task: Make the purchasing process easy, pleasant, and secure.
Stage 5: Post-Purchase Behavior
 What Happens: The consumer assesses whether they are satisfied or dissatisfied. This
leads to Cognitive Dissonance (post-purchase doubt): "Did I make the right choice?"
77
 Marketing Task: Reduce dissonance by providing reassurance (e.g., a confirmation
email, a thank-you note, excellent after-sales service). Satisfaction leads to repeat
purchases and loyalty; dissatisfaction leads to returns and negative reviews.
4. TYPES OF CONSUMER BUYING BEHAVIOR
Not all purchases require the same level of effort. Consumer behavior can be categorized based
on the degree of involvement and the perceived differences between brands.
 Complex Buying Behavior: Occurs when consumers are highly involved in a purchase
and perceive significant differences among brands. They will spend time researching
before deciding.
 Dissonance-Reducing Behavior: Occurs when consumers are highly involved but see
little difference between brands. They may respond to price or convenience, and
experience post-purchase dissonance.
78
 Habitual Buying Behavior: Occurs under conditions of low involvement and little brand
difference. Consumers do not search for information; they simply reach for the same
brand out of habit.
 Variety-Seeking Behavior: Occurs in situations of low involvement but with significant
perceived brand differences. Consumers often do a lot of brand switching for the sake of
variety.

WEEK 6: PRODUCT LIFE CYCLE (PLC)
Topic: The Life and Death of Products in the Market
Instructional Objectives: At the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define the Product Life Cycle (PLC) and explain its importance in marketing strategy.
2. Identify and analyze the four main stages of the PLC, including their characteristics and
challenges.
3. Develop appropriate marketing strategies for each stage of the PLC.
4. Evaluate how the PLC concept applies to modern products and services.
1. THE CONCEPT OF THE PRODUCT LIFE CYCLE
The Product Life Cycle (PLC) is a conceptual model that describes the typical stages a product
goes through from its introduction to its eventual withdrawal from the market. Just like living
organisms, products are born, grow, mature, and eventually decline.
 It's a Model, Not a Law: The PLC is a framework for understanding market dynamics,
but not all products follow this exact pattern or timeline. Some products may have very
short life cycles (e.g., fashion fads), while others can last for decades (e.g., Coca-Cola).
 Strategic Importance: Understanding which stage a product is in helps marketers make
informed decisions about pricing, promotion, distribution, and product modifications. It
answers the critical question: "What should we do next with this product?"
81
Illustration: The Classic Product Life Cycle Curve
2. THE FOUR STAGES OF THE PLC: CHARACTERISTICS AND STRATEGIES
STAGE 1: INTRODUCTION
This is the launch phase where the product is first made available to the public.
 Characteristics:
o Sales: Low and slow as the market becomes aware of the product.
o Costs: Very high due to heavy investment in research, development, and launch
marketing.
o Profit: Negative or minimal (losses are common).
o Competition: Little or none.
o Customers: "Innovators" - a small group willing to try new things.
 Marketing Objectives:
o Create awareness and trial.
82
o Establish a foothold in the market.
o Build distribution channels.
 Marketing Strategies:
o Product: Basic model, focus on building quality.
o Price:
 Price Skimming: Set a high initial price to recover development costs
(e.g., new gaming consoles, high-end smartphones).
 Penetration Pricing: Set a low initial price to attract a large number of
customers quickly and gain market share (e.g., new streaming services).
o Promotion: Heavy spending on advertising to educate the market and generate
demand.
o Place (Distribution): Selective distribution, building relationships with key
retailers.
 Nigerian Example: Electric Vehicles (EVs) are currently in the introduction stage in
Nigeria. They are expensive, awareness is low, and charging infrastructure is limited.
STAGE 2: GROWTH
If the product is accepted, it enters the growth stage, where sales begin to climb rapidly.
 Characteristics:
o Sales: Rapidly increasing.
o Costs: High, but decreasing per unit due to economies of scale.
o Profit: Rising significantly.
o Competition: Competitors enter the market, attracted by the opportunity.
o Customers: "Early Adopters" and the "Early Majority" join the Innovators.
 Marketing Objectives:
o Maximize market share.
83
o Build brand preference and loyalty.
o Differentiate from new competitors.
 Marketing Strategies:
o Product: Improve product quality, add new features, models, or support services.
o Price: Maintain or slightly reduce price as competition increases.
o Promotion: Shift from product awareness to brand preference. Emphasize the
product's unique benefits.
o Place (Distribution): Intensive distribution to make the product available in as
many outlets as possible.
 Nigerian Example: Home Solar Power Systems are in the growth stage. Sales are
rising, more companies are entering the market, and consumers are becoming more aware
of the benefits.
STAGE 3: MATURITY
Sales peak and begin to slow down as the market becomes saturated.
 Characteristics:
o Sales: Peak and then slowly decline.
o Costs: Low, due to established production.
o Profit: High initially, then begins to erode due to intense competition and price
wars.
o Competition: Fierce; the number of competitors is at its highest.
o Customers: The "Late Majority" buys. The market is saturated.
 Marketing Objectives:
o Defend market share.
o Extend the product's life.
o Maximize profit while minimizing cost.
84
 Marketing Strategies:
o Product Modifications:
 Quality Improvements: "New and improved formula!"
 Feature Improvements: Add new functionalities.
 Style Improvements: Update the aesthetic design.
o Price: Competitive pricing, discounts, and sales promotions to lure customers
from competitors.
o Promotion: Heavy emphasis on advertising and sales promotions to highlight
differences.
o Place (Distribution): Find new, untapped distribution channels or market
segments.
 Nigerian Example: Mobile Smartphones are in the maturity stage. Almost everyone
who wants one has one, and competition between Tecno, Infinix, Samsung, and Apple is
intense, focusing on camera quality, battery life, and price.
STAGE 4: DECLINE
The product experiences a sharp drop in sales and profitability.
 Characteristics:
o Sales: Significant and steady decline.
o Costs: May increase due to low production volumes.
o Profit: Dwindles to zero or becomes negative.
o Competition: Number of competitors decreases as they leave the market.
o Customers: Only "Laggards" and a few loyalists remain.
 Marketing Objectives:
o Phase out the product gracefully.
o Reduce costs and "milk" the remaining brand loyalty.
85
o Free up resources for new products.
 Marketing Strategies (The 3 D's):
o Deletion: Simply discontinue the product.
o Divestment: Sell the product/brand to another company.
o Harvesting: Reduce marketing support to a minimum while continuing to sell to
a small, loyal segment. Cut costs to maximize short-term profits until the product
is no longer viable.
 Nigerian Example: DVD Players are in the decline stage, replaced by streaming
services like Netflix and Showmax. Public Phone Booths (payphones) have been
completely displaced by mobile phones.

3. THE IMPORTANCE AND LIMITATIONS OF THE PLC
Why the PLC is Important:
86
 Forecasting: Helps predict sales trends and plan for the future.
 Strategic Planning: Allows companies to adapt their marketing mix proactively.
 Resource Allocation: Helps decide where to invest money and effort (e.g., invest in

 Risk Management: Acknowledging that products will eventually decline encourages
innovation and new product development.
Limitations of the PLC Model:
 It's Descriptive, Not Predictive: It describes what has happened but cannot precisely
predict how long each stage will last.
 Not All Products Follow the Curve: Some products (like classic staple foods) may stay

 Self-Fulfilling Prophecy: If a manager decides a product is in decline and withdraws
support, they may cause the very decline they predicted.
 Stage Boundaries are Blurry: It can be difficult to determine exactly when a product
moves from one stage to another.

1. BRANDING: BEYOND A NAME - THE HEART OF A PROMISE
A brand is a name, term, sign, symbol, design, or a combination of these that identifies the
maker or seller of a product or service and differentiates it from competitors. It is not just a logo;
it is the entire perception and experience a customer has with a product or company.
 The Brand as a Promise: A strong brand makes a promise about quality, consistency,
and value. When you see the "Apple" logo, you expect innovation and sleek design.
When you buy "Dangote" cement, you expect reliability and strength.
The Components of a Brand (The Brand Identity):
1. Brand Name: The verbal part of the brand (e.g., "Guinness," "Indomie").
2. Brand Mark (Logo): The symbol, design, or distinctive coloring associated with the
brand (e.g., the Nike Swoosh, the MTN yellow and red).
3. Trademark: A legally protected brand name or brand mark. No one else can use it.
4. Trade Character: A personified brand mark (e.g., the Michelin Man, the Airtel
"Champion").
5. Slogan/Tagline: A short, memorable phrase that summarizes the brand's essence (e.g.,
"MTN: Everywhere You Go," "Guinness: Made of More").

2. THE STRATEGIC LANDSCAPE: TYPES OF BRANDING
Companies use different branding strategies depending on their goals and product portfolio.
1. Manufacturer's Brand (National Brand)
 The brand is owned by the producer of the product.
 Examples: Dangote (Sugar, Cement), Unilever (Omo, Knorr), Samsung.
 Advantage: The manufacturer has full control over the brand's image and marketing.
90
2. Private Label/Store Brand (Distributor's Brand)
 The brand is owned by a retailer or distributor who gets the products manufactured by a
third party.
 Examples: Rite brands (from Shoprite), Just Done (from JustRite), Tesco value brands.
 Advantage: Higher profit margins for the retailer and often lower prices for consumers.
3. Family Branding
 A single brand name is used for several related products.
 Examples: Virgin (Virgin Atlantic, Virgin Mobile, Virgin Money), Sony (Sony Bravia
TV, Sony PlayStation, Sony Walkman).
 Advantage: Lower marketing costs and instant recognition for new products. A risk is
that a problem with one product can damage the entire family's reputation.
4. Individual Branding
 Giving each product a distinct brand name, even if they are from the same company.
 Examples: Procter & Gamble owns Always, Ariel, and Pampers. Unilever owns Dove,
Lipton, and Blue Band.
 Advantage: Allows the company to target different market segments without confusing
the consumer. The failure of one brand does not affect the others.
5. Co-Branding
 Two established brands combine in one product to create synergy and mutual benefit.
 Examples: Nike + Apple (Nike+ running shoes and Apple Watch integration), a
Mastercard offered by a specific bank like UBA.
 Advantage: Leverages the strengths and customer bases of both brands.

3. PACKAGING: THE SILENT SALESMAN
Packaging involves designing and producing the container or wrapper for a product. It is often
the first physical contact a customer has with a product and plays a critical role in the purchasing
decision.
The Multifaceted Functions of Packaging:
1. Protection (The Primary Function):
o Protects the product from damage, spoilage, leakage, contamination, and pilferage
during transportation and storage.
o Example: A sealed, airtight container for biscuits; a shock-absorbent box for
electronics.
2. Containment and Convenience:
o Holds the product in a manageable form. Makes it easy to handle, transport, store,
and use.
o Example: A squeezable ketchup bottle; a milk carton with a screw cap; singleserve
sachets of water or detergent.
3. Information and Communication:
o Provides essential information to the consumer: ingredients, nutritional facts,
expiry date, manufacturer details, and usage instructions. It is a legal requirement
in many cases.
92
o Example: The label on a can of milk or a drug package.
4. Promotion and Persuasion (The Silent Salesman):
o Attracts attention at the point of sale. The design, color, shape, and graphics can
create desire and differentiate the product from competitors.
o Example: A uniquely shaped perfume bottle; vibrant, colorful packaging for
children's cereals.
5. Environmental Sustainability:
o A modern and increasingly important function. Using recyclable, biodegradable,
or reusable materials to meet consumer demand for eco-friendly products.
o Example: Packaging made from recycled paper or cornstarch.
4. THE SYMBIOTIC RELATIONSHIP: HOW BRANDING AND PACKAGING WORK
TOGETHER
Branding and packaging are two sides of the same coin. A strong brand guides the packaging
design, and effective packaging reinforces the brand identity.

 Consistency is Key: The colors, fonts, and logos on the packaging must be consistent
with the overall brand identity. The "Cadbury purple" is instantly recognizable worldwide.
 Packaging as a Brand Touchpoint: The unboxing experience of an iPhone—the sleek
box, the careful arrangement—reinforces Apple's brand promise of premium quality and
attention to detail.
 Reinforcing Brand Values: A brand that stands for eco-friendliness (like "Thank U
Earth") must use sustainable packaging to be credible.
5. CHALLENGES IN BRANDING AND PACKAGING
1. Counterfeiting and Piracy: Perhaps the biggest challenge in Nigeria. Fake products
copy successful brands' names and packaging to deceive consumers, damaging the
original brand's reputation and causing revenue loss.
2. High Cost: Building a strong brand requires significant, sustained investment in
advertising and quality control. Effective packaging also adds to the product's cost.
3. Maintaining Consistency: Ensuring that every unit of the product, across all locations,
delivers on the brand promise is difficult.
4. Cultural Sensitivity: A brand name or packaging color that is successful in one country
might have a negative meaning in another (e.g., white is for mourning in some cultures).
5. Environmental Regulations: Increasing government and consumer pressure to reduce
packaging waste poses a challenge for designers to create packaging that is both
attractive and sustainable.

WEEK 9: PRICING POLICIES AND STRATEGIES
Topic: The Art and Science of Setting the Right Price
Instructional Objectives: At the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define price and analyze its unique role in the marketing mix.
2. Evaluate the key internal and external factors that influence pricing decisions.
3. Differentiate between various pricing strategies and determine when to use each.
4. Apply cost-based pricing formulas to calculate selling prices.
1. THE CONCEPT OF PRICE: MORE THAN JUST A NUMBER
Price is the monetary value that a seller sets for a product or service. It is the amount of money a
customer must pay to acquire ownership or use of an offering.
 The Revenue Generator: Unlike the other Ps of the marketing mix (Product, Place,
Promotion), which are costs, Price is the only element that generates revenue for a
business. All other activities represent expenses.
 Value Expression: Price is a powerful communicator. It signals quality, positioning, and
value to the customer. A high price suggests luxury and exclusivity, while a low price
suggests affordability and value.
 The Price Equation: From the customer's perspective, price is the sacrifice made to
obtain a desired product, which includes not just money, but also time, effort, and
psychological cost.
The Strategic Role of Pricing: Pricing is not just about covering costs. It is a strategic tool that
can:
 Determine a product's market positioning.
 Influence customer perception.
 Drive specific consumer behaviors (e.g., bulk buying, trial).
 Respond to competitive moves.
96
2. THE PRICING INFLUENCE MATRIX: FACTORS SHAPING THE DECISION
Setting the right price requires balancing internal capabilities with external market realities.
A. Internal Factors (Within the Company's Control)
1. Cost of Production (The Floor Price):
o This is the absolute minimum a price can be set for the business to survive in the
long run. The price must cover:
 Variable Costs: Costs that change with the level of production (e.g., raw
materials, direct labor).
 Fixed Costs: Costs that remain constant regardless of output (e.g., rent,
salaries, insurance).
o Formula: Total Cost = Fixed Costs + Variable Costs
2. Company Objectives (The Strategic Goal):
o Survival Pricing: Setting low prices to cover costs and stay in business during
tough times.
o Profit Maximization: Setting prices to generate the highest possible profit.
o Market Share Leadership: Setting low prices to attract a large number of
customers and achieve a dominant market position.
o Product Quality Leadership: Setting high prices to cover the high cost of
research, development, and premium quality (e.g., Apple, Mercedes-Benz).
3. Marketing Mix Strategy (The Integrated Plan):
o Price must be consistent with the other 3 Ps.
o A high-quality product, sold through exclusive distribution and supported by
heavy promotion, must command a premium price.
B. External Factors (Outside the Company's Control)
1. Market Demand and Customer Perception (The Ceiling Price):
97
o The maximum price is determined by what customers are willing to pay based on
their perceived value of the product.
o Elasticity of Demand: How sensitive demand is to a change in price.
 Elastic Demand: A small price change causes a large change in quantity
demanded (e.g., luxury goods, holidays).
 Inelastic Demand: A price change has little effect on quantity demanded
(e.g., essential goods like salt, life-saving drugs).
2. Competition (The Benchmark):
o Companies must be aware of competitors' prices and strategies.
o In a highly competitive market (e.g., the mobile telecoms sector), companies have
less freedom to set their own prices.
3. Economic Conditions:
o In a recession, consumers become more price-sensitive, forcing companies to
lower prices or offer discounts.
o Inflation may force companies to increase prices to maintain profitability.
4. Government and Regulations:
o Governments can impose price controls on essential commodities (e.g., fuel,
electricity) to protect consumers.
o Regulations may prevent predatory pricing (setting very low prices to eliminate
competition).

3. STRATEGIC PRICING MODELS: A TOOLKIT FOR DIFFERENT GOALS
1. Cost-Based Pricing (The Inside-Out Approach) Setting prices based primarily on the cost
of producing and selling the product, plus a markup for profit.
 Cost-Plus Pricing:
o Formula: Selling Price = Total Cost per Unit + (Markup Percentage × Total Cost
per Unit)
o Example: If a shoe costs N5,000 to produce and the company wants a 20%
markup, the selling price is N5,000 + (0.20 × N5,000) = N6,000.
o Advantage: Simple to calculate; ensures costs are covered.
o Disadvantage: Ignores demand, competition, and perceived value.
2. Value-Based Pricing (The Outside-In Approach) Setting prices based on the customer's
perceived value of the product, not the seller's cost.
 Process: Understand the customer's needs and the value they derive from the product,
then set a price that captures a portion of that value.
100
 Example: A designer handbag may cost only N10,000 to make, but because of the
brand's prestige (perceived value), it sells for N100,000.
 Advantage: Can lead to higher profits; aligns price with customer expectations.
 Disadvantage: Difficult to measure perceived value accurately.
3. Competition-Based Pricing (The Market-Follower Approach) Setting prices based on what
competitors are charging.
 Going-Rate Pricing: Setting a price that is equal to the average market price.
 Sealed-Bid Pricing: Setting a price based on what the company believes competitors will
bid (common for government contracts).
 Advantage: Simple and safe; avoids price wars.
 Disadvantage: Ignores own costs and value proposition.
4. TACTICAL PRICING STRATEGIES: PSYCHOLOGY AND TIMING
A. New Product Pricing
101
 Price Skimming: Setting a high initial price for a new, innovative product to "skim"
maximum revenue from customers willing to pay a premium. The price is lowered over
time as new customer segments are targeted.
o Example: New PlayStation or iPhone models.
 Penetration Pricing: Setting a very low initial price to attract a large number of
customers quickly and gain a substantial market share. The price may be raised later.
o Example: New mobile network operators (like Glo in its early days) offering very
low call rates.
B. Psychological Pricing Setting prices that have a psychological impact.
 Odd-Even Pricing: Setting prices just below a round number (e.g., N999 instead of
N1,000). The left-digit effect makes the price seem significantly lower.
 Prestige Pricing: Setting high, round-number prices to reinforce a product's premium
quality and status (e.g., a luxury car priced at N20,000,000, not N19,999,999).
C. Product Mix Pricing Setting prices for multiple products in a way that maximizes profits for
the entire portfolio.
 Captive Product Pricing: Setting a low price for the core product (e.g., a printer) but a
high price for the necessary consumables (e.g., ink cartridges).
 Product Bundle Pricing: Combining several products and selling the bundle at a
reduced price (e.g., a "meal deal" at a fast-food restaurant).

WEEK 10: CHANNELS OF DISTRIBUTION
Topic: The Pathways from Producer to Consumer
Instructional Objectives: At the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define a distribution channel and explain its role in creating time and place utility.
2. Analyze the different levels and types of distribution channels.
3. Evaluate the functions and value-added by various marketing intermediaries.
4. Determine the key factors that influence the choice of a distribution channel.
1. THE CONCEPT OF DISTRIBUTION CHANNELS: BRIDGING THE GAP
A Channel of Distribution (or Marketing Channel) is the network of businesses and
intermediaries involved in getting a product or service from the producer to the final consumer. It
is the route or pathway that ownership of goods follows as they travel from origin to
consumption.
 Creating Utility: While transportation creates place utility and warehousing creates time
utility, the entire distribution channel is the system that manages and executes these
functions, ensuring products are available where and when customers want them.
 The Flow of Ownership: It's crucial to understand that the channel tracks the flow of
title (ownership), not necessarily the physical product. The physical product might move
directly from a factory to a retailer, but the ownership may have passed through a
wholesaler.
Key Participants in a Distribution Channel:
 Producer/Manufacturer: The creator of the good or service.
 Intermediaries (Middlemen): Independent organizations that assist in the flow of goods
from producer to consumer.
 Final Consumer/End-User: The person or organization that uses the product.
104
2. THE CHANNEL STRUCTURE: FROM DIRECT TO COMPLEX
Distribution channels can be categorized by their length—the number of intermediary levels
between the producer and the consumer.
Channel Level 0: The Direct Channel (Producer → Consumer) No intermediaries are
involved. The producer sells directly to the end-user.
 Methods: Company-owned retail stores, direct sales (e.g., Tupperware), online sales
from the manufacturer's website, farm gate sales.
 Examples: Innoson Vehicles selling from its factory showroom, a farmer selling
produce at a local market, Jumia (when acting as the seller, not the platform).
 Advantages: Full control over marketing, higher profit margins, direct customer
feedback.
 Disadvantages: High cost and responsibility for distribution, limited market reach.
Channel Level 1: One Intermediary (Producer → Retailer → Consumer) A retailer is
introduced. The retailer buys from the manufacturer and sells to the consumer.
 Examples: Large supermarkets like Shoprite or Spar buying directly from Dangote or
Nestlé. Car dealerships.
 Advantages: Wider market reach than direct channels, reduces the producer's burden of
dealing with thousands of individual consumers.
 Disadvantages: Producer gives up some margin and control.
Channel Level 2: Two Intermediaries (Producer → Wholesaler → Retailer → Consumer)
This is the traditional and very common channel for Fast-Moving Consumer Goods (FMCGs).
 Examples: A PZ Cussons factory sells soap to a wholesaler in Onitsha Main Market,
who then sells to small retailers across the South-East, who then sell to consumers.
 Advantages: Provides a vital link for small producers and small retailers. The wholesaler
breaks bulk, providing financial and logistical support.
 Disadvantages: Longer time for products to reach the market, higher final price due to
multiple markups.
105
Channel Level 3: Three Intermediaries (Producer → Agent → Wholesaler → Retailer →
Consumer) An agent (a facilitator who does not take title) is added. This is common in
international trade or for small manufacturers entering new markets.
 Examples: A Nigerian cocoa bean farmer uses an export agent who sells the beans to a
foreign wholesaler, who then supplies chocolate manufacturers or retailers.
 Advantages: Agents provide specialized market knowledge and contacts.
 Disadvantages: The longest and most complex channel; the final consumer price is
highest.
3. THE VITAL ROLE OF INTERMEDIARIES: DISPELLING THE "MIDDLEMAN
MYTH"
A common misconception is that intermediaries simply add cost without adding value. In reality,
they perform essential functions that make the distribution system more efficient. They add
value by resolving the discrepancies between a producer's output and a consumer's needs.
A. The Wholesaler: The Bulk-Breaking Hub
106
 Bulk Breaking: Buying in large quantities from producers and selling in smaller
quantities to retailers. A retailer cannot buy 10,000 bars of soap, but a wholesaler can.
 Warehousing and Storage: Holding stock, creating time utility.
 Transportation: Arranging for the movement of goods from producers to their
warehouses and to retailers.
 Financing: Extending credit to retailers and paying producers promptly.
 Risk Bearing: Assuming the risk of theft, spoilage, or obsolescence of the goods they
hold.
 Market Information: Providing feedback to producers about market trends and
competitor activities.
B. The Retailer: The Final Touchpoint
 Convenience: Located close to consumers, offering easy access.
 Product Assortment: Providing a variety of products from different manufacturers in
one place.
 Breaking Bulk Further: Selling in unit quantities that consumers can afford.
 Customer Service: Providing advice, after-sales support, and handling returns.
 Promotion and Merchandising: Creating in-store displays and running local
promotions.
107
Illustration: How Intermediaries Create Efficiency
4. CHOOSING THE RIGHT PATH: FACTORS INFLUENCING CHANNEL CHOICE
There is no single "best" channel. The choice depends on a strategic analysis of several factors:
1. Product Characteristics:
o Perishability: Fresh fish, milk, and bread need very short channels (Level 0 or 1).
o Complexity/Technicality: High-tech products or custom machinery often use
direct channels (Level 0) for expert sales support.
o Value/Bulk: Low-value, high-bulk products (like cement) need channels that
minimize handling and transportation costs.
2. Market and Customer Factors:
o Geographic Dispersion: A mass market spread across the country (e.g., for soft
drinks) requires long channels with many intermediaries. A concentrated market
allows for a direct sales force.
108
o Purchase Behavior: How, when, and where do customers buy? For routine
purchases of FMCGs, customers expect intensive distribution in nearby stores.
3. Company Resources and Objectives:
o Financial Strength: A company with ample funds (like Dangote) can establish
its own retail outlets. A small startup may need to rely on wholesalers and agents.
o Desired Control: A company that wants to control its brand image, price, and
promotion (like Apple) will opt for shorter channels.
4. Competitive Landscape:
o A company may choose to distribute its products in the same outlets as its
competitors to facilitate comparison. Alternatively, it may choose a unique
channel to stand out (e.g., selling high-end cosmetics only in exclusive
department stores).

Topic: The Art of Creating Urgency and Action
Instructional Objectives: At the end of the lesson, students should be able to:

2. Analyze and categorize various sales promotion tools targeted at consumers and trade

3. Evaluate the strategic importance and potential limitations of sales promotion campaigns.

1. THE CONCEPT OF SALES PROMOTION: THE "ACTION" COMPONENT
Sales Promotion refers to a diverse collection of short-term incentive tools designed to stimulate
quicker or greater purchase of particular products or services by consumers or the trade.
 The "Why Buy Now?" Factor: While advertising gives consumers a reason to buy
(building long-term brand image), sales promotion gives them an incentive to buy now
(driving short-term action).
 The Bridge in the Promotional Mix: It serves as a crucial link between above-the-line
activities (like advertising) and below-the-line activities (like personal selling), making
the overall promotional effort more effective.
Key Characteristics:
 Short-term orientation: Designed for immediate impact.
 Offers an incentive: Tangible added value (price reduction, extra product, gift, etc.).
112
 Target-specific: Can be aimed at consumers, retailers, or wholesalers.
 Measurable: Results are often directly observable in sales figures.
2. THE SALES PROMOTION TOOLKIT: METHODS AND APPLICATIONS
Sales promotion strategies are typically divided into two main categories: those targeting
Consumers and those targeting the Trade (retailers and wholesalers).
A. Consumer Promotion Tools (Pull Strategy: Pulling products through the channel)
1. Price-Off Promotions (Discounts):
o A direct reduction in the product's price for a specified period.
o Examples: "50% Off," "Buy One Get One Free (BOGOF)," "₦500 Off."
o Objective: To clear old stock, attract price-sensitive customers, and counter
competition.
2. Coupons:
o Certificates that offer buyers a stated saving when they purchase a specific
product.
o Distribution Channels: Newspapers, magazines, in-store, online, mobile apps.
o Objective: To encourage trial of a new product, reward loyal users, and induce
brand switching.
3. Premiums (Gifts):
o Goods offered free or at a low cost as an incentive to buy a product.
o Types:
 Free-in-the-mail premium: Send proof of purchase to receive a gift.
 In-pack or On-pack premium: The gift is inside or attached to the
product package.
 Self-liquidating premium: The consumer pays a low price for the gift,
which covers its cost.
o Example: "Buy a large bottle of Hypo bleach and get a free washing sponge."
113
4. Free Samples:
o The most effective—but most expensive—way to introduce a new product. It
involves offering a small amount of a product for trial.
o Distribution: In-store tasting, door-to-door, included with other products, at
events.
o Objective: To break down barriers to trial and generate initial user experience.
5. Contests and Sweepstakes:
o Contests: Require some skill or effort (e.g., writing a jingle, solving a puzzle).
Winners are judged.
o Sweepstakes: Winners are chosen by pure luck (e.g., draws, scratch-and-win
cards).
o Objective: To create excitement and engagement, generate publicity, and build
mailing lists.
6. Loyalty Programs:
o Rewarding customers for their repeated business.
o Examples: Point-based systems (Shoprite Loyalty Card), tiered memberships
(frequent flyer programs).
o Objective: To increase customer retention and lifetime value.
B. Trade Promotion Tools (Push Strategy: Pushing products into the channel)
1. Trade Allowances:
o Advertising Allowance: Price reduction given to retailers for featuring the
manufacturer's product in their advertising.
o Display Allowance: Price reduction given for setting up a special in-store display.
2. Free Goods (Buying Allowances):
o Offering extra cases of merchandise to intermediaries who buy a certain quantity
or who buy a new product.
o Example: "Buy 10 cases, get 1 case free."
114
3. Trade Shows and Conventions:
o Industry-wide events where manufacturers can display their products, generate
leads, and build relationships with distributors and retailers.
o Example: The Lagos International Trade Fair.
3. THE STRATEGIC IMPACT: WHY COMPANIES USE SALES PROMOTION
A. The Importance (Advantages)
1. Stimulates Short-Term Sales: Creates a sense of urgency that can quickly reduce excess
inventory and boost cash flow.
2. Encourages Product Trial: Free samples and coupons are highly effective for getting
new customers to try a product for the first time.
3. Rewards and Retains Loyal Customers: Loyalty programs make existing customers
feel valued, reducing the chance they will switch to a competitor.
4. Complements Other Marketing Efforts: A sales promotion can reinforce an
advertising campaign by giving consumers a concrete reason to act on the message
they've seen.
5. Levels the Playing Field: A smaller brand can use aggressive sales promotions to gain
shelf space and attention away from larger, more established competitors.
B. The Limitations (Disadvantages)
1. Short-Lived Results: Sales often spike during the promotion and then fall sharply
afterward. This is known as the "post-promotion dip."
2. Brand Image Erosion: Overuse of price promotions can train consumers to buy only on
deal, damaging the brand's perceived quality and value. A brand known for constant
discounts may be seen as "cheap."
3. High Cost: Promotions can be expensive to administer (printing coupons, producing
premiums) and eat into profit margins.
4. Forward Buying and Diversion:
115
o Forward Buying: Retailers stock up during a manufacturer's promotion and then
sell the goods at the regular price after the promotion ends, pocketing the extra
profit.
o Diversion: Wholesalers or retailers buy large quantities in a region where a
promotion is running and then ship the goods to another region to sell.
4. DESIGNING AN EFFECTIVE SALES PROMOTION
For a sales promotion to be successful, it must be part of a clear plan:
1. Set Objectives: What exactly do you want to achieve? (e.g., increase market share by 5%,
clear 10,000 units of old stock).
2. Select the Right Tool: Choose the tool that best fits your objective, target audience, and
budget.
3. Develop the Program: Decide on the details: duration, terms and conditions, and how it
will be communicated.
4. Implement and Control: Launch the promotion and monitor its execution.
5. Evaluate the Results: Measure the campaign's success against the original objectives.

1. THE CONCEPT OF ADVERTISING: PAID, NON-PERSONAL PERSUASION
Advertising is any paid form of non-personal presentation and promotion of ideas, goods, or
services by an identified sponsor.
Let's break down this definition:
 Paid For: Unlike public relations, which earns "free" media coverage, advertising space
or time must be purchased. This guarantees control over the message's content, placement,
and timing.
 Non-Personal: It communicates with a large audience simultaneously, not on a one-toone
basis like personal selling. There is no immediate interaction or feedback.
 Identified Sponsor: The advertiser is clearly known. This creates accountability and is
used to build brand recognition.
Advertising in the Promotional Mix: Advertising is a "pull" strategy, designed to create
consumer demand that pulls the product through the distribution channel. It works alongside
sales promotion, personal selling, and public relations to create a cohesive communication
strategy.

2. THE MEDIA LANDSCAPE: CHOOSING THE RIGHT CHANNEL
The choice of advertising medium is a critical strategic decision, balancing reach, cost, impact,
and target audience. Media can be categorized as above-the-line (mass media) and below-theline
(more targeted).
Illustration: The Advertising Media Spectrum
A. Broadcast Media (Wide Reach, High Impact)
1. Television:
o Strengths: Combines sight, sound, and motion for high emotional impact; mass
reach; high credibility.
o Weaknesses: Extremely high production and airtime costs; fleeting message
(cannot be revisited); audience fragmentation due to multiple channels.
o Best For: Building brand personality and launching new products to a mass
market. Example: MTN's "Everywhere You Go" campaign.
2. Radio:
119
o Strengths: High frequency and reach, especially in rural areas; low cost; mobile
and local; uses imagination and sound effects.
o Weaknesses: Audio only (no visual element); background medium (listeners are
often doing other things); short message life.
o Best For: Local businesses, time-sensitive promotions, and reinforcing messages
from other media. Example: OAPs (On-Air Personalities) promoting a local
restaurant.
B. Print Media (Tangible and Detailed)
1. Newspapers:
o Strengths: Good local coverage; timely (daily); high credibility; readers can clip
and save coupons.
o Weaknesses: Short lifespan (one day); poor print quality; cluttered environment;
declining readership.
o Best For: Sales promotions, local events, and job advertisements.
2. Magazines:
o Strengths: High-quality printing; long lifespan (kept for weeks); targeted
audience (by interest); high pass-along readership.
o Weaknesses: Long lead times (ads must be placed weeks in advance); higher cost
than newspapers.
o Best For: Products that benefit from high-quality visuals and target specific
lifestyles (e.g., fashion, travel, business).
C. Out-of-Home (OOH) Media (Constant Reminders)
1. Billboards:
o Strengths: High visibility and frequency; geographic flexibility; operates 24/7.
o Weaknesses: Brief exposure (glance from a moving vehicle); limited message
complexity ("glance media"); can be subject to weather damage.
120
o Best For: Brand reinforcement and simple, visual messages. Example: A large
billboard for a new housing estate.
2. Transit Advertising:
o Ads on buses, trains, taxis, and at bus stops.
o Strengths: Reaches a captive, mobile audience; highly localised.
o Weaknesses: Cluttered environment; limited to transit routes.
D. Digital Media (Interactive and Measurable)
1. Social Media Advertising:
o Ads on platforms like Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, and X (Twitter).
o Strengths: Highly targeted by demographics, interests, and behavior; interactive
(likes, shares, comments); measurable ROI.
o Weaknesses: Ad fatigue; privacy concerns; constantly changing algorithms.
2. Search Engine Marketing (SEM):
o Paid ads that appear on search engine results pages (e.g., Google Ads).
o Strengths: High intent (reaches people actively searching for a product); pay-perclick
(PPC) model is cost-effective.
o Weaknesses: Can be competitive and expensive for popular keywords.

3. THE STRATEGIC FUNCTIONS OF ADVERTISING
Advertising is not just about making a sale; it serves multiple strategic functions throughout the
customer journey:
1. Informative Function:
o To introduce new products and educate the market about features, uses, and
benefits. Example: An ad for a new smartphone explaining its camera capabilities.
2. Persuasive Function:
o To persuade customers that a brand is the best choice, often by comparing it to
competitors or appealing to emotions. Example: A Pepsi ad implying it is the
choice of a new, cool generation.
3. Reminder Function:
o To keep the brand top-of-mind for consumers, reinforcing previous messages.
This is crucial for mature products. Example: Constant Coca-Cola ads reminding
people of the brand's association with happiness.
4. Reinforcement Function:
122
o To assure current customers they made the right choice, reducing cognitive
dissonance. Example: An ad showing satisfied Toyota owners.
5. Brand Image Building:
o To create a unique personality and set of associations for a brand over the long
term. Example: Apple's ads project an image of innovation, simplicity, and
creativity.
6. Support for Other Channels:
o It "paves the way" for salespeople by creating awareness and interest, making the
salesperson's job easier.
4. A CRITICAL VIEW: THE LIMITATIONS AND ETHICAL CONCERNS
While powerful, advertising faces significant challenges and criticisms:
1. High Cost:
o Producing a high-quality TV commercial and buying prime-time airtime can cost
millions of Naira, making it inaccessible for small businesses.
2. Media Clutter:
o The average person is exposed to thousands of commercial messages daily.
Breaking through this clutter to get noticed is increasingly difficult.
3. Lack of Immediate Feedback:
o As a one-way communication channel, it doesn't allow for immediate questions or
objections from the audience.
4. Persuasion vs. Manipulation:
o Criticism: Advertising can create artificial needs, making people feel
dissatisfied with what they have and desire things they do not truly need, leading
to materialism.
123
o Ethical Concern: Some ads use subliminal messages (messages below the
threshold of conscious awareness) or exploit emotions in unethical ways.
5. Deceptive and Misleading Advertising:
o Exaggerating product benefits or making false claims (e.g., a "miracle" cream that
promises to remove wrinkles instantly). This is illegal and regulated by bodies
like Nigeria's Advertising Practitioners Council of Nigeria (APCON).
6. Cultural and Social Impact:
o Advertising can perpetuate negative stereotypes (e.g., always showing women
cleaning or men as bosses). It also has a powerful influence on children's values
and desires.

2. THE SALES PROCESS: A ROADMAP TO SUCCESS
Effective personal selling is not a random event; it is a structured process known as the Sales
Cycle. Understanding these steps is crucial for any sales professional.
Step 1: Prospecting and Qualifying
 What it is: Identifying potential customers ("prospects") and determining if they have the
need, authority, budget, and willingness to buy.
 Methods: Referrals from existing customers, cold calling, networking at events, using
social media (e.g., LinkedIn).
 Key Question: "Is this person a good fit for what I'm selling?"
Step 2: Pre-approach
 What it is: The research and planning stage before making contact. This is where the
salesperson does their homework.
 Activities: Researching the prospect's company, industry, and potential needs. Setting
clear objectives for the sales call.
 Key Question: "What do I need to know to provide value to this prospect immediately?"
Step 3: The Approach
 What it is: The initial contact with the prospect. The goal is to make a good first
impression, build rapport, and secure permission to proceed with the presentation.
 Techniques: A reference from a mutual contact, a comment on a recent company
achievement, or a thought-provoking question about their business.
 Key Goal: To transition smoothly from the introduction to the needs-discovery phase.
Step 4: Presentation and Demonstration
 What it is: The core of the process, where the salesperson presents how their product or
service solves the prospect's specific problems.
 Modern Approach: This is not a monologue. It's a dialogue focused on benefits, not just
features. Using the FAB technique is crucial:
128
o Feature: A factual characteristic of the product (e.g., "This phone has a 48MP
camera.").
o Advantage: What the feature does (e.g., "...which allows you to capture incredibly
detailed photos.").
o Benefit: How that advantage helps the customer (e.g., "...so you can preserve your
family memories with stunning clarity.").
 Key Principle: "Sell the sizzle, not the steak." Sell the benefit and the outcome.
Step 5: Handling Objections
 What it is: Prospects will naturally have concerns or objections (e.g., "It's too
expensive," "I need to think about it"). A good salesperson doesn't fear these; they
welcome them as signs of interest.
 Techniques:
o Listen Fully: Let the prospect voice their entire objection.
o Acknowledge and Validate: "I understand why cost is a concern."
o Respond: Provide information to overcome the objection. For "It's too
expensive," you might reframe it as an investment, highlight long-term value, or
break down the cost.
 Key Mindset: An objection is not a rejection; it is a request for more information.
Step 6: Closing the Sale
 What it is: Asking for the order. This is the step many find difficult, but it is the ultimate
goal of the process.
 Closing Techniques:
o The Assumptive Close: "Shall we get the delivery started for next Tuesday?"
o The Alternative Choice Close: "Would you prefer the blue model or the black
one?"
o The Summary Close: Summarizing the agreed-upon benefits and then asking for
the order.
 Key Action: You must ask for the business.
129
Step 7: Follow-up
 What it is: The process does not end with the sale. Follow-up ensures customer
satisfaction, resolves any post-purchase issues, and lays the foundation for repeat
business and referrals.
 Activities: A thank-you call, checking that the product was delivered and works correctly,
providing ongoing support.
 Key Benefit: It costs far less to retain an existing customer than to acquire a new one.

WEEK 4: PUBLIC RELATIONS (PR) IN MARKETING

1. THE CONCEPT OF PUBLIC RELATIONS: EARNING TRUST, NOT BUYING IT
Public Relations (PR) is the strategic management function that identifies, establishes, and
maintains mutually beneficial relationships between an organization and the various publics on
whom its success or failure depends.
 The "Publics" in PR: These are all the groups an organization interacts with, including
customers, employees, investors, media, government, local communities, and suppliers.
 The Core Idea: While advertising is about promoting a product, PR is about building a
reputation. It's the art of earning, rather than buying, favorable public perception and
goodwill.
Key Characteristics of PR:
 Third-Party Endorsement: PR often works through journalists and influencers, making
the message more credible than a paid ad.
 Long-Term Orientation: Focused on building and maintaining a positive image over
time.
 Two-Way Communication: PR listens to public sentiment and communicates the
organization's perspective.
134
 Management Function: It is not just a tactical tool; it informs senior management
decision-making to align company actions with public expectations.
2. THE STRATEGIC FUNCTIONS OF PR IN MARKETING
PR supports marketing by creating a favorable environment in which to sell. It builds the brand's
"social license" to operate.
1. Media Relations and Publicity:
o Building strong relationships with journalists, bloggers, and influencers to secure
positive, unpaid media coverage.
o Publicity is the visible output of this function—a news story, a feature article, or
a segment on TV.
2. Product Publicity:
o Specifically focused on generating buzz and awareness for new products or
services. This can be more effective than advertising for a launch because it
carries the credibility of the media outlet.
135
o Example: Organizing a press conference to launch a new smartphone, resulting in
tech reviews and news articles.
3. Corporate Communications:
o Shaping and communicating the overall identity of the organization. This includes
managing the corporate website, annual reports, and internal newsletters to
present a consistent and positive image.
4. Public Affairs and Lobbying:
o Managing relationships with government bodies and influencing public policy
that affects the organization.
o Example: A telecommunications company lobbying the government for better
spectrum allocation.
5. Crisis Management:
o The most critical PR function. It involves preparing for, responding to, and
recovering from events that could damage the organization's reputation (e.g.,
product recalls, scandals, accidents).
o Goal: To protect the brand and restore public trust as quickly as possible.
6. Social Responsibility (CSR):
o Initiating and promoting programs that benefit society, demonstrating that the
company is a good corporate citizen.
o Example: A company like Dangote Group funding health initiatives or MTN
providing scholarships.

PR professionals use a diverse set of tools to execute their strategies.
1. Press Release (News Release):

2. Media Kit:
o A package of information given to journalists, containing press releases, fact

o Example: MTN sponsoring the Nigerian Football Federation or Milo sponsoring

5. Special Events:
o Hosting events like factory open days, anniversary celebrations, or product

6. Social Media Management:
o Using platforms like Twitter, LinkedIn, and Instagram to engage directly with the
public, share news, and manage the brand's online reputation in real-time.
4. THE POWER OF PR: STRATEGIC IMPORTANCE
1. Credibility and Trust:
o A positive story written by a journalist is perceived as more objective and credible
than a paid advertisement. This "earned media" is trusted more.
2. Cost-Effectiveness:
137

3. Crisis Shield and Recovery:

4. Attracts and Retains Talent:
o A company known for its good reputation and social responsibility finds it easier

5. Enhances Other Marketing Efforts:

WEEK 6: CONSUMER PROTECTION

1. THE CONCEPT OF CONSUMER PROTECTION: RESTORING THE BALANCE
Consumer Protection refers to the laws, regulations, and institutional frameworks designed to
ensure the rights of consumers are protected, and to shield them from unfair, deceptive, and
dangerous business practices.
 The Power Imbalance: In the modern marketplace, individual consumers often have less
information, resources, and power than the large corporations they buy from. Consumer
protection exists to correct this imbalance.
 Foundation of Trust: Effective consumer protection is not anti-business; it is pro-market.
It builds the trust necessary for commerce to flourish. When consumers trust that
products are safe and claims are truthful, they are more likely to buy, which benefits
ethical businesses.
Why is Consumer Protection Necessary?
 To prevent the sale of hazardous or substandard products.
 To ensure fair competition and prevent monopolistic exploitation.
 To provide legal recourse for consumers who have been wronged.
 To empower consumers with information to make informed choices.
142
2. THE CONSUMER'S CHARTER: THE EIGHT FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS
The modern consumer rights movement is based on a framework established by President John F.
Kennedy in 1962, which has since been expanded. These rights form the foundation of consumer
protection laws worldwide, including in Nigeria.
1. The Right to Safety
o Meaning: Protection against products, production processes, and services that are
hazardous to health or life.
o Example: The right to expect that electrical appliances won't electrocute you, that
toys won't choke children, and that drugs won't have fatal side effects.
o Agency Role: NAFDAC ensures the safety of food and drugs; SON sets safety
standards for other products.
2. The Right to be Informed
143
o Meaning: Protection against fraudulent, deceitful, or grossly misleading
information, and the right to be given the facts needed to make an informed
choice.
o Example: Clear labeling of ingredients, nutritional facts, expiry dates, and
country of origin. A landlord must not hide structural defects in a house.
o Agency Role: FCCPC acts against false advertising.
3. The Right to Choose
o Meaning: The ability to select from a range of products and services at
competitive prices. This right is violated by monopolies and cartels that limit
choices and fix prices.
o Example: Having multiple telecom providers (MTN, Airtel, Glo, 9mobile) to
choose from, rather than being forced to use a single, state-owned provider.
o Agency Role: FCCPC promotes competition and prevents monopolistic practices.
4. The Right to be Heard
o Meaning: The guarantee that consumer interests will receive full and sympathetic
consideration in the formulation of government policy and fair treatment in the
legal system. Consumers have the right to complain and be taken seriously.
o Example: The ability to file a complaint with the FCCPC about a company's
practices and have that complaint investigated.
5. Right to Redress
o Meaning: The right to receive a fair settlement of just claims, including
compensation for misrepresentation, shoddy goods, or unsatisfactory services.
o Forms of Redress: Repair, replacement, refund, or compensation.
o Example: Returning a faulty television to the store and getting a full refund or a
new replacement set.
6. The Right to Consumer Education
o Meaning: The right to acquire the knowledge and skills needed to be an informed
consumer throughout life.
144
o Example: School curricula that include consumer rights, public awareness
campaigns by the FCCPC on how to avoid scams, and information on how to file
a complaint.
7. The Right to a Healthy Environment
o Meaning: The right to live and work in an environment that is non-threatening to
the well-being of present and future generations.
o Example: Protection from pollution caused by industrial waste, noisy factories in
residential areas, or the dumping of toxic materials.
o Agency Role: NESREA (National Environmental Standards and Regulations
Enforcement Agency) is key here.
8. The Right to Satisfaction of Basic Needs
o Meaning: The right to have access to essential goods and services: adequate food,
clothing, shelter, healthcare, education, and sanitation.
o Example: Government policies aimed at ensuring food security and affordable
housing.
3. THE GUARDIANS: CONSUMER PROTECTION AGENCIES IN NIGERIA
Nigeria has established several specialized agencies to enforce these rights.
1. FCCPC (Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission)
145
o Role: The apex consumer protection body in Nigeria. It has a broad mandate to
protect consumers from unfair business practices and promote fair competition.
o Functions: Investigates consumer complaints, prosecutes offending businesses,
prohibits abusive and monopolistic practices, and promotes public consumer
education.
2. NAFDAC (National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control)
o Role: Regulates and controls the manufacture, importation, exportation,
distribution, advertisement, and use of food, drugs, cosmetics, medical devices,
and chemicals.
o Key Function: Ensures product safety. A NAFDAC registration number on a
product is a key indicator of its safety for consumption/use.
3. SON (Standards Organisation of Nigeria)
o Role: To prepare standards relating to products, measurements, materials, and
processes, and to certify products that meet these standards.
o Key Function: Ensures product quality and reliability. The SONCAP Certificate
is a mandatory conformity assessment program for imported goods.
4. Sector-Specific Regulators:
o CBN (Central Bank of Nigeria): Protects users of financial services from
exploitation, fraud, and excessive bank charges.
o NCC (Nigerian Communications Commission): Protects telecom consumers
from issues like unauthorized subscription, poor service quality, and unfair billing.
o NERC (Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission): Handles complaints
from electricity consumers.
o NDLEA (National Drug Law Enforcement Agency): Protects consumers from
the dangers of illicit drugs.
4. CONTEMPORARY CHALLENGES AND SOLUTIONS
Despite these agencies, significant challenges remain in protecting Nigerian consumers.
146
A. Common Marketplace Abuses:
 Sale of Substandard and Counterfeit Goods: Fake products, from drugs to phone
batteries, flood the market.
 False and Misleading Advertising: "Get rich quick" schemes, "miracle" cures, and
exaggerated product claims.
 Exploitative Terms and Conditions: Hidden fees in service contracts, particularly in
banking and telecommunications.
 Poor After-Sales Service: Difficulty getting repairs, honouring warranties, or receiving
refunds.
B. Hindrances to Effective Protection:
1. Lack of Awareness: Many consumers are unaware of their rights or how to enforce them.
2. Weak Enforcement: Regulatory agencies may be underfunded or understaffed, limiting
their reach and impact.
3. Lengthy Legal Processes: Seeking redress through the courts can be slow, complex, and
expensive.
4. Cultural Apathy: A tendency to accept poor service or faulty goods with resignation ("It
is Nigeria").
C. Empowering Solutions:
1. Consumer Education: Mass literacy campaigns in schools and through the media.
2. Strengthening Institutions: Providing more funding and autonomy to agencies like the
FCCPC.
3. Utilizing Technology: Creating easy-to-use mobile apps and online portals for filing
complaints (e.g., the FCCPC's reporting platform).
4. Consumer Advocacy: Supporting the work of non-governmental organizations (NGOs)
that fight for consumer rights.

5. THE CONSUMER'S RESPONSIBILITY: "BUYER BEWARE" IS NOT ENOUGH
While rights are crucial, responsible consumer behavior is the first line of defense.
The Responsible Consumer:
 Is Informed: Researches products before buying, reads labels, and checks for standard
marks (NAFDAC, SON).
 Keeps Proof of Purchase: Always collects receipts, invoices, and warranty cards.
 Speaks Up: Complains promptly and firmly to the seller or service provider when
dissatisfied.
 Reports Violations: Takes serious complaints (especially about safety) to the relevant
protection agency.
 Makes Ethical Choices: Considers the environmental and social impact of their
purchases.

Topic: Building a Future in a Dynamic Profession
Instructional Objectives: At the end of the lesson, students should be able to:

1. THE MARKETING PROFESSION: BEYOND SELLING
Marketing is not a single job but a vast and dynamic ecosystem of interrelated professions. It is
the engine that drives business growth by connecting products and services with the customers
who need them. Every organization—from multinational corporations to non-profits and
government agencies—requires marketing expertise.
The Evolution of Marketing Careers:
 Traditional Focus: Advertising, sales, and public relations.
 Modern Expansion: Digital marketing, data analytics, customer experience management,
and content strategy.
2. THE MARKETING CAREER LANDSCAPE: A SPECTRUM OF ROLES
Marketing careers can be broadly categorized into strategic, creative, analytical, and salesoriented
paths.
157
A. Strategic Roles (The Planners & Leaders)
1. Marketing Manager:
o Role: The conductor of the marketing orchestra. They develop, implement, and
execute the overall strategic marketing plan for a company, product line, or region.
o Key Responsibilities: Setting budgets, managing a team, overseeing campaigns,
and analyzing market trends.
o Skills Needed: Leadership, strategic thinking, budgeting, analytical skills.
2. Brand Manager:
o Role: The guardian of the brand's soul. They are responsible for the image,
perception, and market performance of a specific brand.
o Key Responsibilities: Developing brand strategies, managing product launches,
overseeing advertising, and ensuring all marketing is on-brand.
o Skills Needed: Creativity, market research, communication, project management.
o Example: The manager for the Maltina brand at Nigerian Breweries.
3. Product Manager:
158
o Role: The voice of the customer for a product. They guide the success of a
product from conception to launch and beyond.
o Key Responsibilities: Defining product vision, working with
engineering/development teams, setting pricing, and creating a product roadmap.
o Skills Needed: Technical understanding, strategic planning, user empathy.
B. Creative & Content Roles (The Storytellers)
1. Copywriter:
o Role: The wordsmith. They craft the persuasive and engaging text (copy) for
advertisements, websites, emails, social media, and brochures.
o Skills Needed: Exceptional writing skills, creativity, understanding of consumer
psychology.
2. Content Marketing Manager:
o Role: The publisher. They create and distribute valuable, relevant content (blogs,
videos, podcasts, infographics) to attract and retain a target audience.
o Skills Needed: Storytelling, SEO knowledge, editorial planning, multimedia
skills.
3. Art Director / Graphic Designer:
o Role: The visual storyteller. They are responsible for the visual style and images
in magazines, newspapers, product packaging, and marketing materials.
o Skills Needed: Graphic design software (e.g., Adobe Creative Suite), creativity,
visual communication.
C. Analytical & Research Roles (The Data Detectives)
1. Market Research Analyst:
o Role: The insights generator. They study market conditions to examine potential
sales of a product or service.
o Key Responsibilities: Designing surveys, conducting focus groups, analyzing
data on consumer demographics, preferences, and buying habits.
159
o Skills Needed: Statistical analysis, critical thinking, data interpretation,
familiarity with research tools.
2. Data Analyst (Marketing):
o Role: The performance decoder. They analyze marketing campaign data (website
traffic, conversion rates, social media engagement) to measure ROI and optimize
future efforts.
o Skills Needed: Data analysis tools (e.g., Google Analytics, Excel, SQL),
analytical mindset, problem-solving.
D. Digital & Growth Roles (The Online Experts)
1. Digital Marketing Manager:
o Role: The online campaign leader. They specialize in planning and executing
marketing campaigns across digital channels.
o Skills Needed: Knowledge of SEO/SEM, social media, email marketing, and data
analytics.
2. Social Media Manager:
o Role: The community builder. They represent a company across social channels,
building and nurturing an online community.
o Key Responsibilities: Creating content calendars, engaging with followers,
running social media ads, and monitoring online conversations about the brand.
o Skills Needed: Creativity, excellent communication, knowledge of social
platforms, customer service skills.
3. SEO/SEM Specialist:
o Role: The visibility engineer. They optimize websites to rank higher in search
engine results (SEO) and manage paid search advertising campaigns (SEM).
o Skills Needed: Technical SEO knowledge, keyword research, experience with
platforms like Google Ads.

3. THE PATH TO SUCCESS: SKILLS AND QUALIFICATIONS
Success in marketing requires a blend of formal education, practical skills, and personal
attributes.
A. Educational Pathways:
 Foundation: A bachelor's degree in Marketing, Business Administration, Mass
Communication, Economics, or Psychology is a common entry point.
 Professional Certification: Enhances credibility and specialized knowledge.
o NIMN (National Institute of Marketing of Nigeria): The premier professional
body for marketers in Nigeria. Becoming a certified member (e.g., AMNIMN,
MNIMN) is highly regarded.
o Digital Marketing Certifications: Google Ads Certification, Google Analytics
Certification, HubSpot Content Marketing Certification, etc.

4. CAREER PROSPECTS AND FUTURE OUTLOOK
 High Demand: Marketing is a core business function, ensuring a constant demand for
skilled professionals. The digital shift has created entirely new roles faster than they can
be filled.
 Competitive Salaries: Marketing careers can be very lucrative, especially for managerial,
specialized digital, and analytical roles.
 Versatility and Mobility: The skills acquired in marketing are transferable across
virtually every industry (FMCG, tech, healthcare, finance, entertainment) and can be a
pathway to senior leadership positions like Chief Marketing Officer (CMO) or even CEO.
 The Future is Digital and Data-Driven: The marketers of tomorrow will need to be
even more proficient in data analysis, marketing technology (MarTech), and
understanding the customer journey across digital and physical touchpoints.
