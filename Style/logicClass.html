Why a classroom? Logic is the study of valid reasoning, and there is no better place to watch reasoning happen than in a room full of students answering questions, making claims, and occasionally getting things wrong. Every statement, connective, and truth table below is built from things Mrs. Adeyemi, Tunde, Ngozi, and the rest of JSS 3B actually say and do. By the end, you will not be memorising abstract symbols — you will be reading the classroom.
Section A

The Concepts, as They Happen in the Room

1. Statements and Truth Values — Claims That Can Be Checked

A statement is a sentence that is unambiguously either true or false — you could, in principle, walk into the classroom and verify it. Questions, commands, and exclamations are not statements because they cannot be labelled true or false.

P = "Tunde submitted his mathematics homework."   (True or False?) In the room: Mrs. Adeyemi asks the class for statements about today's lesson. Fatima says, "Ngozi is wearing a green shirt." — a statement, because it can be checked. Chidi says, "Please open the window." — not a statement, because it is a command. Amina says, "What is the answer to number five?" — not a statement, because it is a question.

2. Negation — NOT (¬) — The Opposite Claim

The negation of a statement is the claim that the original is false. It flips the truth value: what was true becomes false, and what was false becomes true.

3. Conjunction — AND (∧) — Both Conditions Must Hold

P ∧ Q is true only when both claims are true. It is the strict rule: one failure collapses the whole statement. Mrs. Adeyemi's classroom rules are built entirely on conjunctions.

4. Disjunction — OR (∨) — At Least One Must Hold

P ∨ Q is true when at least one claim is true. This is the inclusive "or" — being in either group qualifies you, and being in both qualifies you too.

5. Implication — IF…THEN (→) — A Promise That Can Be Broken

P → Q is a promise: if P happens, then Q must follow. It is false only when the promise is broken — that is, when P is true but Q turns out false. When P never happens, no promise was made, so nothing is broken.

In the room: Mrs. Adeyemi promises, "If you score above 80 in the test, you will get a gold star." Fatima scores 85 and gets the star — promise kept. Chidi scores 85 but does not get the star — promise broken, the only failure. Bola scores 60 and gets no star — nothing was promised, so no complaint. Amina scores 60 but receives a star for neatness — still fine, the promise was never about her.

6. Biconditional — IF AND ONLY IF (↔) — Standing or Falling Together

P ↔ Q is true when the two statements carry the same truth value. It means each depends on the other completely — neither can be true without the other.

In the room: The school's official rule: "A student is the class prefect if and only if the student is the highest scorer in the term's assessments." Fatima is the highest scorer, so she is prefect. If Fatima were prefect but not the highest scorer, the rule would be broken. If Fatima were the highest scorer but not prefect, also broken. The two facts stand or fall together.

7. Tautology, Contradiction, and Contingency — Always, Never, or Sometimes

A tautology is true in every possible state of the classroom. A contradiction is false in every possible state. A contingency depends on what actually happens.

Tautology  →  all T   •   Contradiction  →  all F In the room: "Either Ngozi is in class today, or Ngozi is not in class today." — this is a tautology; it cannot fail no matter what. "Ngozi is in class today and Ngozi is not in class today." — a contradiction; impossible. "Ngozi is in class today." — a contingency; it depends on the day.

8. Logical Equivalence and the Classroom Laws

Two statements are logically equivalent (written ≡) if they behave identically across every possible classroom situation. The standard laws are simply sentences from the room, rewritten.

De Morgan: ¬(P ∧ Q) ≡ ¬P ∨ ¬Q Implication: P → Q ≡ ¬P ∨ Q In the room: Mrs. Adeyemi says, "It is not the case that both Tunde and Ngozi passed." De Morgan tells us this is the same as saying, "Either Tunde did not pass, or Ngozi did not pass." Same rule, stated two ways.
Section B

Practice Questions

Questions 1–25
  1. Mrs. Adeyemi writes four sentences on the board. State which are statements: (a) "Fatima is the class prefect." (b) "Sit down and open your books." (c) "Emeka reads in the library every break." (d) "How many students are present today?"
  2. Write the negation of each claim: (a) "Tunde submitted his homework." (b) "Ngozi plays football after school."
  3. Let P = "Bola is new this term" and Q = "Bola sits at the front." Write in words: P ∧ Q.
  4. Using the same P and Q, write in words: P ∨ Q.
  5. Using the same P and Q, write in words: P → Q.
  6. Let P = "Chidi brought his textbook" (True) and Q = "Chidi brought his workbook" (False). Evaluate P ∧ Q.
  7. Using the same values as above, evaluate P ∨ Q.
  8. Using the same values, evaluate P → Q.
  9. Let P = "Amina sits by the window" (False) and Q = "Amina sits at the back" (False). Evaluate P ↔ Q.
  10. Let P = "Fatima is prepared" (True) and Q = "The lesson starts on time" (True). Evaluate ¬(P ∧ Q).
  11. Construct the truth table for P ∧ ¬Q, where P = "The library is open" and Q = "Emeka is reading there."
  12. Construct the truth table for ¬P ∨ Q, where P = "It is raining" and Q = "Break is held indoors."
  13. Construct the truth table for (P ∨ Q) ∧ ¬P, where P = "Tunde is in the science club" and Q = "Tunde is in the drama club."
  14. Construct the truth table for P → (Q ∨ R), where P = "Mrs. Adeyemi gives homework," Q = "The students complain," and R = "The students complete it quietly."
  15. Show, using a truth table, that P ∨ ¬P is a tautology, where P = "Ngozi is present in class today."
  16. Show, using a truth table, that P ∧ ¬P is a contradiction, where P = "The bell has rung for break."
  17. Classify P → Q as a tautology, contradiction, or contingency, where P = "Chidi studies" and Q = "Chidi passes."
  18. Mrs. Adeyemi says, "It is not the case that both Tunde and Ngozi passed." Use De Morgan's law to rewrite this statement.
  19. Mrs. Adeyemi says, "It is not the case that either Emeka or Fatima will represent the class." Use De Morgan's law to rewrite this statement.
  20. Rewrite P → Q using only ¬ and ∨, where P = "You finish your assignment" and Q = "You go out to play."
  21. Write the contrapositive of: "If a student is in JSS 3B, then that student is in the science block."
  22. Write the converse of: "If it rains, break is held indoors." Is the converse logically equivalent to the original?
  23. Determine whether P → Q and ¬P ∨ Q are logically equivalent, using a truth table. Let P = "Amina finishes her homework" and Q = "Amina is allowed to play."
  24. Determine whether P ∧ (Q ∨ R) and (P ∧ Q) ∨ (P ∧ R) are logically equivalent, where P = "Fatima brings her notes," Q = "Fatima brings her textbook," and R = "Fatima brings her calculator."
  25. Mrs. Adeyemi tells the class, "You will go on the excursion only if you submit your permission slip." Using P = "You go on the excursion" and Q = "You submit your permission slip," express this as a logical implication. State whether this is P → Q or Q → P, and explain why.
