WEEK 1
SPEECH WORK: LISTENING AND SPEAKING – REVISION OF PURE VOWEL
SOUNDS
Objective
By the end of this detailed lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define and explain the concept of a pure vowel (monophthong) versus a diphthong.
2. Accurately identify and list all twelve (12) pure vowel sounds by their phonetic symbols.
3. Orally produce all 12 sounds with correct lip and tongue placement.
4. Distinguish between long and short vowels in spoken and written minimal pairs.
Introduction
Speech sounds are the foundation of clear communication. Vowel sounds are particularly
important as they form the "peak" of every syllable. A Pure Vowel, or Monophthong, is a
single, unchanging vowel sound produced by a fixed position of the tongue and lips (e.g., the 'ee'
in 'see'). This is different from a diphthong, which is a glide from one vowel position to another
(e.g., the 'oi' in 'boy').
We will review the 12 pure vowels, grouping them by tongue position (Front, Central, Back)
and length (Short vs. Long).
A. The Short Vowel Sounds (7)
Front Vowels (Tongue forward in the mouth):
1. /ɪ/: (Short 'i') A high-front vowel.
o Examples: pit, sit, him, busy, village
2. /e/: (Short 'e') A mid-front vowel.
2
o Examples: pen, bed, head, said, many
3. /æ/: (Short 'a') A low-front vowel.
o Examples: cat, man, bag, apple, plaid
Central Vowels (Tongue in the middle): 4. /ʌ/: (Short 'u') A low-mid central vowel. *
Examples: cut, luck, son, blood, enough 5. /ə/: (The Schwa) An unstressed, mid-central vowel.
This is the most common sound in English. * Examples: about, teacher, doctor, pencil,
photograph
Back Vowels (Tongue pulled back): 6. /ɒ/: (Short 'o') A low-back, rounded vowel. * Examples:
not, pot, cot, wash, what 7. /ʊ/: (Short 'oo') A high-back, rounded vowel. * Examples: put, foot,
could, woman, book
B. The Long Vowel Sounds (5)
Front Vowels:
1. /iː/: (Long 'ee') A high-front vowel.
o Examples: sheep, feet, sea, me, believe
Central Vowels: 2. /ɜː/: (Long 'er') A mid-central vowel. * Examples: bird, learn, fur, work,
journey
Back Vowels: 3. /ɑː/: (Long 'ah') A low-back vowel. * Examples: father, car, star, pass, clerk 4.
/ɔː/: (Long 'or') A mid-back, rounded vowel. * Examples: door, board, caught, war, four, all 5.
/uː/: (Long 'oo') A high-back, rounded vowel. * Examples: food, blue, loop, group, through
C. Practical Drill: Minimal Pairs
Minimal pairs are two words that sound identical except for one sound. This is the best way to
practice listening and speaking.
 /ɪ/ vs /iː/: ship/sheep, sit/seat, fill/feel, grin/green
 /e/ vs /æ/: pen/pan, bed/bad, met/mat
 /ʌ/ vs /ɑː/: cut/cart, bun/barn, cup/carp
3
 /ɒ/ vs /ɔː/: pot/port, cot/caught, shot/short
 /ʊ/ vs /uː/: pull/pool, full/fool, look/Luke
Evaluation
1. In your own words, what is the main difference between a monophthong and a
diphthong?
2. Listen as I say the following words: "head," "heart," "hot." Transcribe the phonetic
symbol for the vowel sound in each word.
3. Which vowel sound is known as the 'schwa', and in what part of a word is it most

4. Provide two different spelling patterns (e.g., 'ea', 'ee') that can produce the /iː/ sound.
5. Which word in this pair has a long vowel sound: sit/seat, full/fool, bad/bed?
Assignment
1. For each of the following long vowel sounds, write two words that contain it: /ɑː/, /ɔː/,

2. Transcribe the pure vowel sound found in each of these words: work, cat, me, could,

3. Write three minimal pairs that contrast a short vowel with a long vowel (e.g., pit/peat).
4. Write a sentence that contains at least one example of the /æ/ sound and one example of

GRAMMAR: REVISION OF PARTS OF SPEECH – NOUNS, PRONOUNS, VERBS
AND ADJECTIVES
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. Define and provide multiple examples for each of the four parts of speech.
2. Differentiate between the sub-classes of each part of speech (e.g., abstract vs. concrete
nouns, auxiliary vs. lexical verbs).
3. Identify the function of these parts of speech within a sentence (e.g., noun as a subject,
adjective as a modifier).
4. Correctly apply the rules of adjectives (e.g., order of adjectives).
Introduction
Words are the building blocks of sentences, and each word has a specific job. We call these jobs
"Parts of Speech." Today, we review the four most important ones: the "naming" words (Nouns),
the "replacing" words (Pronouns), the "action/state" words (Verbs), and the "describing" words
(Adjectives).
Content Detail
A. Nouns (The Naming Words)
 Definition: Nouns name people, places, things, animals, or ideas.
 Classification:
1. Proper Nouns: Specific names (capitalized). Examples: Mary, Lagos, Monday,
Google.
2. Common Nouns: General names. Examples: girl, city, day, company.
3. Abstract Nouns: Ideas, feelings, concepts (cannot be touched). Examples:
happiness, courage, time, danger.
5
4. Concrete Nouns: Things that can be perceived by the senses. Examples: table,
smoke, music, phone.
5. Countable Nouns: Can be counted. Examples: three books, many chairs.
6. Uncountable (Mass) Nouns: Cannot be counted (usually). Examples: water,
advice, furniture, rice.
7. Collective Nouns: Names for a group. Examples: team, family, crowd,
committee, flock.
 Functions: Nouns can be the Subject (who/what does the verb), Object (who/what
receives the action), or Complement (renames the subject).
B. Pronouns (The Replacing Words)
 Definition: Pronouns replace nouns to avoid repetition.
 Classification:
1. Personal Pronouns: Represent people/things.
 Subject: I, you, he, she, it, we, they. (They went home.)
 Object: me, you, him, her, it, us, them. (She saw them.)
2. Possessive Pronouns: Show ownership.
 Determiner (Adjective): my, your, his, her, its, our, their. (That is my
book.)
 Pronoun: mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs. (That book is mine.)
3. Reflexive Pronouns: Reflect back to the subject. Examples: myself, yourself,
himself, themselves. (He hurt himself.)
4. Demonstrative Pronouns: Point to specific things. Examples: this, that, these,
those. (This is my pen.)
5. Interrogative Pronouns: Ask questions. Examples: who, whom, whose, what,
which. (Who is at the door?)
6
C. Verbs (The Action/State Words)
 Definition: Verbs express action or a state of being. No sentence is complete without
one.
 Classification:
1. Main/Lexical Verbs: Show the main action/state. Examples: run, eat, think, love.
2. Auxiliary (Helping) Verbs: Help the main verb (show tense, mood, etc.).
 Primary: be, have, do. (She is running. He has eaten.)
 Modal: can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, must. (You
must go.)
3. Transitive Verbs: Take a direct object. Example: He kicked the ball. (Kicked
what? The ball.)
4. Intransitive Verbs: Do not take a direct object. Example: The baby slept. (Slept
what? No answer.)
5. Linking (Copular) Verbs: Link the subject to a noun or adjective. Examples: is,
are, was, seem, become, feel. (She is a doctor. He seems happy.)
D. Adjectives (The Describing Words)
 Definition: Adjectives modify (describe or limit) nouns or pronouns.
 Function: They answer questions like: What kind? How many? Which one? Whose?
 Classification:
1. Descriptive (Qualitative): beautiful, tall, red, happy
2. Quantitative: some, many, few, little, all
3. Numeral: one, two, first, second
4. Demonstrative: this, that, these, those
5. Possessive: my, your, his, her, its, our, their
7
 Order of Adjectives: When using multiple adjectives, they follow a specific order
(OSASCOMP): Opinion (lovely), Size (small), Age (old), Shape (round), Colour (red),
Origin (Italian), Material (leather), Purpose (racing) ... Example: "a beautiful small new
red leather bag."
Evaluation
1. Identify the type of noun for each underlined word: "His courage impressed the team."
2. Correct this sentence: "He bought a racing beautiful big German car."
3. In the sentence, "She told me the secret herself," identify one object pronoun and one
reflexive pronoun.
4. Is the verb 'felt' in "He felt sick" Transitive, Intransitive, or Linking? Why?
5. What is the difference between a possessive adjective (e.g., 'my') and a possessive
pronoun (e.g., 'mine')?
Assignment
1. Write a sentence for each: (a) a collective noun, (b) an abstract noun.
2. Write a short paragraph (3-4 sentences) describing your classroom. Underline all the
adjectives you use.
3. For each verb, write two sentences: one where it is Transitive and one where it is
Intransitive. (Verbs: grow, run, write).
4. Fill in the blanks with the correct personal pronoun: "___ (He/Him) and ___ (I/me) went
to the store. The storekeeper gave the book to ___ (we/us)."
5. Write a sentence using at least three adjectives in the correct OSASCOMP order to
describe an object.

READING & COMPREHENSION: REVIEW OF THE SQ3R READING TECHNIQUE
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. Define SQ3R and state its purpose as an active reading strategy.
2. Describe, in detail, the actions required for each of the five steps.
3. Apply the 'Q' (Question) step by turning sample headings into effective questions.
4. Justify why SQ3R leads to better comprehension and retention than passive reading.
Introduction
Most students read by passing their eyes over the words from beginning to end. This is passive
reading, and it's why we forget so much! SQ3R is an active reading strategy. It's a 5-step
"treasure map" for your textbooks that forces your brain to engage with the material, making you
a hunter for information, not just a passive observer.
The 5 Steps in Detail
1. S = SURVEY (To get the big picture)
 Action: Before you read, skim the chapter for 1-2 minutes. Look at:
o Title, Headings, and Subheadings: What is the chapter about?
o Introduction & Conclusion/Summary: These give you the main ideas.
o Pictures, Charts, or Graphs: Visuals often summarize key points.
o Bold or Italicized Words: These are key vocabulary.
o End-of-Chapter Questions: These tell you what the author thinks is important.
 Purpose: To create a mental "skeleton" or "map" of the chapter before you fill it in with
details.
2. Q = QUESTION (To create a purpose for reading)
9
 Action: Turn every heading and subheading into a question. Use the "5 Ws and H": Who,
What, When, Where, Why, How.
o Heading: "The Causes of the Nigerian Civil War"
o Your Questions: "What were the main causes of the war?" "Who were the key
figures?" "When did it start?"
 Purpose: This gives your brain a specific mission. You are now actively searching for
answers, not just reading words.
3. R¹ = READ (To find the answers)
 Action: Read the section under each heading, actively looking for the answers to the
questions you just formed.
o Read with a pencil or highlighter.
o Mark or underline the answers when you find them.
o Make brief notes in the margin.
o Pay attention to the bold words and visuals you surveyed.
 Purpose: To actively engage with the text and extract the most important information.
4. R² = RECITE (To build memory)
 Action: After finishing a section, look away from the book and try to answer your
question(s) aloud or by writing a summary sentence in your own words.
 Purpose: This is the most crucial step for memory. It moves information from your
short-term memory (which forgets in seconds) to your long-term memory. If you can't
say it in your own words, you don't understand it yet.
5. R³ = REVIEW (To lock in the learning)
 Action:
o Immediate Review: When you finish the entire chapter, look over your notes and
the headings. See if you can still answer your questions.
10
o Later Review: Spend 5-10 minutes reviewing your notes 24 hours later, and
again a week later.
 Purpose: This overcomes forgetting and builds lasting, permanent knowledge.
Evaluation
1. What is the difference between "active reading" (like SQ3R) and "passive reading"?
2. A textbook heading says: "The Three Types of Rocks." Write two good "Q" (Question)
step questions for this heading.
3. Why is the 'Recite' step considered the most important step for memory?
4. List three specific things you should look for during the 'S' (Survey) step.
5. How does turning a heading into a question change the way you read the section that
follows?
Assignment
1. In your own words, write a one-sentence description for each of the 5 steps: Survey,
Question, Read, Recite, Review.
2. Take a chapter from any of your other subjects (e.g., Basic Science). Write down its main
title and at least three of its headings.
3. For each of the three headings you listed in question 2, write one "Q" (Question) you
would ask.
4. Imagine you just Read a section on "Photosynthesis." What would you do for the 'Recite'
step?
5. Explain why you must do the 'Survey' step first, before you Read.

COMPOSITION: WRITING AN OUTLINE FOR A NARRATIVE ESSAY – HOW I
SPENT MY LAST HOLIDAY
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. Define a narrative essay and its purpose.
2. Identify the five essential "ingredients" of a good narrative (Plot, Setting, Character,
Theme, Point of View).
3. Understand the function of each part of an outline (Introduction, Body, Conclusion).
4. Construct a detailed, logical outline for the given topic, including a hook, thesis, and
sensory details.
Introduction
A Narrative Essay is a story, but it's more than just a story. It's a story (usually personal) told
with a specific point or purpose (a lesson or main idea). An outline is the skeleton or blueprint
for your essay. A house built without a plan will collapse, and an essay written without an
outline will be disorganized, repetitive, and confusing.
The Key Ingredients of a Narrative
1. Plot: The sequence of events (what happens first, next, last). It needs a clear beginning,
middle (with a climax or main event), and end.
2. Setting: When and where the story takes place.
3. Characters: The people in the story (mainly, "I").
4. Theme: The "so what?" – the main lesson or feeling the reader should get (e.g., "the
importance of family," "a new experience can be scary but rewarding").
5. Point of View: Usually told in the 1st Person ("I", "me", "my").
Content Detail: How to Structure the Outline
We will use the topic: "How I Spent My Last Holiday"
12
I. INTRODUCTION (The "Grabber")
 A. The Hook: A catchy first sentence to grab the reader's attention.
o Types of Hooks: A line of dialogue ("Are we there yet?"), a vivid description
("The smell of my grandmother's cooking always meant the holiday had truly
begun."), or a surprising statement ("My last holiday was a complete disaster.").
 B. Background/Context: Give brief details. When was the holiday? Where did you go?
Who were you with?
 C. Thesis Statement: The main point of your story. It states the topic and hints at the
overall feeling or lesson.
o Example Thesis: "My last holiday, spent in my village with my cousins, started
out boring but turned into an unforgettable adventure that taught me the
importance of family."
II. BODY (The "Story")
This is where the plot unfolds in chronological order (time order). Each paragraph should cover
one main idea.
 A. Body Paragraph 1: The Beginning / The Setup
o Topic Sentence: Introduce the start of the holiday.
o Details: The journey, arriving at the destination, first impressions.
 B. Body Paragraph 2: The Main Event / Climax
o Topic Sentence: Introduce the most important or exciting part of the story.
o Sensory Details (Show, Don't Tell):
 Sight: What did you see? (e.g., "the bright green mangoes")
 Sound: What did you hear? (e.g., "the sound of the river")
 Smell: What did you smell? (e.g., "the smoky scent of the firewood")
o Actions & Dialogue: What happened? What did people say?
13
 C. Body Paragraph 3: The Winding Down / Resolution
o Topic Sentence: Describe what happened after the main event.
o Details: The last few days, feelings of happiness or sadness, packing up, the
journey home.
III. CONCLUSION (The "Reflection")
 A. Restate Thesis: Reword your thesis statement (from the introduction).
 B. Reflection: This is the most important part.
o Reflect on the meaning of the story.
o Why was this holiday memorable? What did you learn? How did it change you?
 C. Concluding Sentence: A final thought that wraps up the essay.
o Example: "I returned home not just with souvenirs, but with a new appreciation
for my family that I would never forget."
Evaluation
1. What is the difference between a simple story and a narrative essay?
2. What are the five "key ingredients" of a narrative?
3. What is a 'Hook'? Write one example of a hook for a story about a rainy day.
4. Explain "Show, Don't Tell." Give an example for "I was sad." (e.g., "Tears welled up in
my eyes.")
5. What is the main purpose of the Conclusion in a narrative essay (beyond just ending it)?
Assignment
1. In your own words, define "Thesis Statement" as it relates to a narrative essay.
2. Write a strong thesis statement for the topic: "My First Day in JSS2."
14
3. Create a detailed, 5-point outline (I-V, using A, B, C sub-points) for the topic: "A Day I
Will Never Forget."
4. List three sensory details (sight, sound, smell, etc.) you could use to describe a busy
market.
5. Why is it important to write your body paragraphs in chronological order?

LITERATURE IN ENGLISH: REVIEW OF FEATURES OF PROSE (SETTING,
THEME, CHARACTERIZATION)
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. Define prose and differentiate it from poetry.
2. Define and explain the functions of Setting, Theme, Characterization, and Plot.
3. Differentiate between Direct and Indirect characterization, providing examples.
4. Identify different types of characters (e.g., Protagonist, Antagonist, Flat, Round).
5. Analyze a simple story to identify these features.
Introduction
Prose is the "ordinary" language we use in speaking and writing, structured in sentences and
paragraphs (e.g., novels, short stories, textbooks, newspapers). It is different from Poetry, which
uses metric rhythm and verse. When we read prose fiction (novels, short stories), we use "tools"
to understand what the author is doing. The most important tools are Setting, Character, Plot, and
Theme.
1. Setting
 Definition: The time (e.g., 1960, modern-day, Tuesday) and place (e.g., Lagos, a school,
a forest) where the story occurs.
 Functions of Setting:
1. Creates Mood/Atmosphere: A dark, stormy night (scary) vs. a bright, sunny
beach (happy).
2. Provides Context: A story set in 1850 will have different technology and social
rules than a story set today.
3. Reveals Character: Is the character's room messy or neat?
16
4. Can be an Antagonist (Conflict): A character trying to survive in the desert or
on a deserted island.
2. Characterization
 Definition: The art of creating and describing characters in a story.
 Methods of Characterization:
1. Direct Characterization: The author tells you the character's personality.
 Example: "Dele was a lazy and unkind boy."
2. Indirect Characterization: The author shows you the personality through the
character's:
 Speech (What they say)
 Thoughts (What they think)
 Effect on others (How others react to them)
 Actions (What they do)
 Looks (How they dress/appear)
 (Acronym: STEAL)
 Types of Characters:
1. Protagonist: The main character (the "hero").
2. Antagonist: The force or character in conflict with the protagonist (the "villain").
3. Round Character: Complex, multi-sided, realistic (like a real person).
4. Flat Character: Simple, one-dimensional (e.g., a "mean teacher" with no other
personality).
5. Dynamic Character: A character who changes or grows during the story.
6. Static Character: A character who stays the same.
17
3. Plot
 Definition: The sequence of events in a story; what happens.
 The Plot Diagram (Freytag's Pyramid):
1. Exposition: Introduction of characters and setting.
2. Rising Action: The build-up of conflict and suspense.
3. Climax: The peak of the action; the turning point of the story.
4. Falling Action: The events immediately after the climax.
5. Resolution (Dénouement): The end, where the conflict is resolved and loose
ends are tied up.
4. Theme
 Definition: The central idea, message, or moral of the story. It's a universal truth about
life.
 Theme vs. Topic:
o Topic: A single word (e.g., "Love," "War," "Friendship").
o Theme: A statement about that topic (e.g., "Love can conquer all," "War is
destructive," "True friendship requires sacrifice").
 Examples of Common Themes: Good vs. Evil, Courage, Betrayal, Social Justice, The
Importance of Family.
Evaluation
1. What is the main difference between Prose and Poetry?
2. Explain the 'STEAL' acronym for Indirect Characterization.
3. What is the 'Climax' of a plot?
4. Is the Antagonist always a person? Explain.
18
5. What is the difference between a story's Topic (e.g., "Money") and its Theme?
Assignment
1. Define 'Setting' and list its three main functions in a story.
2. Think of a character from any book or movie (e.g., Cinderella, a superhero). Is this
character Round or Flat? Dynamic or Static? Explain why.
3. Write a short example (2-3 sentences) of Direct Characterization describing a student.
4. Now, write a short example (2-3 sentences) of Indirect Characterization to show the
same thing (e.g., if he is lazy, show him arriving late, uniform rumpled, etc.).
5. What do you think is a common theme in stories about school?

SPEECH WORK: REVISION OF CONSONANT SOUNDS
Objective
By the end of this detailed lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define a consonant sound and differentiate it from a vowel sound based on airflow.
2. Identify and pronounce several consonant sounds based on their place and manner of
articulation.
3. Define a consonant cluster and differentiate it from a digraph (e.g., 'sh', 'th').
4. Accurately identify and pronounce initial (2- and 3-member) and final consonant clusters
in given words.
Introduction
In Week 1, we learned that vowel sounds are produced with a free, unobstructed flow of air.
Consonant sounds are the opposite. They are produced by partially or completely obstructing
the flow of air from the lungs through the vocal tract.
A Consonant Cluster (also called a consonant blend) is a group of two or more consonants that
appear together in a word, where each consonant sound is pronounced. This is different from a
digraph (like /ʃ/ in 'ship' or /θ/ in 'thin'), where two letters team up to make one new sound.
 Cluster (Blend): In "brick" (/brɪk/), you hear both the /b/ and the /r/.
 Digraph: In "thick" (/θɪk/), the 't' and 'h' combine to make one sound, /θ/.
A. Revision of Consonant Sounds (Brief)
Consonants are classified by:
1. Place of Articulation: Where the obstruction happens (e.g., Bilabial - both lips /p/, /b/,
/m/; Alveolar - tongue-tip on tooth ridge /t/, /d/, /n/, /s/, /z/, /l/).
20
2. Manner of Articulation: How the air is obstructed (e.g., Plosives/Stops - air is stopped
completely /p/, /t/, /k/; Fricatives - air is forced through a narrow gap /f/, /v/, /s/, /z/;
Nasals - air escapes through the nose /m/, /n/, /ŋ/).
3. Voicing: Whether the vocal cords vibrate (Voiced - /b/, /d/, /g/, /v/) or not (Voiceless - /p/,
/t/, /k/, /f/).
B. Consonant Clusters: Initial Position
These appear at the beginning of a word.
1. Two-Member Initial Clusters (s-clusters):
 /sp/: spot, speak, spin
 /st/: stop, stand, stay
 /sk/: skin, sky, skip
 /sm/: smile, smoke, small
 /sn/: snake, snow, snail
 /sl/: slip, sleep, slow
2. Two-Member Initial Clusters (l-clusters):
 /pl/: play, plate, please
 /bl/: blue, blow, black
 /kl/: clean, class, climb
 /gl/: glad, glass, glow
 /fl/: fly, flat, floor
3. Two-Member Initial Clusters (r-clusters):
 /pr/: pray, price, proud
 /br/: bring, bread, brown
21
 /tr/: tree, try, train
 /dr/: dry, dream, drink
 /kr/: cry, crab, crown
 /gr/: grow, green, great
 /fr/: from, free, friend
4. Three-Member Initial Clusters (All start with /s/):
 /spl/: splash, split, splendid
 /spr/: spring, spray, spread
 /str/: street, strong, straight
 /skr/: scream, scrub, script
 /skw/ (/skw/): square, squeeze, squid
C. Consonant Clusters: Final Position
These appear at the end of a word.
 /st/: last, fast, test
 /sk/: mask, task, desk
 /ld/: old, cold, field
 /nd/: hand, land, send
 /nt/: went, plant, tent
 /mp/: jump, lamp, camp
 /lps/: helps
 /ksts/: texts (/tɛksts/)
Evaluation

GRAMMAR: TRANSITIVE AND INTRANSITIVE VERBS
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. Define and differentiate between Transitive and Intransitive verbs.
2. Accurately identify the Direct Object (DO) of a Transitive verb.
3. Define and identify Ditransitive verbs, distinguishing between the Direct Object (DO)
and the Indirect Object (IO).
4. Identify and use ambitransitive verbs (verbs that can be both) correctly in sentences.
Introduction
In Week 1, we learned that a verb expresses action or a state of being. Now, we will classify
verbs based on whether they need an "object" to complete their meaning. An object is a noun or
pronoun that receives the action of the verb.
A. Transitive Verbs (V + DO)
 Definition: A transitive verb is an action verb that must have a Direct Object (DO).
 Direct Object (DO): The noun or pronoun that receives the action. It answers the
question What? or Whom? after the verb.
 Formula: Subject + Transitive Verb + Direct Object (S + V + DO)
 Examples:
1. Tolu kicked the ball. (Kicked what? ... the ball. "kicked" is transitive; "ball" is
DO.)
2. The teacher praised him. (Praised whom? ... him. "praised" is transitive; "him" is
DO.)
3. Ayo bought a new bag. (Bought what? ... a new bag. "bought" is transitive; "bag"
is DO.)
24
 A sentence with only a transitive verb and a subject is incomplete: Tolu kicked. (Kicked
what? It feels unfinished.)
B. Intransitive Verbs (V)
 Definition: An intransitive verb is an action verb (or state of being verb) that does not
take an object. It is complete on its own.
 Formula: Subject + Intransitive Verb (S + V)
 Examples:
1. The baby cried. (Cried what? No answer. The verb is complete.)
2. He arrived early. ("Early" is an adverb telling when, not an object receiving an
action.)
3. Birds fly.
 These verbs cannot have a direct object. You cannot "cried something" or "arrived
something."
C. Ambitransitive Verbs (Verbs that can be both)
 Definition: Many verbs can be either transitive or intransitive, depending on the context.
 Examples:
1. eat:
 Transitive: We ate jollof rice. (Ate what? ... jollof rice.)
 Intransitive: We ate at 7 pm. (Ate what? No answer. "at 7 pm" is when.)
2. read:
 Transitive: She is reading a book. (Reading what? ... a book.)
 Intransitive: She is reading. (The action is complete on its own.)
3. grow:
 Transitive: The farmer grows maize. (Grows what? ... maize.)
25
 Intransitive: The boy grew quickly. (Grew what? No answer.)
D. Ditransitive Verbs (V + IO + DO)
 Definition: A special type of transitive verb that takes two objects: an Indirect Object
(IO) and a Direct Object (DO).
 Indirect Object (IO): The person/thing that receives the Direct Object. It answers the
question To whom? or For whom?. The IO always comes before the DO.
 Formula: Subject + Ditransitive Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object (S + V + IO +
DO)
 Examples:
1. My father gave me the money.
 Gave what? ... the money (DO)
 Gave the money to whom? ... me (IO)
2. She bought her sister a gift.
 Bought what? ... a gift (DO)
 Bought the gift for whom? ... her sister (IO)
3. The teacher told the class a story.
 Told what? ... a story (DO)
 Told the story to whom? ... the class (IO)
Evaluation
1. What is the main difference between a transitive and an intransitive verb?
2. In the sentence, "The student answered the question," identify the verb and the direct
object.
3. In the sentence, "My mother baked me a cake," identify the Indirect Object and the
Direct Object.
26
4. The verb 'run' can be ambitransitive. Write one sentence where 'run' is transitive and one
where it is intransitive.
5. Is the verb 'sleep' transitive or intransitive? Why?
Assignment
1. Write two original sentences using transitive verbs. Underline the verb and circle the
direct object.
2. Write two original sentences using intransitive verbs.
3. Identify the verb, IO, and DO in each sentence: a. The postman handed Funke a letter. b.
My uncle promised me a new phone.
4. For each verb, write two sentences: one where it is Transitive and one where it is
Intransitive. (Verbs: play, write, sing).
5. What is a ditransitive verb? Give an original example.

READING & COMPREHENSION: READING TO UNDERSTAND THE WRITER’S
PURPOSE
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. Define "author's purpose."
2. Identify and explain the four main purposes for writing: to Persuade, Inform, Entertain,
and Describe (PIE-D).
3. Analyze a short text to determine its primary purpose.
4. Identify clues in a text (like tone, diction, and text features) that reveal the author's
purpose.
Introduction
Every time an author writes something—be it a textbook, a novel, a newspaper article, or an
advert—they have a reason or a purpose for writing. As active readers, our job is to figure out
why they wrote it. Understanding the purpose helps us understand the main idea and evaluate the
text properly. Is the author trying to teach us, change our minds, or just make us laugh?
A. The Main Purposes for Writing (Acronym: PIE-D)
1. P = to PERSUADE
o Goal: To make the reader agree with an opinion, change their mind, or do
something.
o Clues: Uses opinions, emotional language, strong adjectives, rhetorical questions,
and a call to action. It presents one side as the best.
o Examples: Advertisements, political speeches, opinion articles, debate
arguments, letters of complaint.
28
o Passage Example: "It is vital that all students stop buying single-use plastic
bottles. They are destroying our environment and polluting our oceans. We must
act now! Bring a reusable bottle to school tomorrow."
2. I = to INFORM
o Goal: To teach the reader, explain a topic, or give facts and information.
o Clues: Uses facts, statistics, dates, definitions, and a formal, neutral tone. It is
objective.
o Examples: Textbooks, news reports (not opinion articles), encyclopedias, user
manuals.
o Passage Example: "The Nile River is the longest river in the world, stretching
over 6,650 kilometers. It flows north through eleven African countries before
emptying into the Mediterranean Sea."
3. E = to ENTERTAIN
o Goal: To provide enjoyment for the reader.
o Clues: Uses a story (plot, characters, setting), suspense, humor, or exciting action.
o Examples: Novels, short stories, poems, song lyrics, comics, jokes.
o Passage Example: "The old door creaked open. Tunde held his breath, peering
into the darkness. A pair of red eyes suddenly glowed from the far corner of the
room. He screamed."
4. D = to DESCRIBE
o Goal: To paint a picture in the reader's mind using words.
o Clues: Uses vivid sensory details (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch) and strong
adjectives and adverbs. (This is closely related to Entertaining and will be our
focus in Composition).
o Examples: Descriptive essays, detailed passages in novels, travelogues.
29
o Passage Example: "The market was a riot of noise and colour. The air was thick
with the smell of overripe mangoes, strong spices, and diesel fumes from the
buses roaring past."
B. How to Find the Purpose
Ask these questions as you read:
1. What is the Tone? Is the author's attitude serious, humorous, angry, objective? (e.g., An
angry tone often means persuade).
2. What is the Diction? What words does the author choose? Are they emotional words
(e.g., terrible, wonderful) or factual words (e.g., percentage, dates)?
3. What are the Text Features? Does the text contain facts and charts (Inform)? Opinions
(Persuade)? Characters and dialogue (Entertain)?
Evaluation
1. What does the acronym "PIE-D" stand for in relation to author's purpose?
2. If an article contains many facts, statistics, and a neutral tone, what is its likely purpose?
3. What is the main goal of a writer whose purpose is to persuade?
4. How does the tone of a text (e.g., humorous, serious) help you identify the author's
purpose?
5. Read this: "The new 'Speedy' phone is the best you can buy. It has a sleek design and a
brilliant camera. Don't waste your money on inferior brands. Buy one today!" What is the
author's purpose?
Assignment
30
1. In your own words, explain the difference between writing to Inform and writing to
Persuade.
2. Find an advertisement in a magazine or newspaper (or online). What is its main purpose,
and how do you know?
3. Find a short news report (not an opinion piece). What is its purpose, and what clues (e.g.,
word choice) tell you this?
4. What is the primary purpose of your English Literature novel?
5. Write a short, 3-sentence paragraph with the primary purpose of entertaining the reader.

COMPOSITION: DESCRIPTIVE ESSAY – WRITING AN OUTLINE
"A Place of Interest I Visited"
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. Define a descriptive essay and differentiate it from a narrative essay.
2. Identify the importance of "Show, Don't Tell" and the use of sensory details.
3. Brainstorm sensory details (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch) for a chosen topic.
4. Construct a logical outline for a descriptive essay.
Introduction
In Week 1, we planned a narrative essay, which tells a story about what happened. This week,
we plan a descriptive essay, which paints a picture with words. The goal is not to tell a story,
but to make the reader feel like they are in the place.
Your most important tool is Sensory Language. You must appeal to the five senses. The secret
is "Show, Don't Tell."
 Telling: "The food was delicious." (Boring)
 Showing: "The hot, spicy aroma of the goat meat pepper soup made my mouth water,
and the first spoonful exploded with flavour." (Vivid)
A. Brainstorming Sensory Details for "A Place of Interest I Visited"
 (Example Place: A Busy Market, e.g., Onitsha Market or Balogun Market)
 SIGHT: Piles of red tomatoes, bright ankara fabrics, mountains of shoes, huge crowds,
rusty roofs, fast-moving wheelbarrows.
 SOUND: Shouting of sellers ("Buy your own!"), haggling voices, rumble of buses, loud
music from speakers, generators humming.
32
 SMELL: Dust, sweat, overripe fruit (plantains, mangoes), spices, dried fish, exhaust
fumes.
 TOUCH: Humid air, sweat on skin, jostling of the crowd, rough texture of a basket.
 TASTE: Sweet slice of pineapple, sachet water, spicy suya bought from a vendor.
B. How to Structure the Outline
Your outline should be organized by senses or by areas of the place (spatial order).
I. INTRODUCTION
 A. The Hook: A strong sensory detail to pull the reader in.
o Example: "The moment you step into Balogun Market, the roar of a thousand
voices and the thick smell of spice and sweat hit you like a wave."
 B. Background: Name the place and state when/why you visited.
 C. Thesis Statement: State the overall feeling or dominant impression of the place.
o Example: "My visit to the market was an overwhelming but thrilling explosion of
life, sound, and colour."
II. BODY (The "Picture")
 A. Body Paragraph 1: The "Big Picture" (Sight & Sound)
o Topic Sentence: Introduce the dominant visual and auditory landscape.
o Details: Describe the overall scene. The crowds (sight), the buildings (sight), the
constant noise (sound). Use strong verbs and adjectives.
 B. Body Paragraph 2: "Zooming In" (Smell & Specific Sights)
o Topic Sentence: Focus on a specific section of the place (e.g., the food section).
o Details: Describe the specific smells (spices, fish). Describe the specific sights
(piles of peppers, bags of rice).
33
 C. Body Paragraph 3: The "Feeling" (Touch, Taste & Atmosphere)
o Topic Sentence: Describe the personal, physical experience.
o Details: Describe the heat, the humidity, the feeling of the crowd (touch).
Describe something you ate (taste). Describe the energy of the place.
III. CONCLUSION (The "Reflection")
 A. Restate Thesis: Reword your thesis (dominant impression).
 B. Reflection: Reflect on the feeling the place left you with.
o Example: "Leaving the market felt like stepping out of a fast-moving river into
quiet, still water."
 C. Concluding Sentence: A final image or thought that summarizes the experience.
o Example: "Balogun Market is not just a place to buy things; it is the loud,
colourful, beating heart of Lagos."
Evaluation
1. What is the main difference between a descriptive essay and a narrative essay?
2. What does the phrase "Show, Don't Tell" mean?
3. List the five senses you must appeal to in a descriptive essay.
4. Write a strong sensory hook for an essay about "A Beautiful Beach."
5. What is the "thesis statement" of a descriptive essay supposed to do?
Assignment
1. Choose "A Place of Interest I Visited" (e.g., your village, a stadium, a museum, a busy
park).
2. Brainstorm a list of at least 15 sensory details for your chosen place (3 for each sense).
34
3. Write a clear thesis statement that captures the dominant impression of your place.
4. Using your brainstorm, create a detailed 5-point outline (I-V, with A, B, C sub-points) for
your descriptive essay.
5. Rewrite this "Telling" sentence into a "Showing" sentence: "The man was angry."

Applying Literary Elements to a Prose Text

Introduction
Last week, we learned the definitions of Plot, Setting, Character, and Theme. This week, our goal

SPEECH WORK: LISTENING AND SPEAKING – THE DIPHTHONGS /ɪƏ/ AND /EƏ/
The Diphthongs /ɪə/ (as in 'near') and /eə/ (as in 'square')
Objective
By the end of this detailed lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define a diphthong and differentiate it from a monophthong (pure vowel).
2. Accurately identify and produce the diphthong /ɪə/.
3. Accurately identify and produce the diphthong /eə/.
4. Distinguish between the two sounds in spoken and written minimal pairs (e.g., 'hear' vs.
'hair').
Introduction
In Week 1, we studied monophthongs (pure vowels), which are single, static vowel sounds.
Today, we are studying diphthongs. A diphthong is a "gliding vowel"—a single sound made by
gliding from one vowel position to another within the same syllable.
We will focus on two diphthongs that are often confused: /ɪə/ (found in "clear") and /eə/ (found
in "bear").
A. The Diphthong /ɪə/ (as in 'near')
 Production: This sound is a glide that starts from the short vowel /ɪ/ (as in 'pit') and
moves towards the schwa sound /ə/ (as in 'about').
 Common Spelling Patterns:
o 'ear': hear, clear, fear, year, dear
o 'eer': beer, deer, cheer, steer
o 'ere': here, mere, sphere
40
o 'ier': fierce, pierce, tier
B. The Diphthong /eə/ (as in 'square')
 Production: This sound is a glide that starts from the short vowel /e/ (as in 'bed') and
moves towards the schwa sound /ə/.
 Common Spelling Patterns:
o 'ear': bear, pear, wear, tear (to rip)
o 'are': care, dare, square, rare
o 'air': hair, fair, chair, pair
o 'ere': there, where
o 'eir': their
C. Practical Drill: Minimal Pairs
This is the best way to hear and practice the difference. Say the following pairs aloud,
exaggerating the starting vowel.

fear fair

GRAMMAR: MORE ON ACTIVE AND PASSIVE VOICE
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. Define Active and Passive voice and identify them in sentences.
2. Identify the 'agent' in a passive sentence.
3. Accurately convert sentences from Active to Passive voice for various tenses.
4. Accurately convert sentences from Passive to Active voice.
5. Explain the main reasons why a writer would choose to use the passive voice.
Introduction
The "voice" of a verb tells us whether the subject of the sentence is performing the action or
receiving the action.
 Active Voice: The subject does the action. (e.g., "The cat chased the mouse.")
 Passive Voice: The subject receives the action. (e.g., "The mouse was chased by the
cat.")
A. Identifying Active vs. Passive
 Active: Subject + Verb + Object
o Example: "The principal punished the students." (Subject 'principal' did the
punishing).
 Passive: Subject (Receiver) + Verb to be + Past Participle + (by + Agent)
o Example: "The students were punished by the principal." (Subject 'students'
received the punishment).
43
o The "doer" of the action in a passive sentence is called the agent, introduced with
"by".
B. How to Convert from Active to Passive
1. The Object of the active sentence becomes the new Subject.
2. The Verb is changed to: [Verb 'to be' in the original tense] + [Past Participle].
3. The Subject of the active sentence becomes the Agent (using "by") or is left out.
C. Tense Conversion Table (Active → Passive)
Tense Active Example Passive Conversion
Simple Present He writes a letter. A letter is written by him.
Simple Past He wrote a letter. A letter was written by him.
Present Cont. He is writing a letter. A letter is being written by him.
Past Cont. He was writing a letter. A letter was being written by him.
Present Perfect He has written a letter. A letter has been written by him.
Past Perfect He had written a letter. A letter had been written by him.
Future (will) He will write a letter. A letter will be written by him.
Modal (can) He can write a letter. A letter can be written by him.
D. When and Why to Use the Passive Voice
Using the active voice is usually stronger and more direct, but the passive voice is very useful in
specific situations:
1. When the agent (doer) is unknown:
o "My bicycle was stolen last night." (We don't know who stole it).
2. When the agent is unimportant or obvious:
o "The president was re-elected." (We know he was re-elected by the voters).
44
o "Rice is grown in Nigeria." (It's grown by farmers).
3. To emphasize the receiver of the action (the subject):
o "The student was praised by the principal." (We want to focus on the student, not
the principal).
4. To sound objective and formal (common in science and news):
o "The solution was heated to 50°C." (Instead of "The scientist heated the
solution...").
Evaluation
1. Is the following sentence Active or Passive? "The food is being cooked by my mother."
2. Identify the 'agent' in this sentence: "The goal was scored by the striker."
3. Convert this sentence to Passive Voice: "The teacher teaches the students."
4. Convert this sentence to Active Voice: "The house will be built by the engineers."
5. What is one good reason to use the passive voice? (e.g., "When the doer is...")
Assignment

3. Rewrite this sentence using the passive voice to sound more scientific: "I added 10ml of

4. Why is the passive voice used in this sentence? "My phone was stolen in the market."

READING & COMPREHENSION: WRITER’S INTENTION & VOCABULARY
A: Identifying Words that Show the Writer’s Intention (Bias) B: Vocabulary Development –
Word Families (In the Hospital)
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. Differentiate between a writer's purpose (PIE-D) and their intention (attitude/bias).
2. Define denotation and connotation.
3. Analyze a text for loaded words (diction) and tone to understand the writer's hidden bias.
4. Identify and correctly use vocabulary related to a hospital setting.
Part A: Identifying Writer’s Intention (Bias)
Introduction
In Week 2, we learned about the author's Purpose (To Inform, Persuade, Entertain, Describe).
Now, we look deeper at Intention (also called Bias or Tone). The intention is the feeling or
attitude the writer has towards their subject. A writer can have the purpose to inform, but their
intention might be to make the subject seem good or bad.
1. Denotation vs. Connotation
 Denotation: The literal, dictionary definition of a word.
o Example: pig = "a farm animal with a snout."
 Connotation: The emotional feeling or idea associated with a word.
o Example: pig = "dirty, greedy, or unpleasant."
Writers show their intention by choosing words with strong connotations.
2. Diction (Word Choice) and Tone Let's analyze the intention on the topic of corruption:
47
 Passage 1 (Neutral/Objective Tone): "An investigation found that the minister
transferred ₦50 million from a public account to a private one."
o Intention: To inform. The word "transferred" is neutral (denotation).
 Passage 2 (Critical/Negative Tone): "The corrupt minister brazenly stole ₦50 million
of the people's money and siphoned it into a secret account."
o Intention: To persuade the reader that the minister is a criminal.
o Loaded Words: "corrupt," "brazenly," "stole," "siphoned" (all have strong
negative connotations).
 Passage 3 (Supportive/Evasive Tone): "The minister reallocated the ₦50 million to a
contingency fund as an executive decision."
o Intention: To persuade the reader that the action was normal or justified.
o Loaded Words: "reallocated," "contingency fund," "executive decision" (all sound
professional and positive).
Part B: Vocabulary Development – Word Families (In the Hospital)
 Core Word: Hospital, Clinic
 People (Nouns):
o Doctor / Physician: A general medical practitioner.
o Surgeon: A doctor who performs operations.
o Nurse: A person who cares for the sick.
o Patient: A person receiving medical treatment.
o Pharmacist: A person who dispenses medicine.
 Places (Nouns):
o Ward: A large room in a hospital for patients.
o (Operating) Theatre: The room where surgeons perform operations.
48
o Laboratory: The place where blood/tests are analyzed.
o Pharmacy / Dispensary: The place where medicine is given out.
o Emergency Room (ER) / Casualty: The department for urgent, life-threatening
cases.
 Actions (Verbs):
o Diagnose: To identify an illness.
o Treat: To give medical care.
o Operate (on): To perform surgery.
o Prescribe: To write an order for medicine.
o Admit: To check a patient into the hospital.
o Discharge: To release a patient from the hospital.
 Concepts & Things (Nouns):
o Diagnosis: The identification of an illness.

COMPOSITION: ELEMENTS OF COMPOSITION – THE WRITING PROCESS
The Writing Process (Pre-writing, Writing, and Post-writing)
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. List and explain the three main stages of the writing process.
2. Describe at least two pre-writing techniques.
3. Explain the function of the Body and Conclusion during the 'Writing' (Drafting) stage.
4. Differentiate between 'Revising' and 'Editing' in the 'Post-writing' stage.
Introduction
Good essays do not appear by magic. They are built. Professional writers use a system called
The Writing Process to turn their ideas into a clear, organized, and polished final piece. We will
learn this 3-stage process:
1. Pre-writing (Planning)
2. Writing (Drafting)
3. Post-writing (Revising, Editing, and Proofreading)
1. Stage 1: Pre-writing (The Plan)
This is the 'thinking' stage. You do this before you write your first sentence.
 A. Brainstorming: Generating all possible ideas on your topic.
o Techniques:
 Listing: Simply writing down all ideas, words, and phrases that come to
mind.
51
 Mind-Mapping: Starting with the topic in a central bubble and drawing
"legs" for main ideas, with smaller "twigs" for details.
 B. Outlining: Organizing your brainstormed ideas into a logical structure. This is the
"skeleton" of your essay, which we practiced in Weeks 1 and 2. It includes:
o I. Introduction (Hook, Thesis)
o II. Body Paragraph 1 (Topic Sentence, Details)
o III. Body Paragraph 2 (Topic Sentence, Details)
o IV. Body Paragraph 3...
o V. Conclusion (Restate Thesis, Final Thought)
2. Stage 2: Writing (The First Draft)
This is the 'building' stage. Your goal is to get your ideas from the outline onto the paper in full
sentences.
 A. Focus on Flow, Not Perfection: Don't stop to check every spelling. Just write. This is
called the "first draft" or "rough draft."
 B. The Body: This is the main part of the essay. Each main idea from your outline (II,
III, IV) becomes a separate Body Paragraph.
o Each body paragraph MUST have a Topic Sentence (the main idea of that
paragraph) and Supporting Details (examples, sensory details, explanations).
 C. The Conclusion: This is the final paragraph that wraps up the essay. It MUST do two
things:
1. Restate the Thesis: Say your main point again, but in different words.
2. Give a Final Thought: End with a reflection, a lesson learned, or a strong final image.
3. Stage 3: Post-writing (The Polish)
This is the most important stage, where a "good" draft becomes an "excellent" essay. This stage
has two parts:
52
 A. Revising (Big Picture): You re-read your draft to check the ideas and organization.
o Use the acronym ARMS:
 Add: Does this paragraph need more details or examples?
 Remove: Does this sentence go off-topic?
 Move: Would this sentence be better in a different paragraph?
 Substitute: Can I use a stronger verb or a more descriptive adjective?
 B. Editing/Proofreading (Corrections): You check your revised draft for errors.
o Use the acronym CUPS:
 Capitalization: Are all proper nouns and first words capitalized?
 Usage: Is the GRAMMAR correct (e.g., subject-verb agreement)?
 Punctuation: Are there full stops, commas, and question marks in the right
places?
 Spelling: Are all words spelled correctly?
Evaluation
1. What are the three main stages of the writing process?
2. What is the purpose of the 'Pre-writing' stage?

LITERATURE IN ENGLISH: FEATURES OF FOLKTALES & PROSE ANALYSIS

Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

2. List and explain at least five common features of folktales (e.g., didactic,
anthropomorphism).
3. Define and differentiate between internal and external conflict.
4. Identify the main types of conflict (Man vs. Man, Man vs. Self, etc.) in their

Part A: Features of Folktales
Introduction
A Folktale is a story that has been passed down from one generation to the next through oral
tradition (speaking and listening), long before it was ever written down. Every culture has them.
The main purpose of folktales is usually didactic—meaning they are designed to teach a moral
lesson about life, behavior, or values.
Common Features
1. Oral Tradition: They are "stories of the people" and often begin with a formula like,
"Once upon a time..." and end with "...they lived happily ever after" or a similar phrase.
2. Didactic (Moral Lesson): This is the most important feature. They teach lessons (e.g.,
"why the tortoise has a cracked shell" teaches a lesson about greed; "the hare and the
tortoise" teaches about consistency).
55
3. Anthropomorphism: This is when animals or objects behave like humans. They talk,
wear clothes, have jobs, and show human emotions like jealousy, wisdom, or cunning.
(e.g., Anansi the Spider, Br'er Rabbit).
4. Stock Characters: The characters are often simple and flat, representing one idea (e.g.,
the "wise old woman," the "evil stepmother," the "innocent child," the "cunning
trickster").
5. Supernatural Elements: The stories often include magic, juju, witches, spirits, or
mythical creatures.
6. Inclusion of Songs/Riddles: The storyteller often pauses the story to involve the
audience with a song, chant, or riddle.
7. Simple, Repetitive Plot: The plot is usually a simple case of good vs. evil, or a weakbut-
clever character defeating a strong-but-foolish one.
Part B: Applying to Recommended Prose Text (Conflict)
Introduction
Just like folktales, our recommended novel also teaches lessons (themes). It does this by showing
how characters behave when they face problems. These problems are called Conflict.
Types of Conflict
1. Internal Conflict: A struggle inside the character's own mind.
o Man vs. Self: The character struggles with a difficult decision, fear, jealousy,
guilt, or a weakness in their own personality.
2. External Conflict: A struggle with an outside force.
o Man vs. Man: The protagonist (hero) struggles against the antagonist (villain).
o Man vs. Society: The protagonist struggles against a law, tradition, or belief of
their community (e.g., fighting against "corruption," as in our Reading lesson).
56
o Man vs. Nature: The protagonist struggles against a natural force (e.g., a storm, a
desert, an illness, a wild animal).
Your Task: As you read your novel, analyze its conflicts.
 What is the main External Conflict? (e.g., Is it Man vs. Man, or Man vs. Society?)
 Does the protagonist also face an Internal Conflict? (e.g., Are they afraid? Are they
tempted to do the wrong thing?)
 Connecting to Theme: What lesson (theme) is the author teaching you through this
conflict? (This is the didactic part of your novel).

GRAMMAR: MORE ON TENSES (PRESENT, PAST, AND FUTURE)
A Detailed Review of Simple, Continuous, and Perfect Tenses
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. Define "tense" as the form of a verb that shows time (past, present, future).
2. Identify and correctly form the Simple tenses (Present, Past, Future).
3. Identify and correctly form the Continuous tenses (Present, Past, Future).
4. Identify and correctly form the Perfect tenses (Present, Past, Future).
5. Explain the function of each tense (e.g., simple present for habits, present continuous for
current action, simple past for finished action).
Introduction
Tense is the grammatical tool we use to place an action in time. In Week 2, we saw how the verb
'to be' and 'past participles' are used to form the passive voice. Today, we will review the main
tenses in the active voice, focusing on when and why we use each one.
We will organize the tenses by their aspect (Simple, Continuous, Perfect).
A. The SIMPLE Tenses
 Function: They describe actions as complete facts, habits, or single events.
1. Simple Present:
o Form: Base verb (e.g., play) or Base verb + s (e.g., plays).
o Use: (1) For habits and routines ("I brush my teeth every day."); (2) For general
truths ("The sun rises in the east.").
2. Simple Past:
o Form: Base verb + ed (e.g., played) or Irregular form (e.g., ate, went).
62
o Use: For a completed action at a specific time in the past ("We watched the film
yesterday.").
3. Simple Future:
o Form: will + Base verb.
o Use: For an action that will happen in the future ("I will see you tomorrow.").
B. The CONTINUOUS (or PROGRESSIVE) Tenses
 Function: They describe actions that are in progress, ongoing, or unfinished at a certain
time.
 Form: [verb 'to be'] + [verb + ing (present participle)]
1. Present Continuous:
o Form: am/is/are + playing
o Use: For an action happening right now ("She is reading a book.") or around this
time ("I am studying for my exams.").
2. Past Continuous:
o Form: was/were + playing
o Use: For an ongoing action in the past that was interrupted by another action ("I
was watching TV when the light went out.").
3. Future Continuous:
o Form: will be + playing
o Use: For an ongoing action at a specific time in the future ("This time tomorrow, I
will be writing my exam.").
C. The PERFECT Tenses
 Function: They describe actions that are completed in relation to another point in time.
 Form: [verb 'to have'] + [Past Participle (e.g., played, eaten)]
63
1. Present Perfect:
o Form: has/have + played
o Use: For an action that started in the past and continues to the present ("I have
lived here for five years.") OR a past action at an unspecified time ("I have seen
that film before.").
2. Past Perfect:
o Form: had + played
o Use: For a past action that was completed before another past action ("The film
had already started when we arrived.").
3. Future Perfect:
o Form: will have + played
o Use: For an action that will be completed before a specific time in the future ("By
8 pm, I will have finished my homework.").

2. What is the main difference between the Simple Past ("I ate") and the Present Perfect ("I

4. Change this sentence to the Simple Future tense: "He reads a book."
5. Change this sentence to the Past Perfect tense: "They arrive."

Introduction
This week, we are adding a new literary tool: Point of View (POV). The POV is the "camera"
the author uses to tell the story. Who is the narrator? Whose eyes are we seeing the story
through? The POV controls how much information we get and whose feelings and thoughts we
know.
We will also continue our search for the Theme (the author's main message).
A. Understanding Point of View (POV)
1. First-Person POV:
o Pronouns: "I," "me," "my," "we," "us."
o Narrator: The narrator is a character in the story, telling it from their perspective.
o Effect: We feel very close to the narrator. We know all their private thoughts and
feelings.

o Limitation: We only know what the narrator knows. We don't know what other
characters are thinking (unless they tell the narrator). This can make the narrator
unreliable.
2. Third-Person POV:
o Pronouns: "He," "she," "it," "they," "them."
o Narrator: The narrator is an outside voice, not a character in the story.
o Types:
 Limited: The narrator sticks closely to one character (usually the
protagonist). We see what they see and know what they think, but not
others. (e.g., "Harry Potter").
 Omniscient ("All-Knowing"): The narrator can go into any character's
mind, tell us any character's thoughts, and describe events happening in
different places at the same time.
B. Analyzing Theme
 Remember from Week 3, the Theme is the moral lesson or big idea about life. It's the "so
what?"
 Common Themes: Good vs. Evil, Courage, Justice, The Effects of Corruption, The
Importance of Family, The Dangers of Greed.
 Finding Evidence: To prove a theme, you must find evidence in the text.
o Example Topic: "Courage"
o Example Theme: "Courage means doing the right thing even when you are
afraid."
o Example Evidence: Find a specific moment in the book where the protagonist was
visibly scared but still chose to do something brave (e.g., stand up to the
antagonist, tell the truth).
75

 Manner: Plosive (you build up pressure behind your tongue and then release it).
 Practice: Place your tongue tip just behind your top teeth, build up air, and release. It
should sound like /t/, /t/, /t/.

GRAMMAR: PAST PERFECT TENSE
The Past Perfect Tense
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. Define the function of the Past Perfect Tense as "the past of the past."
2. Identify the correct form of the Past Perfect Tense (had + Past Participle).
3. Accurately form sentences using the Past Perfect Tense.
4. Differentiate between the Simple Past and the Past Perfect in a timeline.
5. Use the Past Perfect with conjunctions like 'before', 'after', and 'when'.
Introduction
In Week 4, we reviewed the simple, continuous, and perfect tenses. Today, we will do a deep
dive into one of the most important (and sometimes confusing) tenses: the Past Perfect.
Its main job is to show that one action in the past was finished before another action in the past
happened. It's the "past of the past."
A. Form
The form is simple and never changes: had + [Past Participle]
 I had eaten.
 She had left.
 They had finished.
 It had been (passive voice).
80
B. Function: The "Past of the Past"
Imagine a timeline of yesterday. Two things happened:
1. I ate breakfast (at 8:00 AM).
2. My friend arrived (at 9:00 AM).
Both are in the simple past. But if I want to tell a story about 9:00 AM, I need to show that the
eating was already finished.
 "When my friend arrived (Simple Past), I had already eaten (Past Perfect)."
The Past Perfect action (had eaten) is "Past Event 1". The Simple Past action (arrived) is "Past
Event 2".
C. Common Use with Conjunctions
1. before / by the time: The Past Perfect is used for the action that was finished.
o "She had finished her homework before her mother came home."
o "By the time the police arrived, the thieves had escaped."
2. after: The Past Perfect is used for the action that happened first.
o "After she had finished her homework, she watched TV."
3. when: (Be careful! 'When' can be tricky)
o "When I got to the station, the train had left." (I missed the train. The leaving
happened first.)
o "When I got to the station, the train left." (I arrived just in time to see it leave. The
actions happened at the same time.)

LITERATURE IN ENGLISH: USE THE RECOMMENDED TEXT ON DRAMA
Introduction to Drama: Characterization and Plot
Objective
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
1. Define 'Drama' as a genre of literature meant to be performed.

5. Identify the main plot elements (conflict, rising action) in their recommended drama text.
Introduction
This week, we are shifting from Prose (novels) to Drama (plays). A play is a story written to be
performed by actors on a stage. This changes everything.
In a novel, a narrator tells you what is happening and what characters are thinking. In a play,
there is no narrator. You, the reader, must become a detective. You only know what is
happening by:
1. Dialogue: What the characters say to each other.
2. Stage Directions: Notes from the author (often in italics or brackets) that describe the
setting or the character's actions and tone. (e.g., [shouting angrily], [He looks at the floor,
ashamed]).
A. Plot in Drama
The plot structure is the same as in prose (Exposition, Rising Action, Climax, Falling Action,
Resolution), but it is organized differently.
90
 Acts: These are the major "chapters" of a play. A play might have 3 or 5 acts.
 Scenes: An act is divided into smaller "scenes." A new scene usually begins when the
time or location changes.
 Exposition: Usually Act 1. We meet the characters and learn about the setting and the
main conflict.
 Rising Action: The middle acts. The conflict gets more complicated.
B. Characterization in Drama
How do we know a character's personality? We must infer it. We use the same 'STEAL' acronym
from prose, but it works differently:
 S - Speech: What does the character say? Do they speak politely or rudely? Do they use
big words or simple ones? Do they talk a lot or stay quiet?
 T - Thoughts: We do not know their thoughts (unless they have a soliloquy—a long
speech to themselves when they are alone).
 E - Effect on Others: How do other characters react to them? Are people afraid of them?
Do they respect them?
 A - Actions: What does the character do? (These are found in the stage directions). Do
they help others? Do they steal? Do they run from a fight?
 L - Looks: The author's stage directions might describe their costume or appearance,
which gives clues.

Introduction
A conjunction is a word that joins words, phrases, or clauses. They are the "glue" of English.
Using them correctly allows you to move from writing simple sentences ("I am tired. I want to
sleep.") to more mature, complex sentences ("I want to sleep because I am tired.").
There are three main types of conjunctions:
A. Coordinating Conjunctions
These join two equal and independent clauses. The easiest way to remember them is the acronym
FANBOYS:
 For (meaning 'because')
 And (adds information)
 Nor (adds a negative)
 But (shows contrast)
 Or (shows choice)

 Yet (shows contrast, like 'but')
 So (shows result)

 Example: "I like rice, but my brother prefers beans."
 Example: "She studied hard, so she passed the test."
B. Subordinating Conjunctions
These join an independent clause (a full sentence) with a dependent clause (an incomplete
thought). They show the relationship between the two clauses (e.g., time, cause, contrast).
 Common Examples: because, since, as, although, though, while, when, after, before, if,
unless, until.
 Rule 1: Independent Clause + Dependent Clause
o Example: "He went to school although he was sick."
 Rule 2: Dependent Clause, + Independent Clause (If the sentence starts with the
conjunction, use a comma).
o Example: "Although he was sick, he went to school."

These are pairs of conjunctions that work together.
 both... and... ("She likes both rice and beans.")
 either... or... ("You can have either the tea or the coffee.")
 neither... nor... ("He is neither tall nor short.")
 not only... but also... ("He is not only intelligent but also kind.")

 whether... or... ("I don't know whether to go or stay.")

Part A: Differentiating between a Novella and a Novel
Introduction
In literature, we have different lengths of fiction. We know the 'short story'. But what is the
difference between a 'novella' and a 'novel'? The main difference is length and complexity.
1. Novel:
o Definition: A long work of fictional prose (usually 50,000 words or more).
o Plot: Very complex. It has one main plot and several subplots (smaller stories
connected to the main one).
o Characters: Has many characters. The main characters and many minor
characters are 'round' (well-developed).
o Examples: Things Fall Apart by Chinua Achebe, Harry Potter by J.K. Rowling.
2. Novella:
106
o Definition: A work of fictional prose that is shorter than a novel but longer than a
short story (usually 20,000 - 50,000 words).
o Plot: Simpler. It focuses on one main plot, one central conflict, and a single chain
of events.
o Characters: Has few characters. It usually focuses deeply on just one or two
protagonists.
o Examples: Animal Farm by George Orwell, The Old Man and the Sea by Ernest
Hemingway.
Part B: Figures of Speech
Introduction
Figures of speech are tools writers use to make their language more colourful, vivid, and
interesting. We will learn two new ones today.
1. Onomatopoeia (on-oh-mat-oh-PEE-ah)
o Definition: A word that imitates or sounds like the noise it describes.
o Function: It makes the reader hear the story.
o Examples:
 Animal sounds: moo, oink, hiss, meow, woof.
 Action sounds: crash, bang, boom, splash, vroom, thump, whisper.
o Sentence: "The buzz of the bee was annoying."
2. Personification
o Definition: Giving human qualities, actions, or feelings to a non-human thing (an
animal, an object, or an idea).
o Function: It helps the reader connect emotionally with the object being described.
o Examples:
107
 "The wind whispered through the leaves." (Wind cannot whisper; humans
do).
 "My alarm clock yelled at me." (Clocks can't yell).
 "The sun smiled down on the children." (Suns can't smile).
 "Fear grabbed his heart." (Fear cannot grab).

The Consonant Sound /v/ (Voiced Labio-dental Fricative)
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Correctly identify the consonant sound /v/ in words.
2. Describe the production of the /v/ sound (voiced, labio-dental, fricative).
3. Differentiate the /v/ sound from the voiceless /f/ sound and the voiced bilabial /b/ sound.
4. Correctly pronounce words and sentences containing the /v/ sound.
The sound /v/ is known as a voiced labio-dental fricative. Let's break down what this means:
 Voiced: You use your vocal cords to make the sound. If you place your fingers on your
throat, you will feel a vibration or "buzzing."
 Labio-dental: This describes the parts of the mouth used. "Labio" means 'lip' and
"dental" means 'teeth.' To make this sound, your lower lip lightly touches your upper
teeth.
 Fricative: The sound is produced by forcing air through a very narrow gap, causing
friction.
How to Produce the /v/ Sound:
1. Raise your lower lip until it makes light contact with the bottom edge of your upper front
teeth.
2. Vibrate your vocal cords (make a "zzzz" or "buzzing" sound).
3. Push air out of your mouth through the small opening between your lip and teeth. The
sound should be continuous, like vvvvvvv.
109
Contrast with /f/: The /v/ sound is the "twin" of the /f/ sound. They are both made with the
exact same mouth position (lower lip on upper teeth). The only difference is that /f/ is voiceless
(no vibration, just air), while /v/ is voiced (vibration).
 Practice: Say "fan" (no vibration) and then "van" (vibration).
 Minimal Pairs (/f/ vs. /v/):
o fan / van
o fat / vat
o file / vile
o safe / save
o leaf / leave
o strife / strive
Contrast with /b/: Students often confuse /v/ and /b/. The /b/ sound is a bilabial stop, meaning
both lips come together (bi-labial) and the air is stopped completely before being released. The
/v/ sound is a continuous fricative using the lip and teeth.
 Minimal Pairs (/b/ vs. /v/):
o ban / van
o berry / very

o bet / vet
o curb / curve

GRAMMAR: MAKING REQUESTS USING 'WILL,' 'COULD,' AND 'MAY'
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define what a "request" is.
2. Identify 'will,' 'could,' and 'may' as modal verbs used for making requests.
3. Differentiate the levels of formality between these modal verbs.
4. Construct polite and grammatically correct requests for different social situations.
A request is the act of politely or formally asking for something or asking someone to do
something. In English, we use modal verbs to make our requests sound more polite and less like
a direct command.
Using 'Will':
 Form: Will you + (base form of verb)...?
 Usage: This is a common and polite way to make a request, but it is often more direct or
casual. It's common among friends, family, or when asking for a simple, expected action.
It can sometimes sound like a polite instruction.
 Examples:
o "Will you pass the salt, please?" (A simple request at the dinner table)
o "Will you be quiet?" (This sounds more like a command)
o "Will you help me carry this?" (A direct request for help)
Using 'Could':
 Form: Could you + (base form of verb)...?
 Usage: This is a very polite and common way to ask for a favour. It is "safer" and more
formal than 'will.' It sounds less like a command and more like a genuine question,
acknowledging that the other person has the option to say no.
114
 Examples:
o "Could you open the window, please?" (More polite than "Will you open...")
o "Could you please tell me the time?"
o "Could you lend me your pen for a moment?"
Using 'May':
 Form: May I + (base form of verb)...?
 Usage: This is the most formal of the three and is used specifically to ask for permission.
You are not asking the other person to do something for you; you are asking if you are
allowed to do something.
 Examples:
o "May I use your telephone?" (Asking for permission)
o "May I be excused?" (Asking to leave the room)
o "May I ask a question?" (A very polite way to interrupt or speak to a teacher)
Comparing Formality (Lowest to Highest):
1. Command: "Open the door." (Rude)
2. Request with 'Will': "Will you open the door?" (Polite, but direct)
3. Request with 'Could': "Could you open the door?" (More polite and considerate)
4. Request with 'May' (Permission): "May I open the door?" (Asking if you are allowed to
do it)
Class Activity (Role-Play): Have students pair up and practice the following scenarios:
1. Student A: Ask your partner (Student B) to lend you their notebook, using 'could.'
2. Student B: Ask the teacher (Student A) for permission to go to the sickbay, using 'may.'

3. Student A: Ask your friend (Student B) to wait for you after school, using 'will.'

3. Which is more formal: "Will you help me?" or "Could you help me?"

1. What is a Descriptive Essay? The purpose of a descriptive essay is to paint a picture with

1. Key Definitions:
 Folktale: A traditional story that has been passed down orally (by word of mouth) from
one generation to the next. Folktales often feature animal characters that can talk (like
Anansi the Spider or the Tortoise), magic, and exaggerated events. Their main purpose is
usually to entertain or to teach a lesson.
 Plot: The plot is what happens in the story. It is the sequence of events (e.g., "The
tortoise and the hare decide to race. The hare runs fast and then sleeps. The tortoise walks
slowly and steadily. The tortoise wins.")
 Theme: The theme is the main idea, moral, or lesson of the story. It's a universal truth
about life that the story reveals. The theme is not the plot.
o Plot: A boy lies about a wolf.
o Theme: If you lie all the time, no one will believe you when you tell the truth.
2. How to Find the Theme of a Folktale: To find the theme, you must "read between the lines"
and ask yourself these questions:

1. Characters: Who is the main character (protagonist)? What are their good and bad

2. Conflict: What is the main problem the character faces?
3. Climax & Resolution: How is the problem solved? What happens in the end?
4. The Big Question: What lesson did the main character learn (or fail to learn)? What is
the story really trying to teach me about life, or about how to behave?

2. Discuss the Plot: Ask students to summarize what happened in the story.
3. Analyze the Characters:
o Who is the "hero" (protagonist)? Who is the "villain" (antagonist)?
o What qualities does the hero have (e.g., clever, brave, persistent, kind)?
o What qualities does the villain have (e.g., greedy, foolish, arrogant, lazy)?
4. Identify the Theme:
o Ask: What quality is rewarded in the end? What quality is punished?
o (e.g., "The clever tortoise wins, and the arrogant hare loses.")
o Ask: So, what is the lesson the story is teaching us?
o (Possible Themes: "Slow and steady wins the race," "Persistence is better than
arrogance," "Don't underestimate your opponent.")
o Write the identified theme(s) on the board.

The sound /h/ is known as a voiceless glottal fricative.

vibration. It is a quiet sound.

in your throat.
 Fricative: The sound is made by pushing air through a narrow opening.

before you make a vowel sound. Your mouth and lips will take the shape of the vowel that
follows it (e.g., the mouth shape for /h/ in 'he' is different from /h/ in 'ho'). It's the sound you
make when you breathe heavily on a pair of glasses to clean them.

What is Intonation? Intonation is the "music" or "melody" of speech. It is the way our voice
rises (goes up) and falls (goes down) when we speak. Intonation is very important because it can
change the entire meaning of a sentence, even if the words are the same. It shows our mood
(happy, sad, angry) or purpose (asking, telling).
The two main intonation patterns (or tunes) are the Falling Tone and the Rising Tone.
1. The Falling Tone (Tune 1): The pitch of the voice starts high and falls to a low pitch at the
end of the sentence. It sounds final and confident.
 When to use it:
o Statements (Declarative Sentences):
 My name is ↘Bisi.
 The sky is ↘blue.
 We live in ↘Lagos.
o "Wh-" Questions (Questions starting with Who, What, Where, When, Why,
How):
165
 What is your ↘name?
 Where do you ↘live?
 How are you ↘today?
o Commands (Imperative Sentences):
 Go and sit ↘down.
 Open your ↘book.
2. The Rising Tone (Tune 2): The pitch of the voice starts low and rises to a high pitch at the
end. It sounds questioning, incomplete, or polite.
 When to use it:
o "Yes/No" Questions (Questions that can be answered with 'yes' or 'no'):
 Are you ↗coming?
 Is your name ↗Bisi?
 Do you have a ↗pen?
o Listing Items (The voice rises on each item except the last one):
 I bought ↗bread, ↗eggs, ↗milk, and ↘fish.

READING & COMPREHENSION: READING FOR SPEED
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

1. What is Speed Reading? Speed reading is the ability to read and understand text quickly. It is
not about reading every single word, but about understanding the main ideas in less time.
2. Bad Habits That Slow You Down (and How to Stop Them):
 Vocalization: This is the habit of "mouthing" the words as you read them.
 Sub-vocalization: This is the habit of "saying" every word in your head. This is the
biggest barrier to fast reading.
o Solution: Try to silence your "inner voice." Chew gum (quietly) or hum to distract
the part of your brain that wants to "say" the words.
 Regression: This is the habit of your eyes "going back" to re-read words you have
already passed. It's often unnecessary.
o Solution: Use a "pacer"—like a pen, your finger, or a piece of paper—and move it
under the line you are reading. Keep the pacer moving forward at a steady pace
and force your eyes to keep up.
 Word-by-Word Reading: This is reading one word at a time. It is very slow.
o Solution: Learn Phrase Reading.
3. The Technique of Phrase Reading: Instead of reading: "The / cat / sat / on / the / mat." Train
your eyes to "chunk" words into phrases: "(The cat) / (sat on) / (the mat)."
You stop your eyes only 3 times, not 6. This doubles your reading speed.

emphasis) is making one word sound louder, longer, and higher in pitch than the other words.
 Content Words (Nouns, Main Verbs, Adjectives, Adverbs) are usually STRESSED.
(e.g., cat, eat, big, quickly)
 Function Words (Prepositions, Articles, Conjunctions) are usually unstressed. (e.g., on,
the, and)

completely depending on which word you stress. Let's use the sentence: "Bisi ate my rice
yesterday."
1. BISI ate my rice yesterday.
o Meaning: Bisi did it, not Tunde or Ayo. (Emphasizes who)
2. Bisi ATE my rice yesterday.
o Meaning: She ate it; she didn't just look at it or throw it away. (Emphasizes the action)
3. Bisi ate MY rice yesterday.
o Meaning: She ate my rice, not your rice. (Emphasizes whose)
180
4. Bisi ate my RICE yesterday.
o Meaning: She ate the rice, not the beans. (Emphasizes what)
5. Bisi ate my rice YESTERDAY.
o Meaning: She ate it yesterday, not today. (Emphasizes when)

 Statement (Falling Tone):
o BISI ate my rice yester↘day. (Telling someone a fact).
 Question (Rising Tone):

 Command (Falling Tone):

GRAMMAR: REPORTED COMMANDS AND DIALOGUE
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define an imperative (a command or request).
2. Convert a direct positive command into a reported command using to + infinitive.
3. Convert a direct negative command into a reported command using not to + infinitive.
4. Convert a direct request into a reported request, using appropriate reporting verbs (e.g.,
'asked', 'begged').
5. Convert a short, two-line dialogue into reported (indirect) speech.
This lesson builds on Week 1's "Reported Statements." Commands and dialogues follow
different rules.
1. Reporting Commands (Positive): A direct command is a "do" instruction (e.g., "Go away,"
"Sit down").
 Rule: We do not use 'that'. We use a reporting verb (like told, ordered, commanded) +
object + to + the base form of the verb (the infinitive).
 Direct: The teacher said, "Be quiet."
 Indirect: The teacher told us to be quiet.
 Direct: The general shouted, "Attack!"
 Indirect: The general ordered the soldiers to attack.
2. Reporting Commands (Negative): A negative command is a "don't" instruction (e.g., "Don't
talk").
 Rule: We use the reporting verb + object + not to + the base form of the verb.
 Direct: The mother said, "Don't go out."
198
 Indirect: The mother warned her son not to go out.
 Direct: "Don't write on the wall," he said to me.
 Indirect: He told me not to write on the wall.
3. Reporting Requests: A request uses "Please" (e.g., "Please help me").
 Rule: We use a "softer" reporting verb (like asked, begged, requested) + object + to + the
base form. The word "please" is removed.
 Direct: She said, "Please wait for me."
 Indirect: She asked me to wait for her.
 Direct: "Please, please give me some food," the beggar said.
 Indirect: The beggar begged the man to give him some food.
4. Reporting Dialogue: A dialogue is a conversation between two or more people. To report it,
you must report each line, one after the other, using the correct rules for statements, questions, or
commands. You must use reporting verbs like 'said,' 'replied,' 'asked,' 'answered,' 'added,' etc., to
make it flow.
 Direct Dialogue:
o Ayo: "What are you doing here, Bisi?"
o Bisi: "I am waiting for my mother. She will be here soon."
o Ayo: "Please give this book to her."
 Indirect (Reported) Dialogue:
o Ayo asked Bisi what she was doing there. Bisi replied that she was waiting for
her mother and added that she would be there soon. Ayo then asked Bisi to
give that book to her.

1. Setting in Drama:
 Definition: The "Setting" is the Time (When? e.g., 1960, modern-day) and Place
(Where? e.g., a king's palace, a poor village) of the play.
 How it's established: In a play, the setting is not described by a narrator. It is established
through:
1. Stage Directions: At the beginning of an Act or Scene, the playwright writes a
detailed description of the room or location. (e.g., [A sparsely furnished room. A
single wooden chair and a mat on the floor. It is night.])
2. Dialogue: Characters' speech can tell us the time or place (e.g., "The king will be
here soon," "This city is too expensive," "I remember before the war...").
 Analysis: The setting is not just a background; it affects the story.
o Class Discussion: "In our play, [Title of Play], where is the action set? (e.g., a
palace, a village). How does this place affect the problems the characters face?
How would the story be different if it were set in a modern city?"
2. Language in Drama:
208
 Definition: "Language" refers to the style of speaking, or diction, used by the characters.
 Analysis: Language is a primary tool for characterization. We must ask:
o Is the language formal or informal? (e.g., Does the King speak in complex
sentences, while the servant uses simple words or slang?)
o Does the language show the character's education or social class?
o Does the character use proverbs and idioms? (This might show they are
traditional or wise).
o Does the character speak in verse (poetry) or prose (normal sentences)? (e.g., In
Shakespeare, nobles speak in verse, commoners in prose).
 Class Discussion: "Think about [Main Character] and [Minor Character] in our play. Do
they speak the same way? What is the difference? What does [Main Character's] way of
speaking tell us about their personality (e.g., are they educated, humble, arrogant)?"
3. Characterization in Drama:
 Definition: (Review) The method the playwright uses to reveal a character's personality.
 How it differs from Prose: In a novel (prose), the author can simply tell us what a
character is thinking ("Tunde was secretly angry..."). In a play (drama), we cannot hear
the character's private thoughts (unless they have a soliloquy—a speech given alone on
stage).
 Analysis: Therefore, in drama, we must rely only on external evidence:
1. Dialogue: What the character says.
2. Action: What the character does (as written in the stage directions, e.g., [He
slams his fist on the table]).
3. What other characters say about them.
209
 Class Activity: "Let's analyze [Antagonist]. Find one line of dialogue they say that shows
their evil/greedy/selfish nature. Now find one stage direction that describes an action
they take. What do these two things together tell us about them?"

What is Rhythm? Rhythm is the "beat" or "music" of a poem. It is a pattern of sound created by
the arrangement of stressed (strong, emphasized) and unstressed (weak, unemphasized)

syllables. "Cat" has one syllable. "Beau-ti-ful" has three syllables.

(ther) is unstressed.
o Example: In the word "beLIEVE," the first syllable (be) is unstressed, and the
second (LIEVE) is stressed.

unstressed syllables. Let's mark the stressed syllables ( / ) and unstressed syllables ( - ).

in-to the hall ( - / / - / - / - / ) -> This is more complex.
 Let's use a simpler, more regular one: (from "I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud") I wandered

5. Convert negative direct requests into reported requests using not to-infinitive.
This lesson builds on our work with reported speech. While a command is a direct order (e.g.,
"Sit down!"), a request is a polite way of asking someone to do something.
1. Identifying Direct Requests: Direct requests are marked by polite words and phrases:
 "Please help me."
 "Could you pass the salt, please?"
 "Will you be quiet?"
 "Would you mind closing the door?"
2. Reporting Verbs for Requests: When reporting a request, we don't usually use "said" or
"ordered." We use "softer" verbs that show politeness:
 asked (most common)
 requested
 begged (for a very strong, desperate request)
 implored (a formal way of "begged")
3. The Rule for Reporting Requests: The rule is very similar to reported commands.
1. The polite word ("please," "could you") is removed.
214
2. The reporting verb (e.g., asked) is followed by the person spoken to (the object).
3. The main verb is changed to its to-infinitive form (to + verb).
 Structure: Reporting Verb + Object + to + Base Verb
Examples (Positive Requests):
 Direct: She said, "Please help me."
 Indirect: She asked me to help her.
 Direct: The boy said, "Could you lend me your pen, please?"
 Indirect: The boy requested that I lend him my pen.
o OR (simpler): The boy asked me to lend him his pen.
 Direct: "I beg you, give me some food," the beggar said.
 Indirect: The beggar begged the man to give him some food.
4. Reporting Negative Requests:
 Structure: Reporting Verb + Object + not to + Base Verb
 Direct: The teacher said, "Please don't make noise."
 Indirect: The teacher asked us not to make noise.
 Direct: "Please, please don't go," she said to him.
 Indirect: She begged him not to go.

What is an Argumentative Essay? An argumentative essay (also called a persuasive essay) is a

o Use the "PEE" structure: Point (your reason), Example (a fact or scenario),

1. What are Figures of Speech? Figures of speech (or "literary devices") are words and phrases

"picture" in the reader's mind.
2. Key Figures of Speech to Find:
 A. Simile:
o Definition: A comparison between two different things, using the words 'like' or

o Generic Example: "He is as fast as a cheetah." "Her smile was like the sun."
o Text Example: (Teacher: "Let's open our book, [Prose Text Title], to page [X].

 B. Metaphor:
o Definition: A direct comparison, saying one thing is another. It does not use 'like'

o Text Example: (Teacher: "On page [Y], the author describes the angry chief. He

SPEECH WORK: CONSONANT SOUNDS /s/ AND /z/
The consonant sounds /s/ and /z/ are both alveolar fricatives, meaning they are produced with
the tongue close to the alveolar ridge (the hard bump just behind the upper front teeth) and the
airflow is forced through a narrow passage, creating friction. However, they differ in voicing —
/s/ is voiceless while /z/ is voiced.
1. HOW TO PRODUCE /s/ AND /z/
o Both sounds are produced by directing a steady stream of air through a narrow
space between the tongue and the alveolar ridge.
o For /s/ (voiceless): the vocal cords do not vibrate. The sound is simply a hissing
puff of air.
 Example words: sip, sun, stone, bus, loose, peace, rice.
o For /z/ (voiced): the vocal cords vibrate, producing a buzzing sound.
 Example words: zip, zoo, buzz, rise, peas, lose, these.
o To test the difference, place two fingers lightly on your throat as you pronounce
the sounds. You will feel a vibration with /z/ but none with /s/.
2. THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN /s/ AND /z/
o The only difference between them is voicing — whether the vocal cords vibrate
or not.
o /s/ is a voiceless alveolar fricative.
o /z/ is a voiced alveolar fricative.
3. MINIMAL PAIRS PRACTICE
Minimal pairs are words that differ by only one sound. Practice helps learners hear and
pronounce the difference.
/s/ (Voiceless) /z/ (Voiced)

Repeat each pair aloud, paying attention to the buzzing vibration for /z/.
4. THE PLURAL “S” ENDING RULE
The sound of ‘s’ at the end of words can be pronounced as /s/ or /z/ depending on the
sound before it.
o Rule 1: If the final sound before the ‘s’ is voiceless (p, t, k, f), then the ‘s’ is
pronounced as /s/.
 Examples: cats, books, slips, cliffs.
o Rule 2: If the final sound before the ‘s’ is voiced (b, d, g, v, l, m, n, or any
vowel), then the ‘s’ is pronounced as /z/.
 Examples: dogs, boys, beds, fans, leaves.

 Examples:
o She is as gentle as a dove.
o The boy runs like the wind.
 Effect: It makes descriptions more vivid and imaginative.
2. Metaphor:
A metaphor is a direct comparison between two different things without using as or like.
 Examples:
o Time is a thief.
o The world is a stage.
 Effect: It gives strong imagery and emotional force to writing.
3. Personification:
This figure of speech gives human qualities or actions to non-human things.
 Examples:
o The sun smiled down on us.
o The wind whispered through the trees.
 Effect: It makes nature or objects appear alive and friendly.
270
4. Hyperbole:
This is an exaggeration used for emphasis or humor.
 Examples:
o I’ve told you a thousand times to sit down!
o The bag weighed a ton.
 Effect: It stresses importance and adds humour or emphasis.
5. Onomatopoeia:
This is the use of words that imitate natural sounds.
 Examples:
o The bees buzzed in the garden.
o The thunder rumbled across the sky.
 Effect: It helps readers to “hear” the sounds being described.
6. Irony:
Saying the opposite of what is meant, often humorously.
 Example: The lazy man calls the hardworking one idle.
 Effect: It creates surprise or humor.
7. Alliteration:
The repetition of the same sound at the beginning of words.
 Example: Peter picked a peck of pickled pepper.
 Effect: It adds rhythm and musical quality to writing.
Application in Literature:
In literature, authors use figures of speech to create images, express emotions, and make readers
enjoy the text more. For instance, in poetry, similes and metaphors are used to compare love,
nature, or emotions.

Review 2: Stress (The “Beat” of Speech)
Definition: Stress is the emphasis (extra force, length, and higher pitch) given to a particular
syllable in a word, or to a particular word in a sentence.
Type A: Emphatic Stress (Changing Meaning)
Emphatic stress shows the word that carries the main information or contrast in a sentence.
Example Sentence: “Tunde gave Bisi the red bag.”
 “TUNDE gave Bisi the red bag.” → (Meaning: Tunde, not Ayo.)
 “Tunde GAVE Bisi the red bag.” → (Meaning: He gave, not sold.)
 “Tunde gave BISI the red bag.” → (Meaning: Bisi, not her sister.)
 “Tunde gave Bisi the RED bag.” → (Meaning: Not the blue one.)
 “Tunde gave Bisi the red BAG.” → (Meaning: Not the red shoe.)

Many two-syllable words change stress depending on whether they are nouns or verbs.
 Rule:
o Noun → Stress the first syllable.

GRAMMAR: USING DESPITE AND IN SPITE OF
Definition and Purpose:
Despite and in spite of are prepositions that mean the same thing. They are used to show
contrast or unexpected results, similar to although. They both mean “without being affected
by” or “even though.”
Grammatical Rule:
 Despite / In spite of must be followed by a noun, pronoun, or gerund (-ing form).
 Structure: Despite / In spite of + Noun / Gerund

 He passed the test despite being sick.
 She was sad in spite of winning the prize.
Contrasting with “Although”:
 Although is a conjunction, not a preposition.
 Although must be followed by a clause (Subject + Verb).
Expression Usage Example
Although (Clause) Although it was raining, he went out.
Despite (Noun) Despite the rain, he went out.
Despite (Gerund) Despite being sick, he worked hard.

COMPOSITION: REPORT WRITING
1. Meaning of Report Writing
A report is a formal and factual written account of an event, situation, or investigation that has
already taken place. It presents information clearly, truthfully, and in a logical sequence so that
the reader can understand exactly what happened without confusion or bias.
A report is not based on opinions or emotions but on facts and observations.
Reports are often written by students, prefects, secretaries, class captains, and journalists to
inform people in authority—like the principal, the head teacher, or a committee—about an event
or an incident.
2. PURPOSE OF REPORT WRITING
The main purpose of report writing is to inform. It presents accurate information about a
particular event or issue so that the reader can understand what happened and make decisions if
necessary.
Other purposes include:
 To record important events or findings.
 To give feedback to superiors or officials.
 To provide evidence of an occurrence.
 To make recommendations for action.
For example:
 A class captain writes a report to the principal about a broken window.
 A club secretary writes a report to the patron about the club’s recent meeting.
 A journalist writes a report about a public event for the newspaper.
3. CHARACTERISTICS (FEATURES) OF A GOOD REPORT
A good report should:
295
 Be factual: It must be based only on what actually happened.
 Be clear and concise: Avoid unnecessary details or long sentences.
 Be objective: The writer should not include personal opinions or emotions.
 Be organized: The information must be arranged in a logical, time-based order.
 Be formal: The tone should be serious and respectful. Avoid slang, jokes, or
contractions.
 Be written in the past tense: Since it is about something that has already happened.
4. LANGUAGE AND STYLE OF A REPORT
The language used in a report is formal and simple.
Key features include:
 Third person point of view (he, she, they, the students).
 Past tense verbs (e.g., The event began at 9 a.m.).
 No exaggerations or personal emotions.
 Correct grammar, punctuation, and paragraphing.
Avoid phrases like:
“It was so amazing and I was very happy.”
Use instead:
“The event was well-organized and successful.”

A formal report usually follows this structure:
1. Title:
The title should be specific, written in capital letters, and underlined.

o Paragraph 1: Describe how the event began.
o Paragraph 2: State the major activities or incidents.
o Paragraph 3: Mention any special guests, important speeches, or accidents if any

1. The /ʃ/ Sound (The “shh” Sound):
 Description: /ʃ/ is a voiceless post-alveolar fricative.
 How to Produce It:
o Round your lips slightly as if to say “shh.”
o Raise the blade (not the tip) of your tongue toward the hard area behind the

o Do not let your tongue touch the ridge.
o Push air continuously through the narrow space to make a hissing sound.
o The sound is long and continuous: “shshshsh.”

 sh: sheep, ship, mash, cash
 ch (in French words): chef, Chicago, machine
 s / ss: sure, pressure, Russia
 ti: nation, station, patient

 How to Produce It:
o Start by placing your tongue tip on the alveolar ridge, like for /t/, to block the air.

o Release the air slowly into the /ʃ/ sound.
o It is a short, sharp sound as in “church.”

 ch: cheap, chip, march, church
 tch: catch, match, watch
 t (before ‘u’): future, nature, century

w-w-w-wonder.

 How to Produce:

o Glide quickly away from this position to the next vowel, e.g., y-y-y-yellow.
 Common Spellings:
o y: yellow, yes, yard, beyond

LITERATURE IN ENGLISH: REVIEW OF RECOMMENDED PROSE AND DRAMA

1. Prose (Novel or Short Story):

2. Drama (Play):
 Focus on dialogue, conflict, climax, resolution, and message (theme).

3. Major Elements to Review:
 Plot: The arrangement of events.
 Theme: The central idea.
 Characterization: The way characters are portrayed.
 Diction: The playwright’s or author’s word choice.
4. Moral Lessons from Literature:
