WEEK 1: HORTICULTURAL CROP IMPROVEMENT
1.1 INTRODUCTION
Horticultural crop improvement, also known as plant breeding, is the art and science of changing
the genetic traits of plants to develop new varieties with more desirable characteristics. It is a
deliberate, human-driven evolution of crops to better suit human needs.
1.2 AIMS OF CROP IMPROVEMENT
The primary goal is to make plants more useful and productive. Specific aims include:
1. Increased Yield: Developing crops that produce more food, fibre, or ornamental value
per unit area (e.g., tomatoes with more fruits per plant, maize with larger cobs).
2. Improved Quality:
2
o Nutritional Quality: Enhancing vitamin, protein, or mineral content (e.g.,
"Golden Rice" enriched with Vitamin A).
o Eating Quality: Better taste, flavour, tenderness, or texture.
o Aesthetic Quality: Brighter colours, larger flowers, or more attractive shapes for
ornamental plants.
o Keeping Quality: Longer shelf-life for fruits and vegetables, reducing postharvest
losses.
3. Pest and Disease Resistance: Breeding plants that can naturally defend themselves
against specific insects, fungi, bacteria, or viruses (e.g., cassava resistant to Cassava
Mosaic Disease).
4. Abiotic Stress Tolerance: Developing "hardy" crops that can withstand difficult
environmental conditions like:
o Drought tolerance (e.g., maize that survives with less water).
o Salinity tolerance (for coastal or irrigated areas).
o Heat or cold tolerance.
5. Desirable Plant Architecture: Modifying the plant's shape or structure (e.g., creating
dwarf rice varieties that don't fall over, or climbing bean varieties for vertical gardens).
6. Ease of Harvesting: Breeding crops that mature uniformly for mechanical harvesting or
have fewer thorns.
1.3 METHODS OF CROP IMPROVEMENT
Plant breeders use several established methods to achieve these aims.
1. SELECTION
This is the oldest and most basic method. It involves identifying plants with desirable traits and
using them to create the next generation.
 Mass Selection:
o Process: A large number of plants (a "mass") are evaluated in the field. The
"best" looking plants (phenotype) are selected and harvested. Their seeds are
bulked together and planted to form the next generation.
3
o Use: Common for cross-pollinated crops. It's simple and cheap but slow and not
very precise.
 Pure-line Selection:
o Process: Used for self-pollinating crops. A breeder selects a single superior plant.
The seeds from this one plant are collected and grown out. Since the plant selfpollinates,
all its offspring are genetically very similar (a "pure-line").
o Use: Good for creating highly uniform varieties (e.g., a new tomato or bean
variety).
 Clonal Selection:
o Process: Used for crops that are propagated vegetatively (asexually) like cassava,
yams, bananas, or potatoes. A breeder identifies a single superior plant (e.g., a
cassava plant with huge tubers). Cuttings ("clones") are taken from this one plant
and multiplied.
o Use: All offspring are genetically identical to the superior parent.
2. HYBRIDIZATION (CROSS-BREEDING)
This is the most powerful traditional breeding method. It involves crossing two parent plants
with different desirable traits, to produce offspring that combines those traits.
 Process:
1. Parent Selection: Choose two parents (Parent A and Parent B).
 Example: Parent A (Tomato) = High-yielding but disease-susceptible.
Parent B (Tomato) = Low-yielding but disease-resistant.
 Goal: Create a new variety that is High-yielding AND Disease-resistant.
2. Emasculation: To prevent self-pollination, the breeder must carefully remove the
male parts (stamens/anthers) from the flower of Parent A (the designated "female"
parent) before it produces pollen.
3. Pollination: The breeder collects pollen from the flower of Parent B (the "male"
parent) and manually transfers it to the stigma (female part) of the emasculated
flower of Parent A.
4
4. Bagging: The pollinated flower is covered with a small bag (paper or cloth) to
prevent stray, unwanted pollen from landing on it.
5. Harvesting F1: The flower develops into a fruit (a tomato). The seeds inside are
the "First Filial" (F1) generation. These seeds contain genes from both parents.
6. Growing F1 & F2: The F1 seeds are planted. The F1 plants are often selfpollinated
to produce the "Second Filial" (F2) generation.
7. Selection (The long part): The F2 generation is where all the gene combinations
appear. The breeder must plant thousands of F2 seeds and select the very few
individual plants that show the desired combination (high yield + disease
resistance).
8. Stabilization: These selected plants are grown and re-selected over many (6-10)
generations until their desirable traits are "fixed" (i.e., they breed true).
 Types of Hybridization:
o Intraspecific: Crossing two different varieties within the same species (e.g., Zea
mays 'A' x Zea mays 'B'). This is the most common type.
o Interspecific: Crossing two different (but related) species (e.g., to transfer a wild,
disease-resistant gene into a cultivated species).

WEEK 2: GENETIC PRINCIPLES IN CROP PRODUCTION
2.1 INTRODUCTION
Genetics is the science of heredity. It explains how traits are passed from parents to offspring.
This is the "science" behind the "art" of plant breeding. The foundation of this science was laid
by Gregor Mendel.
2.2 KEY GENETIC TERMINOLOGY
 Gene: The basic unit of heredity; a segment of DNA that codes for a specific trait (e.g.,
the gene for flower colour).
 Allele: Different forms of the same gene (e.g., for the flower colour gene, the alleles
might be 'R' for red and 'r' for white).
 Phenotype: The physical appearance or observable trait of an organism (e.g., "The
flower is red").
 Genotype: The genetic makeup of an organism; the combination of alleles it has (e.g.,
'RR', 'Rr', or 'rr').
7
 Homozygous: Having two identical alleles for a trait (e.g., RR or rr). This is "truebreeding."
 Heterozygous: Having two different alleles for a trait (e.g., Rr).
 Dominant Allele: An allele that masks the effect of a recessive allele when present (e.g.,
if 'R' is dominant, both RR and Rr genotypes will have red flowers). Represented by a
capital letter.
 Recessive Allele: An allele that is masked by a dominant allele. It is only expressed in
the phenotype when it is homozygous (e.g., rr for white flowers). Represented by a small
letter.
2.3 MENDEL'S LAWS OF HEREDITY
1. MENDEL'S FIRST LAW: THE LAW OF SEGREGATION
 Concept: For any trait, an organism inherits two alleles, one from each parent. These two
alleles segregate (separate) from each other during the formation of gametes (pollen or
ovules), so that each gamete contains only one allele for that trait.
 Example (Monohybrid Cross):
o A homozygous dominant plant (RR - Red) is crossed with a homozygous
recessive plant (rr - white).
o P (Parent) Generation: RR x rr
o Gametes: The RR parent only makes R gametes. The rr parent only makes r
gametes.
o F1 Generation: All offspring receive one 'R' and one 'r', so all are Rr (genotype)
and all have Red flowers (phenotype).
o Now, cross two F1 plants: Rr x Rr
o Gametes: Each F1 parent produces gametes that are 50% R and 50% r.
o F2 Generation (Using a Punnett Square): | | R | r | | :---: | :---: | :---: | | R | RR |
Rr | | r | Rr | rr |
o Phenotypic Ratio: 3 Red : 1 White (75% Red, 25% White)
o Genotypic Ratio: 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr (25% Homozygous Dominant, 50%
Heterozygous, 25% Homozygous Recessive)
8
 Significance: This law explains why traits can "disappear" in the F1 generation (white)
and "reappear" in the F2 generation.
2. MENDEL'S SECOND LAW: THE LAW OF INDEPENDENT ASSORTMENT
 Concept: Alleles for different traits are inherited independently of one another. The
inheritance of one trait (like flower colour) does not affect the inheritance of another trait
(like plant height).
 Example (Dihybrid Cross):
o A parent that is heterozygous for two traits: Round, Yellow seeds (RrYy)
o The gene for shape (R/r) and the gene for colour (Y/y) are on different
chromosomes.
o When this plant makes gametes, the alleles "assort independently." It can make
four types of gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry.
o When two such plants are crossed (RrYy x RrYy), it results in the classic 9:3:3:1
phenotypic ratio (9 Round-Yellow, 3 Round-green, 3 wrinkled-Yellow, 1
wrinkled-green).
 Significance: This is the scientific reason why hybridization (Week 1) works! It shows
that we can create new combinations of traits. We can take "Round seeds" from one
parent and "Yellow colour" from another and combine them.
2.4 APPLICATIONS IN CROP PRODUCTION
1. Systematic Breeding: Mendel's laws turned plant breeding from guesswork into a
predictive science. Breeders can now use Punnett squares to predict the outcomes of their
crosses.
2. Hybrid Vigour (Heterosis): This is a key application. It is the phenomenon where the
F1 hybrid (e.g., Rr) is superior to both of its homozygous parents (e.g., RR and rr). It
might be bigger, more disease-resistant, or higher-yielding. The entire hybrid seed
industry (e.g., hybrid maize) is built on this principle. Farmers buy F1 seeds, get the
hybrid vigour, but cannot save the seeds, as the F2 generation will segregate and lose the
superior traits.
9
3. Understanding Recessive Traits: Breeders can now understand why a undesirable trait
(like susceptibility to a disease) might be "hidden" as a recessive allele in a heterozygous
plant.

WEEK 3: BIOTECHNOLOGY IN HORTICULTURE
3.1 INTRODUCTION
Biotechnology involves using living organisms or their products to make or modify products for
a specific use. In horticulture, it allows for rapid and precise plant improvement, far beyond the
speed of traditional breeding.
3.2 TISSUE CULTURE (MICROPROPAGATION)
A. DEFINITION
Tissue culture is an in-vitro (in glass) technique of growing new plants from tiny pieces of plant
tissue (like a leaf, stem, or root tip) on a sterile, artificial nutrient medium (like a jelly). The
small piece of tissue used is called an explant. The key principle is totipotency – the ability of a
single plant cell to grow into a complete, new plant.
B. THE PROCESS
11
1. Explant Selection: A small, healthy piece of the desired plant (the "mother plant") is
selected (e.g., the meristem tip, a leaf section, a bud).
2. Sterilization: This is the most critical step. The explant is surface-sterilized (using bleach
or alcohol) to kill all fungi and bacteria. The nutrient medium and all tools are also
sterilized (usually in an autoclave). The work is done in a laminar flow hood which
provides sterile air.
3. Inoculation: The sterile explant is placed onto the sterile nutrient medium (a gel
containing water, sugar, macro/micro-nutrients, and vitamins) in a test tube or jar.
4. Incubation: The jar is placed in a growth room with controlled light and temperature.
5. Callus & Shoot Formation: The explant cells divide and form an undifferentiated mass
of cells called a callus. By adding specific plant hormones (auxins and cytokinins) to
the medium, the breeder can encourage the callus to form shoots.
6. Rooting: The new, small shoots are transferred to a different medium containing
hormones that promote root growth.
7. Acclimatization (Hardening Off): The small "plantlets" are removed from the sterile,
high-humidity jars. Their roots are washed, and they are planted in sterile soil in a
greenhouse. They must be slowly adapted (acclimatized) to the "real world" conditions of
lower humidity and natural soil.
C. APPLICATIONS OF TISSUE CULTURE
1. Mass Propagation (Cloning): One explant can produce thousands or millions of
genetically identical (clonal) plantlets in a short time. This is vital for commercial
production of ornamentals (e.g., orchids) and crops like bananas, plantains, and yams.
2. Production of Disease-Free Plants: The meristem tip (the very tip of a growing shoot) is
often free of viruses, even if the mother plant is infected. By tissue-culturing the
meristem, "clean" planting material can be produced.
3. Germplasm Conservation: Plant varieties can be stored as tiny, slow-growing tissue
cultures in a lab ("gene bank"), saving space and protecting against field losses.
4. Speeding up Breeding: Allows breeders to grow new hybrids or conduct genetic
experiments much faster than in the field.
12
3.3 GENETIC MODIFICATION (GM CROPS)
A. DEFINITION
Genetic Modification (GM), or Genetic Engineering (GE), is a biotechnology technique that
involves directly inserting a specific gene from one organism (e.g., a bacterium, virus, or another
plant) into the DNA of another organism (the crop). The resulting plant is a "Genetically
Modified Organism" (GMO) or a "transgenic" plant. This is different from hybridization, which
can only cross related species.
B. THE PROCESS (SIMPLIFIED)
1. Gene Identification: A scientist identifies a useful gene (e.g., the Bt gene from the
bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis, which is toxic to insects).
2. Gene Isolation: The gene is isolated ("cut out") using special "restriction" enzymes.
3. Gene Insertion (Transformation): The isolated gene is inserted into the plant's cells.
Two common methods are:
o Gene Gun: The gene is coated onto tiny gold particles and "shot" into the plant
cells.
o Agrobacterium tumefaciens: Using a natural soil bacterium that has the ability to
transfer its own DNA (on a "Ti plasmid") into a plant. Scientists replace the
bacterium's DNA with the desired gene.
4. Selection & Regeneration: The cells are grown using tissue culture. The medium
contains an "antibiotic marker" so that only the cells that successfully took up the new
gene will survive. These cells are then regenerated into whole plants.
C. APPLICATIONS OF GM CROPS
1. Insect Resistance: Bt Cotton and Bt Maize. They produce a protein that kills specific
insect pests (like the bollworm), reducing the need for chemical insecticides.
2. Herbicide Tolerance: "Roundup Ready" Soybeans or Maize. They are engineered to be
resistant to the herbicide glyphosate. This allows a farmer to plant the crop and then spray
the entire field with the herbicide, killing the weeds but not the crop.
13
3. Nutritional Enhancement: "Golden Rice." It is engineered to produce beta-carotene, a
precursor to Vitamin A, to help combat vitamin deficiency in developing countries.
4. Disease Resistance: Engineering plants to be resistant to viruses (e.g., Papaya Ringspot
Virus).
D. BENEFITS AND CONCERNS
 Benefits: Increased yields, reduced pesticide use, improved nutrition, enhanced food
security.
 Concerns (The Controversy):
o Environmental: Risk of "superweeds" (herbicide resistance spreading) or harm to
non-target insects.
o Human Health: Concerns (largely unproven) about long-term safety or allergies.
o Economic: Control of seeds by a few large corporations; farmers cannot save GM
seeds.

WEEK 4: ORGANIC HORTICULTURE
4.1 INTRODUCTION
Organic horticulture is a holistic production system that avoids the use of synthetic (man-mat)
fertilizers, pesticides, and herbicides. It focuses on building healthy soil and working with natural
ecological cycles to produce nutritious food.
4.2 PRINCIPLES OF ORGANIC HORTICULTURE
The philosophy is guided by four main principles:
1. Principle of Health: Aims to produce healthy soil, healthy plants, healthy animals, and
healthy people. It avoids inputs that can harm health.
2. Principle of Ecology: Works with living ecological systems, not against them. It
emphasizes recycling, biodiversity, and balance in nature.
3. Principle of Fairness: Seeks to ensure fairness in relationships with all involved, from
farmers and workers to consumers.
15
4. Principle of Care: Emphasizes caution and responsibility in managing the farm to
protect the environment and the well-being of future generations.
4.3 KEY PRACTICES IN ORGANIC HORTICULTURE
Organic farmers replace synthetic inputs with natural, knowledge-based practices.
A. SOIL FERTILITY MANAGEMENT
(Goal: "Feed the soil, not the plant.")
1. Composting: The controlled decomposition of organic matter (kitchen scraps, leaves,
grass clippings, small amounts of manure) by microorganisms. The result, "compost," is a
dark, nutrient-rich soil conditioner.
2. Animal Manures: Using waste from poultry, goats, or cattle. It must be composted or
"aged" first, as raw manure can burn plants and contain pathogens.
3. Green Manures / Cover Cropping:
o Cover Crop: A crop (like Mucuna, Centrosema, or Pueraria) planted between
cash crops or during the off-season to cover bare soil.
o Green Manure: A cover crop that is specifically grown to be cut down and tilled
into the soil to add organic matter. Leguminous cover crops (beans, peas) are
special because they "fix" nitrogen from the air into the soil.
4. Crop Rotation: Not planting the same crop in the same spot year after year. (e.g., Plant
maize (heavy feeder) -> then beans (nitrogen fixer) -> then yams (root crop)). This breaks
pest cycles and balances soil nutrient use.
B. PEST AND DISEASE CONTROL
(Goal: Not to eliminate all pests, but to keep them at a manageable, non-damaging level.)
1. Cultural Control:
o Crop Rotation: (As above) Prevents soil-borne diseases from building up.
o Intercropping: Planting two or more different crops together (e.g., maize and
cassava). This confuses pests.
16
o Sanitation: Removing and destroying diseased plants and crop debris.
2. Biological Control:
o Encouraging "beneficial" insects (like ladybugs, which eat aphids) by planting
flowers they like.
o Using microbial pesticides like Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) – (Note: this is the
natural bacteria, not the GM crop).
3. Physical/Mechanical Control:
o Hand-picking large insects (like caterpillars) off plants.
o Using insect traps or reflective mulches.
4. Natural Pesticides:
o Using plant-based sprays like Neem Oil (Azadirachta indica), garlic spray, or hot
pepper spray. These are biodegradable but must still be used with care.
C. WEED CONTROL
1. Mulching: Covering the soil surface with organic materials (straw, grass clippings, wood
chips). This blocks sunlight, preventing weed seeds from germinating, and also conserves
soil moisture.
2. Cover Cropping: (As above) A dense cover crop will "choke out" and smother weeds.
3. Hand Weeding / Hoeing: Mechanical removal of weeds.
4.4 BENEFITS AND CHALLENGES
 Benefits:
o Environmental: No chemical runoff to pollute water; improves soil health and
biodiversity.
o Health: Food is free of synthetic pesticide residues.
o Sustainability: Builds long-term soil fertility.
o Economic: (Potentially) Higher "premium" price for certified organic food; lower
cost of inputs (no chemicals).
 Challenges:
o Labour Intensive: Weeding and compost-making require more physical work.
17
o Lower Yields: (Sometimes, especially in the transition period).
o Certification: The process to become "Certified Organic" can be complex and
expensive for small farmers.

WEEK 6: GREENHOUSE TECHNOLOGY
6.1 INTRODUCTION
A greenhouse (or glasshouse) is a structure designed to provide a controlled, protected
environment for growing crops. It is covered with a transparent or translucent material (like glass
or plastic) that allows sunlight to enter and heats the interior.
6.2 STRUCTURES AND TYPES
1. Glasshouses: The traditional structure, using glass panels in a metal (aluminum) frame.
o Pros: Very long-lasting (30+ years), best light transmission.
o Cons: Very expensive, heavy, fragile (can break), traps heat (a major problem in
Nigeria).
2. Polytunnels (Hoop Houses):
o Structure: Arched hoops (metal or PVC) covered with a single or double layer of
UV-stabilized polyethylene (polythene) plastic.
o Pros: Much cheaper, easy to build, good for extending seasons.
o Cons: Plastic needs replacing every 3-5 years, can be damaged by wind.
20
3. Net Houses (Shade Houses):
o Structure: A simple frame (wood or metal) covered with shade net or insectproof
netting.
o Pros: Very suitable for Nigerian/tropical climates. Does not trap heat; provides
shade (reducing heat stress); and physically excludes insect pests (like whiteflies).
o Cons: Does not protect from heavy rain.
6.3 ENVIRONMENTAL CONTROL (THE "TECHNOLOGY")
A greenhouse allows the grower to become a "weather man" and control key factors:
1. Temperature:
o Cooling (Critical in Nigeria): Ventilation is key. This includes side-vents that
roll up, roof vents that open, and large "extraction" fans to pull hot air out.
"Evaporative cooling" (using wet pads and fans) is a more advanced method.
o Heating (For cold climates/nights): Using electric or gas heaters.
2. Light:
o Reducing: Using shade cloths (nets) over the roof to reduce intense sunlight.
o Adding: Using artificial "grow lights" (e.g., LEDs) to supplement light on cloudy
days or extend the "day length."
3. Water & Humidity:
o Irrigation: Drip irrigation (delivering water directly to the plant's roots) is the
most common and efficient method.
o Humidity: Misters or foggers can be used to raise humidity for seedlings or
certain crops.
4. Pest & Disease Control:
o The structure is a physical barrier.
o Double-door entry systems act as an "airlock" to stop insects from flying in.
o Fine insect netting on all vents and openings.
6.4 BENEFITS OF GREENHOUSE TECHNOLOGY
21
1. Year-Round Production: Allows farmers to grow crops (especially high-value
vegetables like tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers) even in the off-season.
2. Protection from Elements: Protects crops from heavy rain (which can spread disease),
strong winds, and hailstones.
3. Pest and Disease Control: Drastically reduces pest and disease pressure, leading to less
pesticide use and higher quality produce.
4. Increased Yield: Optimized conditions (water, nutrients) lead to much higher yields per
square meter.
5. Water Efficiency: Drip irrigation in a greenhouse uses up to 90% less water than
traditional field irrigation.
6.5 CHALLENGES IN NIGERIA
1. High Initial Cost: The setup cost for glasshouses or well-equipped polytunnels is very
high.
2. Overheating: This is the number one challenge. A poorly designed, poorly ventilated
greenhouse will become an "oven" in the tropical sun and kill plants.
3. Constant Power Supply: Advanced systems (fans, pumps, coolers) require a reliable
electricity source, which is a major problem (requiring generators or large solar setups).
4. Technical Know-How: Managing a greenhouse environment requires training and skill.
5. Availability of Materials: Sourcing high-quality UV-treated plastic, shade nets, and
parts can be difficult or expensive.

WEEK 8: URBAN AND PERI-URBAN HORTICULTURE
8.1 DEFINITIONS
 Urban Horticulture: The practice of growing horticultural crops (fruits, vegetables,
flowers) inside the city boundaries. This includes backyards, balconies, rooftops, and
community gardens.
 Peri-Urban Horticulture: The practice of growing crops on the immediate fringes or
edges of a city, often on small-scale commercial farms, to supply the city market.
8.2 IMPORTANCE OF URBAN/PERI-URBAN HORTICULTURE
1. Food Security & Nutrition: Provides direct access to fresh, nutritious food (especially
vegetables) for city dwellers, improving family diets.
2. Income Generation: Creates jobs and business opportunities (selling fresh produce,
seedlings, or compost).
3. Environmental Improvement:
o "Greens" the city, improving air quality and reducing the "urban heat island"
effect.
24
o Reduces "Food Miles" (the distance food travels from farm to plate), saving
energy and reducing carbon emissions.
o Waste Recycling: Can utilize composted urban organic waste.
4. Social & Educational: Community gardens bring people together. School gardens are
powerful teaching tools.
8.3 PRACTICES AND SYSTEMS FOR URBAN HORTICULTURE
Since space is the biggest limitation, these methods focus on growing in small, intensive spaces.
1. Container Gardening: Growing plants in any available container: plastic pots, buckets,
"Grow Bags," old car tires, wooden boxes.
2. Sack (Bag) Gardening: Filling large woven sacks (like those for rice or garri) with soil
and compost. Plants (like leafy greens) are planted on the top and in holes cut in the sides.
Very space-efficient.
3. Vertical Gardening: Growing plants upwards to save space.
o Using trellises for climbing plants (beans, cucumbers).
o Using "stackable" pots or "living wall" systems.
4. Rooftop Gardens: Using flat roofs as growing spaces. This requires:
o Ensuring the roof is strong enough to hold the weight of wet soil.
o Waterproofing and good drainage to protect the building.
5. Community Gardens: A single, larger piece of land (often on "borrowed" land) that is
divided into small plots for different families to farm.
8.4 HYDROPONICS: SOIL-LESS HORTICULTURE
Hydroponics is an advanced form of urban horticulture that does not use soil.
 Definition: A method of growing plants in a "nutrient solution" (water mixed with all the
essential plant nutrients) or in an inert growing medium (like gravel, perlite, or coconut
coir).
 Common Systems:
25
o Nutrient Film Technique (NFT): A thin "film" of nutrient water flows
continuously in a channel over the bare plant roots. (Common for lettuce).
o Deep Water Culture (DWC): Plant roots are suspended directly in a large,
aerated (bubbling) tank of nutrient solution.
 Pros (Advantages):
o No Soil Needed: Can be done anywhere (a roof, a warehouse, a balcony).
o Extremely Fast Growth: Plants get exactly the nutrients they need, 24/7.
o Very High Yield: Produces more food in less space.
o Water Efficient: The water is recycled; uses 70-90% less water than soil farming.
 Cons (Disadvantages):
o High Initial Cost: Need for pumps, tanks, plumbing, and nutrient salts.
o Technical Skill: Requires knowledge of chemistry to mix the nutrient solution.
o Reliant on Electricity: The pumps and aerators must run 24/7. A power cut can
kill the entire crop.

WEEK 9: AGROFORESTRY
9.1 MEANING AND DEFINITION
Agroforestry is a sustainable land management system that deliberately combines woody
perennials (trees, shrubs) with agricultural crops and/or livestock, either at the same time or in
sequence, on the same piece of land.
It is the opposite of "monoculture" (planting only one crop) and is a more integrated and
"natural" way of farming. A traditional Nigerian compound farm (with fruit trees, yams,
vegetables, and chickens) is a classic example of agroforestry.
9.2 AGROFORESTRY PRACTICES (SYSTEMS)
1. Alley Cropping (Hedgerow Intercropping):
o What: Planting food crops (like maize, cassava, or vegetables) in the "alleys" or
"lanes" between rows of fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing trees or shrubs (like
Leucaena, Gliricidia, or Faidherbia albida).
27
o How it works: The trees are pruned regularly, and the leaves (which are rich in
nitrogen) are used as mulch and "green manure" for the crops in the alleys.
2. Multi-storey Cropping (Home Gardens):
o What: A system that copies a natural forest, with different "layers" of crops.
o Example:
 Top Layer (Tall trees): Coconut, Oil Palm, Mango, Ogbono.
 Middle Layer (Medium trees/shrubs): Pawpaw, Banana, Plantain,
Cocoa, Coffee.
 Low Layer (Food crops): Cassava, Yams (with trellises), Cocoyam,
Peppers.
 Ground Cover (On the soil): Pumpkin, Melon, Sweet Potato.
3. Silvopasture:
o What: Combining trees with pasture (grass) and livestock (animals).
o How it works: Animals (goats, sheep, cattle) graze on the grass growing under
the trees. The trees provide shade for the animals and can also be a source of
fodder (food).
4. Windbreaks / Shelterbelts:
o What: One or more rows of trees and shrubs planted around the border of a farm
or field.
o Purpose: To protect crops, soil, and animals from strong, damaging winds. They
also prevent wind erosion.
9.3 IMPORTANCE AND BENEFITS OF AGROFORESTRY
1. Improved Soil Fertility:
o Leguminous trees (like Gliricidia) "fix" nitrogen from the air.
o Tree leaf litter (mulch) decomposes, adding rich organic matter to the soil.
o Tree roots can "pump" nutrients from deep in the soil back up to the surface.
2. Soil and Water Conservation:
o Tree roots bind the soil, preventing water and wind erosion.
o The "canopy" (tree leaves) slows down heavy rain, so it "soaks in" rather than
"runs off."
28
o Mulch on the soil surface reduces water evaporation.
3. Diversified Income (Economic Security):
o The farmer is not reliant on one crop.
o They get short-term income (food crops), medium-term income (fruit), and longterm
income (timber, firewood). This spreads the risk.
4. Improved Microclimate:
o The tree canopy provides shade, which lowers soil temperature and reduces heat
stress on crops and animals.
5. Environmental Benefits:
o Increases biodiversity (provides a habitat for birds and "good" insects).
o Sequesters carbon (trees absorb CO2), helping to fight climate change.

WEEK 10: ENVIRONMENTAL SUSTAINABILITY IN HORTICULTURE
10.1 INTRODUCTION
Sustainable horticulture means growing food in a way that is:
1. Ecologically Sound: Does not harm the environment (soil, water, air).
2. Economically Viable: Farmers can make a good living.
3. Socially Just: Good for families and communities.
The goal is to meet our present need for food without compromising the ability of future
generations to meet their own needs. This topic ties together everything we have learned this
term (organic farming, agroforestry, water management).
10.2 KEY PILLARS OF ENVIRONMENTAL SUSTAINABILITY
A. SOIL CONSERVATION AND HEALTH
 The Problem: Soil erosion, loss of organic matter, and "dead" soil.
 Sustainable Practices:
30
1. Conservation Tillage (Zero/Minimum Tillage): A method of planting without
ploughing or tilling the soil. A new crop is planted directly into the "stubble"
(residue) of the previous crop. This protects the soil from erosion, saves water,
and builds organic matter.
2. Cover Cropping & Mulching: (From Week 4 & 9) The number one rule of soil
conservation: Never leave the soil bare. A cover crop (like Mucuna) or a layer of
mulch (like straw) acts as "armour" for the soil, protecting it from sun, wind, and
rain.
3. Contour Ploughing / Terracing: On sloping land, all farming activities
(ploughing, planting) must be done across the slope (on the contour), not up and
down it. This creates small ridges that trap water and prevent it from "rushing"
downhill and causing erosion.
B. WATER MANAGEMENT AND CONSERVATION
 The Problem: Water is scarce, rainfall is unreliable, and traditional irrigation is wasteful.
 Sustainable Practices:
1. Drip Irrigation: (From Week 6) The most efficient method. Delivers water in
slow "drips" directly to the root zone of each plant. This eliminates evaporation
and runoff, saving 70-90% of water.
2. Rainwater Harvesting: Collecting and storing rainwater.
 Small-scale: From roofs into plastic tanks.
 Large-scale: Building small farm ponds or "earth dams" to capture and
store runoff water for use in the dry season.
3. Mulching: (As above) A layer of mulch is also one of the best water conservation
techniques, as it dramatically reduces water loss from evaporation at the soil
surface.
C. INTEGRATED PEST MANAGEMENT (IPM)
 The Problem: Over-reliance on chemical pesticides kills beneficial insects, pollutes
water, and creates "resistant" pests.
31
 IPM is a "middle-ground" approach: It's a pyramid strategy.
1. Base (Foundation): Prevention: Use cultural methods first (crop rotation,
resistant varieties, farm sanitation).
2. Middle: Biological/Physical: Use biological controls (beneficial insects) and
physical controls (traps, hand-picking).
3. Top (Last Resort): Chemicals: Use chemical pesticides only when the pest level
is high enough to cause economic damage, and use the least-toxic, most-specific
chemical available.
D. ENHANCING BIODIVERSITY
 The Problem: Monoculture (planting a single crop) is an "invitation" to pests and
diseases.
 Sustainable Practices:
o Polyculture / Intercropping: Planting many different crops together.
o Agroforestry: (From Week 9) Integrating trees creates habitats.
o Planting Flower Borders: Attracts pollinators (bees) and beneficial insects (pest
predators).

WEEK 1: CROP ROTATION
1.1 INTRODUCTION
Crop rotation is a fundamental and ancient agricultural practice. It is a systematic, planned
sequence of growing different types of crops (from different plant families) on the same piece of
land over a period of years. It is the opposite of monocropping (growing the same crop on the
same land year after year), which is a practice that can degrade soil and increase pest problems.
1.2 PRINCIPLES OF CROP ROTATION
The success of crop rotation relies on several key agronomic principles. The plan is designed to
ensure that different crops, with their unique characteristics, contribute to the soil and the farm
ecosystem in a balanced way.
1. Nutrient Management (Legumes vs. Feeders):
35
o Heavy Feeders: Some crops, like maize and tomatoes, are "heavy feeders" that
consume large amounts of soil nutrients, especially nitrogen.
o Nitrogen-Fixers (Legumes): Crops from the legume family (e.g., cowpea,
groundnuts, soybeans, Mucuna) have a symbiotic relationship with bacteria
(Rhizobium) in their root nodules. These bacteria "fix" atmospheric nitrogen (N₂)
into a form (ammonia) that plants can use.
o The Principle: By planting a legume crop after a heavy feeder, the farmer
naturally replenishes the soil's nitrogen supply, reducing the need for expensive
synthetic fertilizers.
2. Pest and Disease Cycle Disruption:
o Many soil-borne pests (like nematodes) and diseases (like fungal wilts) are cropspecific.
They build up in the soil when their preferred host crop is planted
repeatedly.
o The Principle: When you plant a non-host crop, the pest or pathogen "starves" or
its life cycle is broken. For example, the nematodes that attack yams will not
survive on a crop of maize. By rotating, the pest population crashes, ensuring a
healthier field for the next time the susceptible crop is planted.
3. Varying Rooting Depths (Soil Structure):
o Shallow-rooted crops (e.g., onions, lettuce) primarily use the top layer of soil.
o Deep-rooted crops (e.g., cassava, sunflowers, some cover crops) can break
through compacted soil layers ("hardpan") and draw nutrients and water from
deep within the soil profile.
o The Principle: Rotating between deep and shallow-rooted crops improves soil
aeration, water infiltration, and overall soil structure. The deep-rooted crops also
"recycle" nutrients by bringing them up to the surface, where they become
available to the next crop after the plant decomposes.
4. Weed Control:
o Different crops compete with weeds differently.
o Smother Crops: Some crops (like cassava, sweet potatoes, or Mucuna) develop a
dense canopy that "chokes" or "smothers" weeds by blocking sunlight.
o Poor Competitors: Other crops (like onions or carrots) are easily overgrown.
36
o The Principle: Rotating a "smother crop" into the sequence can naturally reduce
the "weed seed bank" in the soil, making it easier to manage weeds in the next,
more sensitive crop.
1.3 ADVANTAGES OF CROP ROTATION
1. Improved Soil Fertility & Health: Balances nutrient levels, increases soil organic
matter (when cover crops are used), and promotes a healthy soil microbiome.
2. Reduced Chemical Use: Decreases the need for synthetic fertilizers (due to legumes)
and pesticides/nematicides (due to pest cycle disruption). This saves the farmer money
and protects the environment.
3. Increased Crop Yields: Healthier, more fertile soil leads to higher yields over the long
term.
4. Risk Diversification: By planting different crops, the farmer is less vulnerable. If market
prices for one crop crash, or a specific pest attacks, they still have other crops to rely on
for income.
5. Better Soil Structure: Improves water holding capacity and aeration, and reduces soil
erosion.
1.4 EXAMPLES OF A 4-YEAR CROP ROTATION PLAN
A good rotation plan will typically include a crop from each of these categories:
 A - Fruit/Leaf: (e.g., Maize, Tomato, Okra) - Heavy Feeder
 B - Legume: (e.g., Cowpea, Groundnut, Soybean) - Nitrogen Fixer
 C - Root/Tuber: (e.g., Yam, Cassava, Potato) - Heavy Potash (K) Feeder
 D - Fallow/Cover Crop: (e.g., Mucuna, Pueraria) - Soil Builder
Example Plan for a Single Plot of Land:
 Year 1: Maize (Heavy nitrogen feeder)
 Year 2: Cowpea (Replenishes nitrogen)
37
 Year 3: Yams (Use the high organic matter and residual nitrogen; followed by cassava,
which is a deep-rooted, heavy potash feeder)
 Year 4: Fallow with Mucuna (To rest the soil, add massive organic matter, and control
weeds)

WEEK 2: MIXED CROPPING AND INTERCROPPING
2.1 INTRODUCTION
These are traditional farming systems, common throughout Nigeria and West Africa, where
multiple crops are grown on the same piece of land at the same time. This is a form of
polyculture and contrasts sharply with the "monoculture" of large-scale commercial farming.
2.2 DEFINITIONS
 Mixed Cropping (or Mixed-Intercropping):
o Definition: The practice of growing two or more crops simultaneously on the
same field, with no distinct row arrangement.
o Example: A traditional farm where maize, cassava, and pumpkin seeds are all
planted, scattered or mixed together in the same space. It appears random.
 Intercropping (or Strip-Cropping/Row Intercropping):
o Definition: The practice of growing two or more crops simultaneously on the
same field in a specific, patterned arrangement. This is a more systematic and
modern approach.
39
o Examples:
 Row Intercropping: One row of maize, followed by one row of cowpea,
followed by one row of maize.
 Strip Intercropping: A 1-meter "strip" of maize, followed by a 1-meter
"strip" of soybeans.
2.3 BENEFITS OF MIXED/INTERCROPPING
1. Risk Diversification (Main Benefit): This is the farmer's "insurance policy." If one crop
fails due to drought, pests, or low prices, the other crops may survive and provide food
and income. It is the principle of "not putting all your eggs in one basket."
2. Higher Total Yield (Land Equivalent Ratio - LER): LER is a measure of efficiency.
An LER greater than 1.0 means the intercrop is more productive than growing the crops
separately. For example, if you get 0.7-ha-worth of maize and 0.5-ha-worth of cowpea
from a 1-ha intercrop, your LER is 1.2. This happens because the crops use resources
(light, water, nutrients) more efficiently together.
3. Pest and Disease Suppression:
o Pest Confusion: Pests that find their host plant by smell are "confused" by the
other crop's smell.
o Trap Cropping: One crop (the "trap crop") can be planted to attract pests away
from the main, more valuable crop.
o Physical Barrier: Tall crops (like maize) can act as a barrier, preventing flying
pests from reaching the shorter crop (like beans).
4. Weed Suppression: Planting low-growing, spreading crops (like melon, pumpkin, or
sweet potato) as a "ground cover" under a tall crop (like maize or cassava) smothers
weeds by blocking sunlight.
5. Improved Soil Fertility: When a legume (like cowpea) is intercropped with a cereal
(like maize), the legume provides some nitrogen to the cereal.
6. Better Use of Resources: Different crops have different needs:
o Light: A tall, sun-loving crop (maize) can grow alongside a short, shade-tolerant
crop (cocoyam).
40
o Roots: A deep-rooted crop (cassava) can grow with a shallow-rooted crop
(onions), using different soil layers.
2.4 LIMITATIONS (CHALLENGES)
1. Mechanization is Difficult or Impossible: This is the biggest disadvantage. You cannot
use tractors, planters, or combine harvesters on a mixed-crop field. This limits the farm
size and relies heavily on manual labour.
2. Interspecific Competition: If the crops are not chosen carefully, they will compete with
each other for light, water, and nutrients. This can lead to lower yields for both crops.
3. Complex Management: All operations (planting, fertilizing, weeding, harvesting) are
more complicated. The crops may have different planting times, nutrient needs, and
harvesting times.
4. Pest Control Complexity: A pesticide that is safe for one crop might kill the other (e.g.,
a herbicide for maize might kill the intercropped beans).

WEEK 3: CROP YIELD MEASUREMENT
3.1 INTRODUCTION
"Yield" is the measure of agricultural output (the harvested product) per unit area of land. It is
the most common way to measure farm productivity and efficiency.
 Definition: Yield is the mass, volume, or count of a crop produced from a defined unit of
land.
 Standard Unit: The standard international unit for yield is kilograms per hectare
(kg/ha) or tonnes per hectare (t/ha).
3.2 IMPORTANCE OF MEASURING YIELD
Why bother measuring yield accurately?
1. Economic Performance: To calculate profit and loss. A farmer must know their yield
(income) to compare against their costs (inputs).
2. Decision Making & Comparison:
42
o Variety Trials: To know which variety of maize (e.g., 'A' vs. 'B') actually
produces more.
o Treatment Effects: To scientifically test if a new fertilizer or a new irrigation
method actually increased production.
o Farm Management: To identify which fields are high-performing and which are
low-performing.
3. Planning and Forecasting:
o Farmer: To know how much storage to prepare and how much income to expect.
o Government/NGO: To forecast national food production, predict potential
famines, and manage food imports/exports.
3.3 METHODS OF CROP YIELD MEASUREMENT
1. FULL FIELD MEASUREMENT (CENSUS METHOD)
 Process: The farmer harvests the entire field. The entire harvest is collected, cleaned, and
weighed. The total weight is then divided by the total area of the field.
 Example: A farmer harvests a 2-hectare field and gets a total of 8,000 kg of dry maize.
o Yield = 8,000 kg / 2 ha = 4,000 kg/ha (or 4 t/ha).
 Pros: 100% accurate.
 Cons: Only practical for the farmer after harvest; not useful for researchers who need to
sample many plots; time-consuming.
2. SAMPLING METHOD (QUADRANT ANALYSIS)
This is the standard scientific and estimation method used by researchers, agricultural officers,
and assessors.
 Process:
1. Define the Sampling Unit: A small, fixed-size frame (a "quadrant") is used. A
common size is 1 meter x 1 meter (1m²).
43
2. Randomization: The quadrant is thrown or placed randomly at multiple locations
in the field. This is critical to avoid bias (e.g., not just picking the "best-looking"
spots).
3. Harvest and Weigh: The crop inside the quadrant is carefully harvested, and its
weight is recorded.
4. Repeat: This is done multiple times (e.g., 10-20 times) across the field to get a
reliable average.
5. Calculate the Average: All the weights are added up and divided by the number
of samples.
 e.g., 10 samples (throws) produce a total of 6 kg.
 Average = 6 kg / 10 samples = 0.6 kg per 1m² (the average yield).
 Extrapolation (The Calculation):
o Step 1: You know the average yield per 1m² (e.g., 0.6 kg/m²).
o Step 2: You know the number of square meters in one hectare: 1 ha = 10,000 m².
o Step 3: Multiply the average yield by the area.
 Estimated Yield = 0.6 kg/m² × 10,000 m² = 6,000 kg/ha (or 6 t/ha).
3.4 IMPORTANT CONSIDERATIONS
1. Moisture Content: The yield of grain (like maize) must be corrected to a standard
moisture content (e.g., 12.5% or 15%). A farmer harvesting wet maize (25% moisture)
will have a higher weight, but it's not the "true" dry yield.
2. Edge Effects: The outer rows of a field often behave differently (more sun, more wind).
Samples should avoid the very edge of the field.
3. Harvestable Yield vs. Biological Yield: "Biological yield" is the total plant mass.
"Harvestable (or Economic) yield" is only the part that is sold (e.g., the grain, not the
stalk).

WEEK 4: FARM MECHANIZATION IN CROP PRODUCTION
4.1 INTRODUCTION
Farm mechanization is the process of using agricultural machinery to replace human and animal
(draught) labour in farming operations. It ranges from simple hand tools to advanced GPSguided
combine harvesters. It is a key component of modernizing agriculture.
4.2 LEVELS OF MECHANIZATION
1. Level 1: Hand-Tool Technology (Manual):
o The farmer uses simple tools (hoe, cutlass, sickle, digging stick).
o Pros: Low cost, no maintenance, suitable for any land size.
o Cons: Extremely slow, high drudgery (back-breaking work), limits farm size to 1-
2 ha (subsistence).
2. Level 2: Animal-Draught Technology:
o Uses trained animals (e.g., oxen, donkeys) to pull simple implements (plough,
ridger, cart).
o Pros: Faster than manual, can cultivate larger areas (5-10 ha), provides manure.
46
o Cons: Animals get sick, need food/water, requires training.
3. Level 3: Engine-Powered Technology (Motorized):
o Uses engines (petrol or diesel) to power machinery.
o Examples: Tractors, combine harvesters, water pumps, motorized tillers, maize
shellers.
4.3 SIMPLE MACHINES & EQUIPMENT (The focus for smallholders)
While tractors are a symbol of mechanization, intermediate mechanization is more practical for
many farmers.
 Land Preparation: Hand-held tillers, small 2-wheel tractors (power tillers).
 Planting: Hand-push planters, simple seed drills.
 Weed Control: Motorized brush cutters.
 Harvesting: Small-scale rice reapers.
 Post-Harvest Processing (Crucial):
o Cassava Graters: Motorized graters have revolutionized garri production.
o Rice Threshers/Mills: Separate grain from stalk and husk.
o Maize Shellers: Quickly remove kernels from the cob.
o Oil Palm Presses: Extract palm oil much more efficiently than by hand.
4.4 ADVANTAGES OF MECHANIZATION
1. Timeliness of Operations: This is the most important advantage. In rain-fed agriculture,
a farmer has a very small "window" to plant. A tractor can plough 5 hectares in one day;
a man with a hoe would take weeks.
2. Reduces Drudgery: Frees farmers from the hardest, most boring, and back-breaking
physical labour.
3. Increases Farm Size (Scalability): Mechanization allows a single farmer to cultivate a
much larger area of land, moving from subsistence to commercial farming.
4. Improved Efficiency and Quality:
o Planting: A planter places seeds at a uniform depth and spacing, leading to better
germination.
47
o Harvesting: A combine harvester reduces grain loss.
o Processing: A cassava grater or oil press extracts more product with less waste.
4.5 LIMITATIONS AND CHALLENGES IN NIGERIA
1. High Initial Cost: This is the #1 barrier. Tractors and implements are extremely
expensive, far beyond the reach of most small-scale farmers.
2. Lack of Maintenance & Spare Parts: A tractor is useless if it's broken. There is a
severe shortage of trained mechanics and affordable, genuine spare parts in rural areas.
3. Land Tenure & Fragmentation: Most farmers have small (0.5-2 ha) plots that are
scattered in different locations. This "land fragmentation" is highly unsuitable for large
machinery.
4. Soil Compaction: Heavy tractors, especially in wet soil, can compact the earth, creating
a "hardpan" that roots cannot penetrate and water cannot infiltrate.
5. Job Displacement: Mechanization can lead to rural unemployment, as one machine can
do the work of 50 labourers.
6. Lack of Technical Know-How: Farmers need to be trained to operate and maintain the
machinery correctly.

WEEK 6: IRRIGATION SYSTEMS
6.1 INTRODUCTION
Irrigation is the artificial application of water to land to supplement rainfall. It is essential for:
 Dry Season Farming: Growing crops year-round, not just in the rainy season.
 Drought Mitigation: Saving a crop during an unexpected "dry spell."
 Arid/Semi-Arid Regions: Making agriculture possible in dry areas.
6.2 TRADITIONAL IRRIGATION (THE PROBLEM)
 Surface / Flood Irrigation: This involves "flooding" the entire field or running water
down "furrows" (channels) between crop rows.
 Pros: Very cheap to set up, low-tech, requires no special equipment.
 Cons: Extremely wasteful. Up to 60% of the water is lost to evaporation, deep
percolation (sinking too deep), and runoff. It also promotes weed growth and can lead to
soil salinization.
6.3 MODERN METHODS (WATER CONSERVATION)
50
Modern systems focus on "Water Use Efficiency" (WUE) – getting "more crop per drop."
1. SPRINKLER IRRIGATION
 How it Works: Water is pumped through pipes and sprayed into the air through
"sprinkler heads" (e.g., impact sprinklers, pop-up sprinklers, or large "rain guns"). It falls
on the crops like artificial rain.
 Pros:
o Much more efficient (approx. 75-85%) than flood irrigation.
o Suitable for most crops and soil types.
o Can cover large areas.
 Cons:
o High initial cost (pumps, pipes, sprinklers).
o High energy cost to pressurize the water.
o Water can be lost to wind drift and evaporation before it even hits the ground.
o Wets the leaves, which can increase fungal diseases.
 Types: Center-Pivot (giant, moving arms for massive fields), Rain Guns (for large, open
fields).
51
2. DRIP IRRIGATION (MICRO-IRRIGATION)
 How it Works: Water is delivered very slowly, in drops or a "micro-stream," directly to
the soil at the base (root zone) of each individual plant. It uses a network of plastic pipes
("drip lines") and small nozzles called "emitters."
 Pros:
o Highest Efficiency: The most water-efficient method (90-98%).
o Water Conservation: Virtually no water is lost to evaporation or runoff.
o Weed Control: The "alleys" between rows stay dry, which suppresses weed
growth.
o Fertigation: Allows for "fertigation" – the application of liquid fertilizer through
the irrigation water, delivering nutrients directly to the roots.
o Reduces fungal diseases (as leaves stay dry).
 Cons:
o High Initial Cost: Very expensive to install (pipes, emitters, filters).
o Clogging: The tiny emitters can easily get clogged by sand, dirt, or algae.
Requires filters and maintenance.
52
o Not for all crops: Best for high-value, row crops (vegetables, orchards,
greenhouses), not for broadcast crops like wheat.

WEEK 8: STORAGE STRUCTURES
8.1 INTRODUCTION
Post-harvest losses are a massive problem in Nigeria. It is estimated that 30-50% of food
(especially perishables) is lost after it is harvested, before it reaches a consumer. Proper storage
is the key to preventing this loss.
Importance of Storage:
1. Prevent Post-Harvest Losses: Protects crops from spoilage, pests (weevils, rats), and
moisture.
2. Ensure Food Security: Allows food to be available during the "lean season" (off-season).
3. Price Stabilization (Economic): Allows a farmer to store their harvest when prices are
low (at harvest time) and sell it months later when prices are high.
8.2 TYPES OF STORAGE STRUCTURES
1. FOR DRY GRAINS (MAIZE, RICE, COWPEA, SOYBEANS)
55
 Traditional (Low-tech):
o Rhombus: A traditional mud-walled, thatched-roof structure, often raised on
stilts. Problem: Prone to rodents and insects.
o Bag Storage: Storing in woven polypropylene (WPP) or jute bags, stacked on
wooden "pallets" (not on the bare floor) in a ventilated room.
 Modern (Improved):
o Silos: Large, cylindrical towers made of metal or concrete. Used for bulk
(unbagged) storage of grain on a large commercial scale (e.t., by big farms or
food processing companies like Flour Mills). They are airtight and protect from
all pests and weather.
o Hermetic Storage Bags (e.g., PICS Bags): A "low-tech, high-impact" modern
solution. It's a "bag-within-a-bag" system. An inner plastic liner is made of a
special material that is airtight. After dry grain is put in and the bag is sealed, the
insects (weevils) inside use up the oxygen, suffocate themselves, and die.
2. FOR TUBERS (YAMS, POTATOES)
 Traditional:
o Yam Barn: A traditional open-air structure with vertical wooden poles and crosspieces.
Yams are tied individually to the frame. It is designed for maximum
ventilation to prevent rot and allow farmers to monitor the yams.
o Sand Boxes: Storing root crops (like carrots) in boxes of cool, slightly damp sand.
 Modern:
o Ventilated Sheds: Modern "barns" made of wood/metal, with slatted shelves to
store tubers in a cool, dark, and well-ventilated space.
3. FOR PERISHABLES (TOMATOES, PEPPERS, FRUITS, VEGETABLES)
 The Problem: These have high water content and "breathe" (respire) fast, so they spoil
very quickly. They need cold storage.
 Refrigerators: For small-scale, short-term storage (e.g., retailers, homes).
 Cold Rooms (Walk-in Coolers):
56
o Definition: A large, insulated room or container with a refrigeration unit. It is a
walk-in refrigerator.
o Function: They are the backbone of the modern food supply chain. They slow
down respiration, inhibit microbial growth, and can extend the shelf life of
tomatoes from 3 days to 21 days.
 Evaporative Coolers: A low-cost, "off-grid" alternative. A simple frame covered with
charcoal or jute bags, which are kept wet. As water evaporates, it draws heat from the air
inside, making the "cooler" 10-15°C colder than the outside air.

WEEK 9: AGRICULTURAL EXTENSION SERVICES
9.1 INTRODUCTION
There is a large "knowledge gap" in agriculture. Researchers at universities (e.g., OAU, ABU)
and institutes (e.g., IITA, NCRI) develop new technologies (new seeds, new methods, new pest
controls). However, most farmers, especially in rural areas, do not have access to this
information.
 Definition: An Agricultural Extension Service is a system that "bridges" this
knowledge gap. The Extension Agent (or Officer) is the link, the messenger, and the
teacher who takes knowledge from the researcher and extends it to the farmer. It is also
a two-way street, as they take problems from the farmer back to the researcher.
9.2 ROLES OF AGRICULTURAL EXTENSION
The role of the extension agent is multi-faceted:
58
1. Education and Training: This is the primary role. They teach farmers modern and
appropriate farming practices (e.g., how to use fertilizer, correct crop spacing, pest
identification, crop rotation).
2. Technology Transfer: They are responsible for introducing new technologies to farmers,
such as:
o Improved seed varieties (e.g., disease-resistant cassava).
o Simple machines (e.g., maize shellers).
o New methods (e.g., drip irrigation).
3. Facilitation and Farmer Organization: They help farmers form groups, such as the
cooperatives (see Week 10). This helps farmers get a collective voice, access loans, and
buy inputs in bulk.
4. Feedback Channel: The extension agent listens to the farmers' problems (e.g., "A new
insect is destroying our maize," "The new cassava variety yields well, but the garri does
not taste good"). They then report this feedback to the researchers, who can then try to
solve the real-world problem.
5. Advisory Services: Acting as a "farm doctor," visiting farms, diagnosing problems (pests,
diseases, nutrient deficiencies), and recommending solutions.
9.3 EXTENSION TEACHING METHODS
 Individual Methods:
o Farm & Home Visits: Visiting a farmer directly. Very effective, but slow (can
only see a few farmers a day).
 Group Methods:
o Demonstration Plots (Method & Result): This is the most effective method.
 The agent sets up two small plots side-by-side.
 Plot A (Farmer's Practice): Uses the traditional method.
 Plot B (Recommended Practice): Uses the new seed/fertilizer.
 At the end of the season, all the farmers see the result. "Seeing is
believing."
o Farmers' Field Days: A large meeting at the demonstration plot to discuss the
results.
59
 Mass Media Methods:
o Radio: The most important mass-media tool in rural Africa. Farmers listen to
agricultural programs in their local language.
o Posters, Leaflets, and (increasingly) Mobile Phones (SMS/Video).
9.4 IMPORTANCE OF EXTENSION
 Improves farm productivity and farmer income.
 Increases national food security.
 Accelerates the adoption of new, helpful technologies.
 Connects researchers to the real-world problems of farmers.
 Empowers rural communities.

WEEK 10: AGRICULTURAL COOPERATIVES
10.1 INTRODUCTION
The Core Problem: A single, small-scale farmer has no bargaining power.
 When they buy inputs (fertilizer, seeds), they buy in small quantities at a high retail price.
 When they sell produce (maize, cocoa), they sell small quantities to a "middleman" at a
low farm-gate price.
Definition: An Agricultural Cooperative Society is a business organization that is voluntarily
owned, controlled, and operated by its members (the farmers) for their mutual social and
economic benefit. It is a "self-help" group.
10.2 FUNCTIONS (TYPES) OF COOPERATIVES
Cooperatives solve the "core problem" by using the power of bulk buying and selling.
1. Marketing Cooperatives:
61
o Function: Farmers pool or bulk their produce together. Instead of 100 farmers
selling 1 bag of cocoa each, the cooperative sells 100 bags (or more) directly to a
large buyer or exporter.
o Benefit: They cut out the middleman and negotiate a much higher price for all
their members.
2. Purchasing (Input Supply) Cooperatives:
o Function: The cooperative buys fertilizer, seeds, and chemicals in bulk
(wholesale) directly from the manufacturer.
o Benefit: It then sells these inputs to its members at a low price, much cheaper
than the local retail shop.
3. Credit Cooperatives (Savings & Loans):
o Function: Members contribute regular savings (e.g., ₦500 per week). This pool
of money ("capital") is then given out as small, low-interest loans to members.
o Benefit: Provides farmers with access to credit to buy inputs or hire labour, which
traditional banks will not provide.
4. Service Cooperatives:
o Function: The cooperative jointly owns or hires a large piece of equipment that
no single farmer could afford.
o Benefit: Provides members with access to services like a tractor, a storage
warehouse (silo), or a processing machine (e.g., a "Cooperative Rice Mill").
10.3 ADVANTAGES OF COOPERATIVES
1. Economic: Higher profits for farmers (sell high, buy low).
2. Bargaining Power: Gives farmers a "collective voice" in the market and in dealing with
the government.
3. Access to Credit & Services: Provides access to loans, inputs, and machinery.
4. Empowerment: Trains farmers in leadership, business management, and record keeping.
5. Risk Reduction: Helps members manage risks and supports them in times of need.
10.4 CHALLENGES OF COOPERATIVES IN NIGERIA
62
1. Mismanagement and Corruption: This is the #1 cause of failure. Poor leadership or
outright theft of funds destroys the cooperative.
2. Low Member Participation: If members do not attend meetings or feel involved, they
lose trust and the cooperative weakens.
3. Lack of Capital: Many cooperatives struggle to raise enough capital to buy a tractor or
build a warehouse.
4. Poor Loan Repayment: Members taking loans and defaulting (refusing to pay back) can
bankrupt a credit cooperative.

SS2 HORTICULTURE AND CROP PRODUCTION LESSON NOTES
THIRD TERM
WEEK 1: CEREAL CROPS
1.1 INTRODUCTION TO CEREALS
Cereal crops (or grains) are plants from the grass family (Poaceae) cultivated for their edible
starchy seeds (grains). They are the most important source of carbohydrates and staple food for
the majority of the world's population. In Nigeria, they are critical for food security.
1.2 MAIZE (Zea mays)
 Importance: A primary staple food (eaten as 'agbado', 'oka', pap, or 'eko'), a key
component of animal feed (especially for poultry), and an industrial raw material (for
flour, corn starch, oil).
 Ecology:
65
o Climate: Requires warm, sunny weather. Needs adequate rainfall (600-1500mm)
spread throughout the growing season, especially during the "tasseling"
(flowering) and grain-filling stages. It needs a dry period for harvest and drying.
o Soil: Prefers deep, well-drained, fertile loamy soils with a high organic matter
content. pH 6.0-7.0.
 Cultivation Practices:
o Land Preparation: Clear land, plough, and harrow to create a fine tilth. Ridges
or beds can be made.
o Planting: Planted by seed. In Nigeria, there are two seasons: Early (March-April)
and Late (August-September).
o Spacing: 75-90cm between rows, 25-30cm within rows. Plant 2-3 seeds per hole,
3-5cm deep.
o Fertilizer: Maize is a heavy feeder, especially of Nitrogen. A split application is
best:
1. At Planting: Apply NPK 15:15:15 or 20:10:10 (basal application).
2. 4-6 Weeks After Planting: Top-dress with Urea (Nitrogen) to boost
vegetative growth.
o Weed Control: This is critical in the first 6 weeks. Weeds severely reduce yield.
Can be done by hoeing or with pre-emergence (e.g., Atrazine) and postemergence
herbicides.
 Pests & Diseases:
o Pests: Stem Borers (larvae tunnel into the stem), Armyworms (especially the Fall
Armyworm, which devastates crops), Weevils (in storage).
o Diseases: Maize Streak Virus, Rust, Downy Mildew.
 Harvesting: 90-120 days after planting. Matures when the husk turns brown and dry, and
the "black layer" forms at the kernel base.
 Post-Harvest: Harvest cobs, de-husk, dry (to 12-14% moisture), shell (remove kernels),
and store (in bags, PICS bags, or silos) to protect from weevils.
1.3 RICE (Oryza sativa)
 Importance: A major staple food in Nigeria, with enormous (and growing) demand.
66
 Ecology (Two Main Types):
1. Lowland/Swamp/Paddy Rice: Grown in flooded fields ("paddies") or river
valleys (fadamas). Requires bunds (walls) to hold water.
2. Upland Rice: Grown on rain-fed land, just like maize or sorghum.
 Cultivation Practices:
o Land Preparation:
 Lowland: Plough, harrow, and then "puddle" the soil (mix soil and water)
to create a soft, level, water-holding mud.
 Upland: Plough and harrow to a fine tilth.
o Planting:
1. Direct Seeding: Broadcasting or drilling seeds directly into the field.
2. Transplanting: (Common for lowland) Seeds are first grown in a "nursery" for 21-30
days. The seedlings are then uprooted and transplanted manually into the puddled field.
o Water Management: This is the key. Lowland rice needs 5-10cm of standing
water for most of its growth. Upland rice relies entirely on rainfall.
o Weed Control: The major problem, especially in upland rice. Flooding in
lowland rice naturally suppresses many weeds.
 Pests & Diseases:
o Pests: Stem Borers, Birds (a major pest that eats ripening grain), Termites.
o Diseases: Rice Blast, Rice Yellow Mottle Virus (RYMV).
 Harvesting: 100-150 days. Harvest when 80-90% of the panicles (heads) are goldenyellow
and drooping. Cut with sickles.
 Post-Harvest: Thresh (beat to separate grain from stalk), Dry (on tarps or floors),
Winnow (remove chaff), Parboil (a key process in Nigeria: soak, steam, and re-dry to
improve milling and nutrition), Mill (remove husk and bran), De-stone.
1.4 SORGHUM (Sorghum bicolor) & MILLET (Pennisetum glaucum)
 Importance: The primary staple food crops of the arid and semi-arid Northern zones
(Sudan and Sahel savanna). They are vital for food security in dry regions.
67
 Ecology: Their defining feature is drought tolerance.
o Climate: Thrive in high temperatures and low rainfall (300-600mm) where maize
and rice would fail.
o Soil: Can grow on poorer, sandier soils.
 Cultivation: Similar land preparation to maize, but planted on ridges. Often intercropped
with cowpea.
 Pests & Diseases:
o Pests: Stem Borers, Birds (Quelea birds), which fly in massive flocks and can
destroy a whole field.
o Diseases: Smut (replaces grain with a black powder), Downy Mildew.
 Harvesting: Cut the seed heads (panicles), dry them, and thresh.

WEEK 2: LEGUMINOUS CROPS
2.1 INTRODUCTION TO LEGUMES
Legumes (from the Fabaceae family) are crops grown for their edible seeds ("pulses") or pods.
 Key Characteristic: Their ability to fix nitrogen. They have nodules on their roots
containing Rhizobium bacteria, which convert atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) into a usable
form.
 Importance:
1. Protein: They are the "poor man's meat," a critical source of plant-based protein.
2. Soil Fertility: They naturally enrich the soil with nitrogen, making them perfect
for crop rotation and intercropping with cereals.
2.2 COWPEA (Vigna unguiculata)
 Importance: The most important food legume in Nigeria (used for beans, 'akara', 'moinmoin').
69
 Ecology: A warm-weather crop, relatively drought-tolerant (but needs water at
flowering). Prefers well-drained sandy loam.
 Cultivation:
o Planting: Plant seeds directly, 2-3 per hole. Spacing (e.g., 60x30cm).
o Fertilizer: Does not require Nitrogen fertilizer. A small "starter" dose of NPK or
just P and K (e.g., SSP) is sufficient.
 Pests & Diseases:
o Pests: This is the major limiting factor for cowpea. It is attacked by a complex
of pests at every stage:
 Seedling: Aphids.
 Flowering: Thrips (cause flowers to dry up and fall off).
 Podding: Maruca Pod Borer (larva bores into pods).
 Late Pod: Pod-sucking Bugs (cause shrivelled seeds).
o Control: Requires multiple, timed insecticide sprays to get a good yield.
 Harvest & Storage: Pods are picked as they dry, then threshed. The grains must be
protected from Bruchids (Weevils), which destroy them in storage. PICS bags (hermetic
storage) are an excellent solution.
2.3 SOYBEAN (Glycine max)
 Importance: A major global commodity. Used for oil (vegetable oil), animal feed
(Soybean Meal), and food (soymilk, awara - local tofu).
 Ecology: Needs more water and better soil than cowpea.
 Cultivation:
o Inoculation: In new fields, seeds must be inoculated with the correct bacteria
(Bradyrhizobium japonicum) to ensure nitrogen fixation.
o Planting: Drilled in rows. Weeds must be controlled.
 Harvesting: A key challenge is pod shattering. The whole plant is harvested (cut or
uprooted) when 90% of leaves have fallen and pods are dry. If left too long, pods will
shatter and spill the seeds.
 Processing: Threshed, dried, and stored.
70
2.4 GROUNDNUT / PEANUT (Arachis hypogaea)
 Importance: Grown for its edible seeds (eaten raw, boiled, roasted) and for its oil.
 Botany (Geocarpy): A unique plant. It flowers above ground, but after pollination, a
"peg" (stalk) grows from the flower down into the soil, where the pod (the "nut")
develops.
 Ecology: Requires light, sandy, friable (loose) soil to allow the pegs to penetrate easily.
 Cultivation: Planted on ridges or beds.
 Pests & Diseases:
o Groundnut Rosette Disease: A virus (spread by aphids) that stunts the plant.
o Aflatoxin: A major problem. A fungus (Aspergillus flavus) grows on the pods if
they are not dried properly. This fungus produces aflatoxin, a deadly toxin and
potent carcinogen.
 Harvesting: Uproot the entire plant. Curing (letting the plants dry in the field for a few
days) is essential to reduce moisture. Pods are then picked and dried thoroughly.

WEEK 3: TUBER CROPS
3.1 INTRODUCTION TO TUBERS
Root and Tuber crops are plants grown for their starchy, bulky, underground storage organs.
They are staple "energy foods" and crucial for food security in West Africa. They are mostly
propagated vegetatively (clonally).
3.2 CASSAVA (Manihot esculenta)
 Importance: The most widely cultivated tuber in Nigeria. A food security crop.
 Ecology: Its main advantage is its hardiness. It tolerates drought, grows on poor/acidic
soils where other crops fail, and can be "stored" in the ground (left unharvested).
 Planting Material: Stem cuttings (25-30cm long), taken from the woody part of the stem.
 Cultivation:
o Land Prep: Planted on ridges or mounds to promote tuber growth.
o Planting: Cuttings are planted at an angle, 1m x 1m spacing.
 The Cyanide Problem: Cassava roots contain cyanogenic glycosides (which produce
cyanide, a poison).
72
o Sweet Varieties: Low cyanide, can be boiled and eaten.
o Bitter Varieties: High cyanide, are toxic if eaten raw. Must be processed.
 Processing (Essential): Processing (grating, fermenting, boiling, frying) removes the
cyanide.
o Garri: Peeled, grated, fermented, de-watered, and fried (toasted).
o Fufu/Akpu: Fermented in water, sieved, boiled, and pounded.
o Lafun: Peeled, chopped, dried, and milled into flour.
 Pests & Diseases: Cassava Mosaic Disease (CMD) (virus), Cassava Green Mite, Cassava
Mealybug.
 Harvest: 8-18 months. Uproot roots. Problem: Harvested roots are highly perishable
and will rot in 2-3 days. Must be processed immediately.
3.3 YAM (Dioscorea spp.)
 Importance: A culturally significant crop ("The King of Crops"), especially in Nigeria
(the world's largest producer).
 Ecology: Requires deep, fertile, loose, well-drained soil. Cannot tolerate waterlogging.
Needs high rainfall.
 Planting Material: "Setts" (pieces of a yam tuber, 250-500g).
 Cultivation:
o Land Prep: Requires deep soil. Planted in large mounds or high ridges.
o Staking: This is essential for most varieties. A stake (pole) is provided for the
yam vine to climb. This exposes leaves to maximum sunlight, leading to large
tubers.
 Harvest: 9-12 months. Requires careful digging to avoid bruising the tubers.
 Storage: Unlike cassava, yams can be stored fresh for months. They are stored in a Yam
Barn – a shaded, well-ventilated structure where yams are tied to poles.
3.4 COCOYAM (Colocasia esculenta & Xanthosoma sagittifolium)
 Importance: A key staple in some parts of the SE/SW.
73
 Ecology: Opposite of most crops. It is shade-tolerant and water-loving. It thrives in
damp, humid conditions and is often intercropped under tree crops (e.g., cocoa, plantain).
 Planting Material: Cormels or small setts.
3.5 POTATO (IRISH POTATO) (Solanum tuberosum)
 Ecology: This is a temperate crop, not a tropical one. It requires cool temperatures to
form tubers.
 In Nigeria: It is only grown in high-altitude, cool areas: Jos Plateau (Plateau State) and
Mambilla Plateau (Taraba State).

WEEK 4: OIL CROPS
4.1 INTRODUCTION
Oil crops are plants cultivated primarily for the oil extracted from their seeds or fruits. These oils
are used for cooking, industrial applications, soap making (saponification), and biofuels.
4.2 OIL PALM (Elaeis guineensis)
 Importance: The most important oil crop in Nigeria.
 Ecology: A humid tropical crop. Requires high rainfall (2000mm+), high humidity, and
high temperatures. Needs deep, moist, fertile soils. (Dominant in the South-East and
South-South).
 Planting Material: Seedlings. The Tenera hybrid (a cross between Dura and Pisifera) is
the preferred commercial variety because it has a thick, fleshy part (mesocarp) and a thin
shell, giving the highest oil yield.
 Cultivation: A plantation crop. Spacing is wide (e.g., 9m triangular).
 Harvesting: Begins 3-4 years after planting. Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFB) are harvested
using a cutlass (for short trees) or a "Malaysian knife" (a long pole with a sickle).
75
 Processing (Two Oils):
1. Palm Oil (Red Oil): Extracted from the outer fleshy part (mesocarp).
 Process: Bunches are sterilized (boiled), fruits are stripped, digested
(pounded), pressed (to extract oil), and the oil is clarified.
2. Palm Kernel Oil (PKO): Extracted from the nut/kernel inside the hard shell.
 Process: The nuts are separated, cracked, the kernels are roasted, and then
pressed to extract PKO.
4.3 COCONUT (Cocos nucifera)
 Importance: A versatile crop ("tree of life").
 Ecology: A coastal crop. It is salt-tolerant and thrives in sandy, well-drained soils in
high-humidity areas (e.g., Lagos, Badagry).
 Products:
o Copra: The dried kernel (flesh). This is the part from which coconut oil is pressed.
o Coconut Water: The liquid inside the young nut.
o Coir: The fibre from the outer husk, used for ropes, mats, etc.
o Coconut Milk: Extracted from the grated fresh kernel.
4.4 SUNFLOWER (Helianthus annuus)
 Importance: A minor but growing oil crop.
 Botany: Unlike the others (which are perennial trees), this is an annual crop (grows and
dies in one season).
 Ecology: More adaptable and drought-tolerant than oil palm.
 Cultivation: Grown in rows, similar to maize. The large "head" contains the seeds.
 Products: High-quality cooking oil and seeds (snacks).

WEEK 6: VEGETABLES
6.1 INTRODUCTION
Vegetables are parts of plants consumed by humans as food. They are "protective foods,"
essential for health as they provide vitamins, minerals, and fibre. They are high-value, perishable
crops.
79
6.2 TOMATO (Solanum lycopersicum)
 Importance: A major vegetable, used in virtually every Nigerian stew, sauce, and Jollof
rice.
 Ecology: Requires warm, sunny days. Problem: High humidity and rainfall promote
fungal diseases. Therefore, dry season production (using irrigation) is the most reliable
and productive, resulting in better quality fruit.
 Cultivation:
o Nursery: Seeds are not planted directly. They are raised in a "nursery" (a
protected bed) for 3-4 weeks.
o Transplanting: Seedlings are carefully moved to the main field.
o Staking: Indeterminate (tall/climbing) varieties must be staked to keep fruit off
the ground, prevent rot, and make spraying easier.
 Pests & Diseases:
o Diseases: The major problem. Fungal blights (Early & Late Blight), bacterial wilt,
Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus (TYLCV).
o Pests: Whiteflies (spreads TYLCV), Tuta absoluta (Tomato Leaf Miner) is a
devastating pest.
 Problem: Nigeria has a massive tomato deficit due to post-harvest losses and poor dryseason
production.
6.3 PEPPER (Capsicum spp.)
 Importance: A key ingredient for flavour and heat.
 Types in Nigeria:
o Ata Rodo / Habanero (C. chinense): Very hot, aromatic.
o Ata Jije / Bird's Eye (C. frutescens): Small, very hot.
o Tatashe / Bell (C. annuum): Mild, large, used for colour.
 Cultivation: Very similar to tomato (nursery, transplanting). They are generally hardier
and more disease-resistant than tomatoes.
6.4 OKRA (Abelmoschus esculentus)
80
 Importance: A popular vegetable, used as a "thickener" for soups ('draw soup').
 Cultivation: Planted directly by seed.
 Harvesting: This is the key point. Pods must be harvested every 2-3 days while they are
young and tender. If left on the plant, they quickly become fibrous and inedible. Regular
harvesting also encourages the plant to produce more flowers.
6.5 LEAFY VEGETABLES (SPINACH, AMARANTH)
 Types:
o Amaranth (Amaranthus spp.): "Efo Tete," "Green."
o Lagos Spinach / Fluted Pumpkin (Telfairia occidentalis): "Efo Ugwu." (A
vine).
o Celosia (Celosia argentea): "Efo Shoko."
 Cultivation:
o Land Prep: Planted on raised beds to ensure good drainage.
o Planting: Seeds are tiny and are often "broadcast" (scattered) over the bed and
lightly raked in.
o Fertilizer: These are "leaf" crops, so they respond very well to Nitrogen (e.g.,
Urea or poultry manure) to promote lush leaf growth.
 Harvesting: Can be "once-over" (uprooting the whole plant) or "multiple-cut" (cutting
the leaves, allowing the plant to re-sprout for 2-3 more harvests).

WEEK 8: FRUITS
8.1 INTRODUCTION
Fruits are the mature, seed-bearing ovaries of flowering plants, typically eaten raw. They are
sources of sugar (energy), vitamins (especially C and A), and fibre. They are high-value
horticultural crops.
8.2 MANGO (Mangifera indica)
 Importance: A popular seasonal fruit.
 Propagation:
o Seed: (Traditional) Easy, but the tree may not be "true-to-type" (like the parent)
and takes long (7+ years) to fruit.
o Grafting/Budding: (Modern/Commercial) This is the best method. A bud or
branch from a superior tree (e.g., 'Kent', 'Keith') is joined onto a hardy
"rootstock." This guarantees the fruit type and quality, and the tree fruits much
faster (3-4 years).
 Problems:
84
o Alternate Bearing: A tendency to fruit heavily one year and very poorly the next.
o Anthracnose: A fungal disease that causes black spots on leaves, flowers, and
fruits, leading to rot.
8.3 CITRUS (Citrus spp.)
 Importance: Includes Sweet Orange (C. sinensis), Lemon (C. limon), Lime (C.
aurantifolia), and Grapefruit (C. paradisi).
 Propagation:
o Budding (T-Budding): This is the standard commercial method. A bud from the
desired variety is inserted under the bark of a hardy rootstock (e.g., rough lemon).
This ensures uniformity and disease resistance.
 Cultivation: A plantation crop, requires good soil, fertilizer, and pest control.
8.4 BANANA & PLANTAIN (Musa spp.)
 Botany: These are giant herbs, not trees. The "trunk" is a "pseudostem" made of rolled
leaf sheaths.
 Propagation: They do not (normally) produce seeds. They are propagated vegetatively
using "suckers" (also called "pups") that grow from the base of the mother plant.
 Management:
o De-suckering: Removing excess suckers, leaving only 1 or 2 to replace the
mother plant.
o Propping: The fruit bunch is heavy. The plant must be "propped" with a forked
stick to prevent it from falling over.
 Pests & Diseases: Banana Weevil, Black Sigatoka (a fungal leaf disease).
8.5 PINEAPPLE (Ananas comosus)
 Propagation (Vegetative):
o Suckers: Grow from the base.
o Slips: Grow from the stalk just below the fruit.
o Crown: The leafy top of the fruit itself.
85
 Cultivation: A terrestrial (ground) plant. Planted on raised beds, often with black plastic
mulch (to control weeds and warm the soil).
 Harvest: Takes 1.5 - 2 years from planting.

WEEK 9: SPICES AND CONDIMENTS
9.1 INTRODUCTION
Spices and condiments are substances added to food in small quantities to add flavour, aroma,
colour, or "heat" (pungency). They are not eaten as a main course but are essential for making
food palatable.
87
9.2 GINGER (Zingiber officinale)
 Botany: The part used is the rhizome (a fleshy, underground stem), not a root.
 Ecology: A tropical plant that needs a warm, humid climate, high rainfall, and some
shade. It does well in forest or semi-forest conditions.
 In Nigeria: Kaduna State (Southern Kaduna) is the undisputed hub of ginger production.
 Cultivation:
o Planting: Small pieces of the rhizome ("setts"), each with a few "eyes" or buds,
are planted in beds or ridges.
o Mulching: The beds are heavily mulched (with grass) to conserve moisture, keep
the soil cool, and suppress weeds.
 Harvest: 8-10 months. The rhizomes are carefully dug up, washed, and dried. Sold fresh
or dry.
9.3 GARLIC (Allium sativum)
 Botany: A member of the onion family. The part used is the bulb, which is composed of
"cloves."
 Ecology: This is not a humid-tropical crop. It requires a cool, dry season (Harmattan)
for the bulb to form properly.
 In Nigeria: Grown almost exclusively in the far North (e.g., Sokoto, Kano) during the
dry season using irrigation.
 Cultivation: Individual "cloves" are separated and planted.
9.4 PEPPER (BLACK PEPPER) (Piper nigrum)
 Important Distinction: This is not the same as Capsicum (Ata rodo/Tatashe). This is the
small, round peppercorn (Black, White, or Green pepper).
 Botany: It is a climbing vine.
 Cultivation: It must be "staked" or grown up a support (a pole or a living tree).
 Products:
o Black Pepper: The unripe (green) fruit, harvested and dried in the sun, which
makes it turn black and shrivel.
88
o White Pepper: The ripe (red) fruit, soaked in water (retting) to remove the outer
flesh, leaving the white seed, which is then dried.
9.5 CLOVES (Syzygium aromaticum)
 Botany: Cloves are the un-opened, dried flower buds of a tropical evergreen tree.
 Harvest: The buds are hand-picked just before they open (when they turn pink) and are
dried in the sun until they turn dark brown.

WEEK 10: MEDICINAL PLANTS
10.1 INTRODUCTION (ETHNOBOTANY)
Medicinal plants are plants that are used for their therapeutic properties to treat, cure, or prevent
illness in humans and animals. This is the foundation of "Traditional Medicine." Many modern
drugs are derived from compounds first discovered in plants.
90
10.2 NEEM (Azadirachta indica)
 Common Name: "Dogonyaro" (Hausa).
 Origin: Native to India, but now widespread ("naturalized") all over Nigeria. It's a hardy,
drought-tolerant tree.
 Medicinal Uses:
o Antimalarial: The leaves and bark are boiled into a bitter tea (Agbo) to treat
fever and malaria.
o Skin: The oil and leaf-paste are used to treat skin diseases (e.g., eczema, rashes).
 Horticultural Use (Very Important):
o Natural Insecticide: The seeds contain a powerful compound called
Azadirachtin. This is extracted to make "Neem Oil," a safe, biodegradable
insecticide used in organic farming to control pests.
10.3 MORINGA (Moringa oleifera)
 Common Names: "Miracle Tree," "Zogale" (Hausa).
 Importance: A fast-growing tree valued for its incredible nutritional and medicinal
properties.
 Medicinal/Nutritional Uses:
o Nutrition: The leaves are a "superfood." They are packed with protein, Vitamin
A, Vitamin C, calcium, and potassium. Used to combat malnutrition.
o Water Purification: The seeds, when crushed, act as a "flocculant." They cause
dirt and microbes in water to clump together and sink, clarifying the water.
o Blood Pressure: The leaves are used to make a tea to help regulate blood
pressure.
10.4 ALOE VERA (Aloe barbadensis)
 Botany: A succulent plant that stores water in its thick, fleshy leaves.
 Uses:
o Skin (Topical Use): The clear gel inside the leaf is a famous remedy for:
 Burns: Cools and soothes fresh burns (sunburn, kitchen burns).
91
 Skin Irritation: Treats rashes, insect bites, and eczema.
o Internal Use: The juice (a different part, the yellow "latex") is a powerful
laxative, used to treat constipation. Must be used with care.
