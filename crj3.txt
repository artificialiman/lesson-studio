WEEK 1: THE PROMISE OF THE HOLY SPIRIT (PART 1)
This lesson explores the promise of the Holy Spirit, a central theme in the New Testament,
emphasizing God’s provision of guidance, comfort, and empowerment to believers. The Holy
Spirit is the third person of the Trinity, co-equal with the Father and the Son, and plays a vital
role in the life of Christians by teaching, guiding, comforting, convicting, and empowering
them for service.
JESUS PROMISES THE COMING OF THE HOLY SPIRIT (John 14:16-17, 26; John
16:5-16):
 Jesus reassured His disciples that even though He would leave them physically, He
would send the Holy Spirit to remain with them forever.
 The Holy Spirit is referred to as the Comforter, Advocate, Teacher, and Helper. The
roles include:
3
o Teacher: Guides believers into all truth and reveals the meaning of Jesus’
teachings.
o Reminder: Reminds believers of everything Jesus taught.
o Convictor: Convicts the world of sin, righteousness, and judgment.
o Empowerer: Gives courage, wisdom, and strength to fulfill God’s mission.
 Jesus highlighted that the Holy Spirit’s coming was essential for continuing His
ministry on earth through the disciples.
Key points:
 The promise of the Holy Spirit was made before Jesus’ crucifixion, showing God’s
forethought and plan for the disciples.
 Believers are not left directionless or powerless; the Holy Spirit equips them to live
godly lives.
 This promise assures Christians of God’s continuous presence, guidance, and
protection.
WAITING FOR THE PROMISE (Acts 1:3-5):
 After His resurrection, Jesus spent forty days teaching His disciples about the kingdom
of God and preparing them for their mission.
 He instructed them to stay in Jerusalem and wait for the promise of the Father, which
is the baptism of the Holy Spirit.
 Waiting signifies:
o Patience: Learning to trust God’s timing rather than rushing ahead.
o Obedience: Following Jesus’ instruction demonstrates faithfulness.
o Preparation: Spiritual readiness is required to receive empowerment from the
Holy Spirit.
4
 The promise was fulfilled on Pentecost (Acts 2:1-4) when the Holy Spirit descended,
enabling the disciples to speak in different tongues and boldly preach the gospel.
Key points:
 The period of waiting highlights the importance of faith, discipline, and spiritual
readiness.
 The coming of the Holy Spirit marked the beginning of the Christian mission to the
world.
 Believers today are also called to wait on God through prayer, devotion, and
obedience to receive His guidance and empowerment.
LESSON APPLICATION:
 Students are encouraged to trust God’s promises, just as the disciples did.
 Understanding the role of the Holy Spirit helps believers to:
o Grow spiritually by developing a personal relationship with God.
o Make wise decisions in line with God’s will.
o Serve effectively in the church, community, and society.
o Experience comfort, guidance, and conviction in daily life.
 The lesson teaches the importance of faithful waiting, prayer, and spiritual readiness
for God’s empowerment.
EVALUATION:
1. Explain in your own words the promise of the Holy Spirit.
2. List four roles of the Holy Spirit as described in John 14:16-17, 26 and John 16:5-16.
3. Describe the instructions Jesus gave His disciples regarding waiting for the promise.
5
4. Why is waiting for God’s promises important in the life of a believer?
5. How did the coming of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost empower the disciples?

John 16:5-16, with explanations of each role.
3. Discuss in two paragraphs the significance of waiting faithfully for God’s promises and
how it applies to modern Christian life.

WEEK 2: THE COMING OF THE HOLY SPIRIT
Acts 2:1–13 – The Day of Pentecost, Speaking in Tongues, Reactions of the People
INTRODUCTION
 The coming of the Holy Spirit is a major turning point in the history of Christianity.
 It fulfilled the promises of Jesus to His disciples before His ascension (John 14:16–17;
Acts 1:8).
 Pentecost was originally a Jewish harvest festival celebrated 50 days after the Passover.
In Hebrew it was called Shavuot, the Feast of Weeks, commemorating both harvest and
the giving of the Law on Mount Sinai.
7
 On this day, the disciples, who had been waiting and praying in Jerusalem, received the
Holy Spirit.
 This event marked the beginning of the disciples’ public ministry and the official birth of
the Church.
THE DAY OF PENTECOST (ACTS 2:1–4)
 The disciples were gathered together in the upper room, in unity and prayer, as Jesus
had commanded them.
 Suddenly, unusual supernatural events occurred:
o A sound from heaven like a mighty rushing wind filled the entire house. Wind
symbolizes the invisible, life-giving breath of God.
o Tongues as of fire appeared and rested on each disciple. Fire represents
purification, power, and the presence of God.
o Each disciple was filled with the Holy Spirit.
o They began to speak in different tongues (languages) as the Spirit enabled them.
 This was not a natural occurrence but a divine empowerment, showing that God had
poured His Spirit upon them.
SPEAKING IN TONGUES (ACTS 2:5–11)
 Jerusalem was crowded with devout Jews and proselytes from many nations because of
the Pentecost festival.
 They were amazed to hear the apostles speaking in their own native languages, even
though the apostles were Galileans with little education.
 The disciples spoke of the wonderful works of God, glorifying Him in many tongues.
 The nations and regions represented included:
8
1. Parthia
2. Media
3. Elam
4. Mesopotamia
5. Cappadocia
6. Pontus
7. Asia Minor
8. Phrygia
9. Pamphylia
10. Egypt
11. Libya (near Cyrene)
12. Visitors from Rome
13. Crete
14. Arabia
 This shows the universal nature of the gospel – Christianity was not only for Jews but
for the whole world.
REACTIONS OF THE PEOPLE (ACTS 2:12–13)
 The event caused mixed reactions among the people present:
1. Amazement and Awe – Many were astonished that they could hear their own
languages being spoken fluently. They marveled at the power of God.
2. Confusion and Perplexity – Some did not understand the meaning and asked
questions like: “What does this mean?”
9
3. Mockery and Ridicule – Others mocked the apostles, accusing them of being
drunk with new wine. They dismissed the miracle as foolishness.
 These reactions show that not everyone accepts the works of God in the same way. While
some believe and are inspired, others doubt and criticize.
SIGNIFICANCE OF THE COMING OF THE HOLY SPIRIT
1. Fulfillment of Prophecy – It fulfilled Joel 2:28–29 about God pouring His Spirit upon
all flesh.
2. Fulfillment of Jesus’ Promise – Jesus had assured His disciples they would receive the
Comforter (John 14:16–17; Acts 1:8).
3. Birth of the Christian Church – Pentecost is regarded as the day the Christian Church
officially began.
4. Empowerment for Evangelism – The disciples received boldness, courage, and
supernatural ability to preach the gospel.
5. Universality of the Gospel – Speaking in many languages showed that the gospel was
for all nations, not for Jews alone.
6. Unity of Believers – The event demonstrated the importance of praying and waiting
together in one accord.
7. Presence of God’s Spirit – The wind, fire, and tongues symbolized God’s living
presence among His people.
8. Foundation for Missionary Work – The miracle prepared the way for rapid expansion
of Christianity across nations.
LESSONS FOR CHRISTIANS TODAY
 Christians must learn to wait on God in prayer for His power and guidance.
10
 The Holy Spirit is essential for living a holy and bold Christian life.
 Believers must be open to the Spirit’s gifts, which enable effective service in God’s
kingdom.
 Christianity is a universal faith – it cuts across tribes, races, and languages.
 Just like in Pentecost, Christians may face mockery and opposition, but they must
remain steadfast.
 The Church grows when believers are united in faith and obedience.
EVALUATION
1. Explain what happened to the disciples on the Day of Pentecost.
2. Mention and explain the two major symbols that appeared when the Holy Spirit came.
3. List at least five nations whose people were present in Jerusalem during Pentecost.
4. State three reactions of the people to the event of Pentecost.
5. Why is the Day of Pentecost regarded as the “birthday of the Church”?
ASSIGNMENT
1. Describe in detail the supernatural signs that accompanied the coming of the Holy Spirit.
2. State and explain four significance of the coming of the Holy Spirit to the early Church.
3. Write short notes on the following:
a. The rushing wind
b. Tongues of fire
c. Speaking in tongues
d. The accusation of drunkenness

WEEK 3: PETER’S FIRST SPEECH AND ITS EFFECTS
Acts 2:14–41 – Peter’s Boldness, Message of Repentance, Conversion of about 3,000 Souls
INTRODUCTION
 After the resurrection and ascension of Jesus Christ, the disciples received the Holy Spirit
on the Day of Pentecost (Acts 2:1–13).
 This extraordinary event drew a large crowd in Jerusalem, many of whom were confused,
amazed, or mocking the disciples.
 Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, stood up and delivered a bold speech explaining the
meaning of the event, pointing to the prophecy of Joel, and preaching Jesus Christ as the
risen Messiah.
12
PETER’S BOLDNESS
 Peter had previously denied Jesus three times out of fear, but after receiving the Holy
Spirit, he became courageous and fearless.
 He boldly addressed the multitude of Jews in Jerusalem without shame or hesitation.
 He clarified that the disciples were not drunk as some accused, but rather filled with the
Holy Spirit as prophesied by Joel (Joel 2:28–32).
 His boldness shows the transforming power of the Holy Spirit in the life of a believer.
MESSAGE OF REPENTANCE
 Peter preached that Jesus of Nazareth was approved by God through mighty works,
wonders, and signs.
 He told the people that they crucified Jesus, but God raised Him from the dead.
 He declared that Jesus’ resurrection was in fulfillment of the Old Testament prophecy
(Psalm 16:8–11).
 Peter explained that Jesus had been exalted at the right hand of God and had poured out
the Holy Spirit.
 He concluded by affirming that God made Jesus both Lord and Christ, whom they
crucified.
 On hearing this, the people were “cut to the heart” and asked, “Brothers, what shall we
do?”
 Peter told them to repent and be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the
forgiveness of sins, and they would receive the gift of the Holy Spirit.
CONVERSION OF ABOUT 3,000 SOULS
13
 After Peter’s speech, many accepted his message wholeheartedly.
 About 3,000 souls were baptized and added to the Church that very day.
 This marked the beginning of rapid Church growth and the spread of Christianity.
 The new believers devoted themselves to the apostles’ teaching, fellowship, breaking of
bread, and prayers (Acts 2:42).
 The conversion demonstrated the power of the gospel message and the work of the Holy
Spirit in convicting people of sin.
LESSONS FROM PETER’S SPEECH
 The Holy Spirit empowers believers with boldness and courage.
 Repentance and faith in Jesus Christ bring forgiveness of sins and salvation.
 God’s promises in Scripture are always fulfilled.
 Preaching the true gospel brings transformation and church growth.
 Every Christian has the responsibility to share the message of repentance and salvation.
EVALUATION
1. Explain how Peter showed boldness in his first speech.
2. What was the central message of Peter’s sermon in Acts 2:14–41?
3. State three effects of Peter’s speech on the people who heard him.
4. Why is repentance important in the Christian faith?
5. Mention three lessons Christians can learn from the conversion of 3,000 souls.
ASSIGNMENT
14
1. Write in detail the role of the Holy Spirit in Peter’s boldness and the success of his first
sermon.
2. Compare Peter’s behavior before and after Pentecost, showing the change in his character.
3. Explain how the message of Peter’s speech is still relevant to Christians today.

WEEK 4: FELLOWSHIP IN THE EARLY CHURCH (PART 1)
Acts 2:41-47 – Unity, Sharing, Prayers, Breaking of Bread
INTRODUCTION TO FELLOWSHIP
 Fellowship in the Christian context refers to the close relationship, association, and
communion among believers.
 It emphasizes unity, mutual support, spiritual growth, and collective worship.
 The early church in Jerusalem, as described in Acts 2:41-47, provides a model for
Christian fellowship.
16
FEATURES OF FELLOWSHIP IN THE EARLY CHURCH
1. UNITY
 Believers were united in faith, purpose, and worship.
 Unity involved sharing common beliefs, goals, and commitment to God’s teachings.
 Acts 2:44: “All that believed were together and had all things in common.”
 Significance: Unity strengthened the church, promoted peace, and enhanced cooperation
among members.
2. SHARING
 Members shared resources, possessions, and support with one another.
 Sharing included food, wealth, and material needs to help the poor and less privileged.
 Acts 2:45: “They sold their possessions and goods and distributed the proceeds to all, as
any had need.”
 Significance: Sharing fostered love, reduced inequality, and reflected Christ-like
generosity.
3. PRAYERS
 Prayer was central to the fellowship of the early church.
 Believers prayed together, sought guidance, and interceded for one another.
 Acts 2:42: “They devoted themselves to the apostles’ teaching and to fellowship, to the
breaking of bread and to prayer.”
 Significance: Prayer strengthened faith, unity, and dependence on God.
4. BREAKING OF BREAD
 Refers to communal meals and the Lord’s Supper (Eucharist).
 It was a symbol of remembrance, gratitude, and spiritual communion with Christ.
 Acts 2:42: Members met “for the breaking of bread” regularly.
17
 Significance: Reinforced spiritual unity, shared faith experience, and mutual
encouragement.
IMPORTANCE OF FELLOWSHIP IN THE CHURCH
1. Spiritual Growth: Fellowship nurtures faith, understanding, and discipleship.
2. Mutual Support: Helps members in times of need through material, emotional, or
spiritual support.
3. Promotes Unity and Harmony: Reduces conflicts and fosters peace among believers.
4. Strengthens Evangelism: United Christians attract others to the faith through love and
example.
5. Encourages Regular Worship and Prayer: Collective worship deepens devotion and
dependence on God.
6. Reflects Christ’s Teachings: Fellowship demonstrates obedience to Jesus’ command to
love one another (John 13:34-35).
EVALUATION
1. Define fellowship in the context of the early church.
2. List four features of fellowship in Acts 2:41-47.
3. Explain the importance of unity among believers.
4. Why was sharing significant in the early church?
5. How did prayers and breaking of bread strengthen the fellowship?
ASSIGNMENT
1. Write an essay: “Lessons Modern Churches Can Learn from Fellowship in the Early
Church.”
18
2. Conduct an interview with a church member and identify three ways your local church
practices fellowship today.
3. List five ways Christians can maintain unity and sharing in their fellowship groups.

WEEK 6: FELLOWSHIP IN THE EARLY CHURCH (PART 2)
Acts 5:1–11 – Fraud in the Church (Ananias and Sapphira); Acts 6:1–2 – Murmuring and
Complaints
FRAUD IN THE CHURCH (ANANIAS AND SAPPHIRA) – ACTS 5:1–11
The early Christians lived in fellowship, sharing their belongings and selling their possessions to
help one another. Everyone contributed honestly and willingly. However, a couple named
Ananias and Sapphira decided to act dishonestly.
Ananias sold a piece of land and, with the knowledge of his wife Sapphira, kept back part of the
money for themselves. He brought only a portion to the apostles but pretended that he had
brought all the proceeds.
20
Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, confronted Ananias and asked him why he had allowed Satan
to fill his heart to lie to the Holy Spirit. Peter reminded him that the land was his before selling it,
and even after the sale, the money was his to do with as he wished. But his deceit was not against
men but against God. Upon hearing this, Ananias fell down and died instantly.
Later, Sapphira came in without knowing what had happened. She too was questioned by Peter,
and she repeated the same lie. She also fell down and died. Great fear fell upon the entire church
and all who heard the story.
Lessons from Ananias and Sapphira’s fraud:
1. God hates dishonesty and hypocrisy.
2. Fraud and deceit in the church bring judgment.
3. The Holy Spirit cannot be deceived.
4. Christians must give sincerely and not for show or recognition.
5. The fear of God must be upheld in the church.
MURMURING AND COMPLAINTS – ACTS 6:1–2
As the number of disciples grew in Jerusalem, a problem arose in the church. The Greekspeaking
Jews (Hellenists) complained against the Hebrew-speaking Jews because their widows
were being neglected in the daily distribution of food.
This murmuring brought division and dissatisfaction among the believers. To solve this problem,
the twelve apostles called the disciples together. They explained that it was not proper for them
to abandon preaching the word of God in order to serve tables. They instructed the church to
select seven men of good reputation, filled with the Spirit and wisdom, to oversee the daily
distribution.
This led to the appointment of seven deacons, including Stephen, Philip, Prochorus, Nicanor,
Timon, Parmenas, and Nicolas. The apostles then devoted themselves fully to prayer and the
ministry of the word.
21
Lessons from murmuring and complaints:
1. Growth in the church may bring challenges, but they must be solved with wisdom.
2. Complaints should be handled fairly and justly.
3. Leaders must not neglect the spiritual work of prayer and teaching.
4. Delegation of responsibilities is necessary in the church.
5. The appointment of deacons shows the importance of service in God’s house.
SUMMARY
 In Acts 5:1–11, Ananias and Sapphira were punished for lying and committing fraud in
the early church. Their hypocrisy brought death and fear among believers.
 In Acts 6:1–2, murmuring arose between the Hellenists and Hebrews over the neglect of
widows. The apostles resolved the matter by delegating duties to seven deacons, allowing
them to focus on prayer and preaching.
 These stories teach the importance of honesty, unity, service, and proper leadership in the
church.

3. Explain the cause of murmuring in Acts 6:1–2.
4. How did the apostles solve the problem of neglect in food distribution?
5. Mention three lessons Christians can learn from the appointment of deacons.

WEEK 8: SELECTION OF DEACONS
The selection of deacons in the early church is a biblical example of organized church
leadership. It highlights the importance of service, fairness, and effective administration in
church growth. This lesson examines Acts 6:1-7, the appointment of Stephen and six others, and
the responsibilities of deacons in the church.
BIBLICAL ACCOUNT (ACTS 6:1-7):
 In the early church, as the number of believers grew, a problem arose: Greek-speaking
widows were being neglected in daily food distribution.
24
 The apostles recognized the issue and appointed seven men of good reputation, full of
the Spirit and wisdom, to take charge of the distribution of food and welfare of the
community.
 The men appointed were: Stephen, Philip, Prochorus, Nicanor, Timon, Parmenas, and
Nicolas.
 This decision ensured fairness, allowed apostles to focus on prayer and ministry of
the Word, and demonstrated organizational skills within the church.
SELECTION CRITERIA FOR DEACONS:
 Good reputation: Must be trustworthy and respected by the church community.
 Full of the Holy Spirit: Spiritually mature, with integrity and faithfulness.
 Wisdom: Able to make fair and just decisions in church administration.
 Commitment to service: Willing to serve selflessly and diligently.
RESPONSIBILITIES OF DEACONS:
 Distribution of resources: Ensure fair distribution of food, aid, and other church
materials.
 Assistance in church administration: Support pastors and elders in organizing church
programs.
 Spiritual guidance: Help maintain discipline and moral standards among members.
 Pastoral support: Assist in visiting, caring for the sick, elderly, widows, and vulnerable
members.
 Promoting unity: Resolve disputes and ensure harmonious relationships within the
congregation.
 Prayer and service: Participate actively in spiritual and community activities.
25
LESSONS FROM THE SELECTION OF DEACONS:
 Leadership is service: True leaders prioritize the needs of others.
 Delegation is important: Distributing responsibilities ensures efficiency and prevents
neglect.
 Spiritual maturity matters: Leaders should be spiritually equipped to serve faithfully.
 Fairness and integrity are essential: Maintaining justice strengthens trust and unity in
the church.
EVALUATION:
1. Explain why the apostles appointed deacons in the early church.
2. List the qualities required for selecting deacons.
3. Describe four responsibilities of deacons in the church.
4. How does the selection of deacons promote unity and fairness in the church?
ASSIGNMENT:
1. Write an essay (200–250 words) on the importance of deacons in the modern church.
2. Prepare a table showing the names of the first deacons and their responsibilities
according to Acts 6:1-7.
3. Role-play a scenario where deacons are resolving a problem in the church and explain the
steps taken.

WEEK 9: PERSECUTION OF EARLY BELIEVERS (PART 1)
Acts 3:1–11, 4:1–22 – Arrest of Peter and John; Acts 6:8–15, 7:1–60 – Arrest and Martyrdom of
Stephen
ARREST OF PETER AND JOHN (Acts 3:1–11, 4:1–22)
1. Healing of the Lame Man (Acts 3:1–11):
o Peter and John went to the temple at the hour of prayer.
o At the Beautiful Gate, they met a lame man who begged for alms.
27
o Peter declared, “Silver and gold I have none, but in the name of Jesus Christ of
Nazareth, rise up and walk.”
o The man was instantly healed, leaping and praising God.
o This miracle drew a large crowd, leading to Peter’s sermon about Jesus.
2. The Arrest (Acts 4:1–22):
o The priests, the captain of the temple, and the Sadducees were angry because
Peter and John preached about Jesus and the resurrection.
o They arrested them and kept them in custody until the next day.
o When brought before the council, Peter boldly declared that the lame man was
healed by the power of Jesus Christ, whom they crucified but whom God raised
from the dead.
o The Sanhedrin, seeing the boldness of Peter and John, were amazed, knowing
they were unlearned men but had been with Jesus.
o The healed man stood with them, and since the miracle was undeniable, the
leaders warned them not to speak in Jesus’ name again.
o Peter and John replied that they must obey God rather than men.
o They were threatened and released because of the support of the people who
glorified God for the miracle.
Lessons from the Arrest of Peter and John:
 Christians should remain bold in the face of opposition.
 Miracles authenticate the power of Jesus Christ.
 Persecution is part of the Christian faith.
 God gives His servants courage to defend the truth.
 We must obey God rather than men.
28
ARREST AND MARTYRDOM OF STEPHEN (Acts 6:8–15, 7:1–60)
1. Stephen’s Ministry (Acts 6:8–15):
o Stephen, one of the seven deacons, was full of faith, wisdom, and the Holy Spirit.
o He performed great wonders and signs among the people.
o Certain members of the synagogue argued with him but could not withstand his
wisdom and the Spirit by which he spoke.
o They secretly instigated false witnesses to accuse him of speaking against Moses
and the temple.
o He was seized and brought before the council.
o False witnesses claimed he said that Jesus would destroy the temple and change
the customs of Moses.
o His face appeared like that of an angel as he stood before the council.
2. Stephen’s Speech and Martyrdom (Acts 7:1–60):
o Stephen addressed the council, recounting the history of Israel from Abraham to
Solomon.
o He accused the Jewish leaders of resisting the Holy Spirit, betraying, and
murdering Jesus Christ, the Righteous One.
o The council was enraged and gnashed their teeth at him.
o Stephen, full of the Holy Spirit, looked up and saw the glory of God and Jesus
standing at the right hand of God.
o He declared his vision, but the people cried out loudly, stopped their ears, and
rushed at him.
o They dragged him out of the city and stoned him.
o Witnesses laid their garments at the feet of Saul (later Paul).
29
o Stephen prayed, “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit,” and “Lord, do not hold this sin
against them.” Then he died, becoming the first Christian martyr.
Lessons from the Arrest and Martyrdom of Stephen:
 Christians should stand firm in faith even in the face of death.
 The Holy Spirit empowers believers with wisdom and boldness.
 False accusations may arise against true believers.
 Stephen’s forgiveness teaches Christians to love even their enemies.
 Persecution spreads the gospel, as seen later through Saul’s conversion.
EVALUATION
1. Narrate the healing of the lame man and explain why Peter and John were arrested.
2. How did Peter and John defend their faith before the council?
3. Who was Stephen and why was he arrested?
4. Explain Stephen’s message before the council.
5. What lessons can Christians learn from the martyrdom of Stephen?
ASSIGNMENT
1. Compare the persecution of Peter and John with that of Stephen.
2. Explain five lessons Christians today can learn from the arrest of Peter and John.
3. Discuss the significance of Stephen’s prayer of forgiveness at his death.
30
WEEK 10: PERSECUTION OF EARLY BELIEVERS (PART 2)
Acts 8:1–3; 9:1–2 – Saul’s Persecution; Acts 12:1–4 – Martyrdom of James
INTRODUCTION
 The early Christians faced severe persecution after the death and resurrection of Jesus
Christ.
 This persecution arose because the Jewish leaders and Roman authorities saw the
Christian faith as a threat to their traditions, power, and influence.
 In this lesson, we shall examine the persecution led by Saul (later called Paul) and the
martyrdom of James, the brother of John.
31
SAUL’S PERSECUTION OF THE CHURCH (ACTS 8:1–3; 9:1–2)
 Saul, a Pharisee and strict defender of Jewish traditions, was one of the greatest
persecutors of early believers.
 After the stoning of Stephen, Saul supported and supervised severe attacks on Christians.
 He went from house to house, dragging men and women to prison because of their faith
in Jesus Christ.
 Saul obtained letters from the high priest to go to Damascus to arrest Christians and bring
them back to Jerusalem for punishment.
 His actions scattered many believers to different regions such as Judea and Samaria, but
the gospel continued to spread wherever they went.
MARTYRDOM OF JAMES (ACTS 12:1–4)
 King Herod Agrippa I also persecuted the Church to gain the favor of the Jews.
 He ordered the arrest of James, the brother of John (one of the sons of Zebedee and a
close disciple of Jesus).

 The blood of the martyrs became a seed for the Church, encouraging others to hold firmly
to their belief in Christ.
 Persecution brought the fulfillment of Jesus’ prophecy that His disciples would suffer for
His name’s sake (John 16:33).
LESSONS FOR CHRISTIANS TODAY
 Christians should expect persecution for their faith in Christ.
 Persecution can be a tool that God uses to spread the gospel.
 Believers must remain courageous and faithful even in the face of suffering or death.
 Like James, Christians should be ready to sacrifice everything for Christ, including their
lives.
 God can turn the greatest persecutors (like Saul) into great instruments for His work.

INTRODUCTION
 The ministry of Peter played a vital role in the expansion of the early Church.
 Up until Acts 10, the gospel had been preached mainly to Jews and Jewish converts
(proselytes).
 The conversion of Cornelius, a Gentile, marked a turning point in Christian history,

 This event also settled the question of whether Gentiles could be accepted without first

CONVERSION OF CORNELIUS (ACTS 10:1–48)
 Cornelius was a Roman centurion (army officer) living in Caesarea.
 He was a devout and God-fearing man, who gave generously to the poor and prayed
regularly to God.
 An angel of God appeared to him in a vision and instructed him to send for Peter in Joppa.
 At the same time, Peter had a vision of a sheet let down from heaven containing clean

 Peter initially refused, saying he had never eaten anything unclean. But God told him not
to call impure what He had made clean.
 This vision prepared Peter to understand that God shows no favoritism and that the
gospel is for all nations.
 Peter obeyed the Spirit, went to Cornelius’ house, and preached Jesus Christ as Lord of
all, who died and rose again for the salvation of mankind.
 While Peter was still speaking, the Holy Spirit came upon Cornelius and his household,
and they began to speak in tongues and praise God.
 Peter then commanded that they be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ.
PETER DEFENDS HIS ACTION (ACTS 11:1–18)
 When Peter returned to Jerusalem, some Jewish believers (the circumcised group)
criticized him for going into the house of Gentiles and eating with them.
 Peter explained the vision he had received from God and how the Holy Spirit fell upon
Cornelius and his household just as it did upon the apostles at Pentecost.
45
 He reminded them of Jesus’ promise that believers would be baptized with the Holy
Spirit.
 He concluded that if God gave the same gift of the Spirit to Gentiles as He did to Jews,

then, God has granted even to the Gentiles repentance unto life.”

 It marked the official opening of the gospel to Gentiles.
 It showed that God is no respecter of persons – salvation is for all who believe.
 It confirmed that the Holy Spirit is given to both Jews and Gentiles.
 It broke down the barrier between Jews and non-Jews in the Church.
 It fulfilled Jesus’ command that the gospel should go to all nations (Matthew 28:19–20;
Acts 1:8).
LESSONS FOR CHRISTIANS TODAY
 God shows no favoritism – all humans are equal before Him.
 Good works are important, but salvation comes only through faith in Jesus Christ.
 Christians should not discriminate based on race, tribe, or social class.
 The Holy Spirit is for everyone who believes in Christ.
 Christians must be obedient to God’s call, even when it challenges tradition or personal
beliefs.

PAUL’S NATIVITY AND EDUCATION (ACTS 22:1–5, 26–30)
Paul, originally known as Saul, was born in Tarsus, a prominent city in Cilicia. This gave him
the privilege of being a Roman citizen by birth, which later played a very important role in his
ministry and legal protection.
Saul was a Jew by race, belonging to the tribe of Benjamin. From childhood, he was trained
strictly in Jewish customs and religion. He was sent to Jerusalem, where he studied under
52
Gamaliel, a famous teacher of the Law. Under Gamaliel, Saul became well-versed in the Law of
Moses and traditions of the fathers.
Because of his strict upbringing, Saul grew into a zealous Pharisee, passionate for the Jewish
faith and determined to defend it against anything he considered a threat.
When Paul was arrested later in his ministry, he reminded the authorities of his background: his
Jewish identity, his Roman citizenship, and his education under Gamaliel. These qualifications
gave him respect both among Jews and Romans, and also helped in spreading the gospel across
both Jewish and Gentile communities.
Lessons from Paul’s nativity and education:
1. God can use our background and training for His purpose.
2. A good education prepares one for future responsibilities.
3. Religious zeal without true knowledge can lead to wrong actions.
4. Roman citizenship helped Paul in his mission, showing that every privilege can serve
God’s work.
PAUL’S PERSECUTION OF THE CHURCH (ACTS 7:57–58; 8:1–3; 9:1–3)
Saul’s zeal as a Pharisee made him a fierce persecutor of the early Christians.
In Acts 7:57–58, Saul was present at the stoning of Stephen, the first Christian martyr. Though
he did not throw stones himself, he kept the garments of those who killed Stephen, showing his
support for the act.
In Acts 8:1–3, Saul actively persecuted the church. He went from house to house, dragging men
and women out and putting them in prison. His mission was to destroy the followers of Christ
and wipe out Christianity from Jerusalem.
In Acts 9:1–3, Saul went further, requesting letters from the High Priest to the synagogues in
Damascus so that he could arrest any followers of “the Way” (Christians) and bring them bound
53
to Jerusalem. It was on this journey that he later encountered the risen Christ and his life was
transformed.
Lessons from Paul’s persecution of the Church:
1. Zeal without knowledge leads to violence and error.
2. Persecution of Christians cannot stop God’s work.
3. God can transform even the worst persecutor into a great servant.
4. Christians must be prepared to endure persecution for their faith.
5. God’s grace is greater than man’s sins.
SUMMARY
 Paul (Saul) was a Jew from Tarsus, a Roman citizen, and educated under Gamaliel in
Jewish law (Acts 22:1–5, 26–30).
 His background made him a zealous Pharisee, but he initially used this zeal to persecute
the church.
 He approved of Stephen’s stoning and arrested Christians, attempting to destroy the
church (Acts 7:57–58; 8:1–3).
 On his way to Damascus to arrest more Christians (Acts 9:1–3), his encounter with Christ
began his transformation.
 His life shows that God can change anyone and use their background for His glory.

THE COUNCIL AT JERUSALEM (Acts 15:1–35)
1. The Dispute:
o Some men from Judea came down to Antioch teaching the brethren that unless
they were circumcised according to the custom of Moses, they could not be saved.
o This teaching caused confusion and disagreement among the believers.
o Paul and Barnabas strongly opposed this view, leading to sharp disputes.
59
o The church at Antioch decided to send Paul, Barnabas, and some others to
Jerusalem to consult the apostles and elders concerning the matter.
2. The Meeting at Jerusalem:
o The apostles and elders gathered to discuss the issue.
o There was much debate, but Peter stood up and reminded them that God had
chosen him to preach to the Gentiles, and God gave them the Holy Spirit just as
He gave the Jews.
o He stressed that salvation comes through the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ and
not through the law of Moses or circumcision.
o Paul and Barnabas also testified of the miracles and wonders God had done
among the Gentiles through them.
o James, the leader of the Jerusalem church, gave the final judgment: Gentiles
should not be burdened with the full law of Moses but should abstain from things
polluted by idols, fornication, eating strangled animals, and blood.
3. The Decision and Its Outcome:
o A letter was drafted and sent with Paul, Barnabas, Judas (called Barsabbas), and
Silas to the Gentile churches.
o The letter encouraged the Gentiles and freed them from unnecessary burdens.
o The believers at Antioch rejoiced when they received the message.
o This decision united the church and resolved the conflict peacefully.
WRONG WAYS OF RESOLVING CONFLICTS
 Resorting to violence, fighting, or quarrelling.
 Spreading rumours or gossiping about the issue.
 Insulting, mocking, or abusing others.
60
 Taking laws into one’s own hands instead of following due process.
 Being rigid and refusing to listen to other people’s opinions.
 Keeping malice or withdrawing from fellowship.
 Using force, intimidation, or threats.
RIGHT WAYS OF RESOLVING CONFLICTS
 Dialogue and discussion: Listening to all sides before making a decision.
 Consulting elders and leaders: Seeking advice from wise and experienced people, just
as the apostles and elders did in Jerusalem.
 Prayer and guidance of the Holy Spirit: Asking God for wisdom to know the right step.
 Patience and tolerance: Being calm and willing to understand other people’s viewpoints.
 Compromise where necessary: Agreeing on what is fair and just for everyone.
 Love and forgiveness: Approaching issues with humility and readiness to forgive.
 Use of written agreements or official letters: As seen in the council’s letter to the
Gentiles.
LESSONS FROM THE COUNCIL AT JERUSALEM
 Disputes and conflicts are natural in the church and society, but they should be resolved
peacefully.
 Leadership and guidance are necessary in handling sensitive matters.
 Salvation is by faith in Jesus Christ, not by observing human traditions or laws.
 Collective decision-making helps to promote unity in the church.
 Conflicts, if properly resolved, can strengthen the bond among believers.
61

FUNCTIONS OF CHRISTIAN ORGANISATIONS
1. Promote unity among Christians.
2. Preach the gospel and defend the faith.
64
3. Provide education, health, and humanitarian services.
4. Speak with one voice on national issues.
5. Encourage Christians to live peacefully among non-Christians.
INTERDENOMINATIONAL COOPERATION
 Refers to collaboration between different Christian denominations (Catholic,
Anglican, Baptist, Methodist, Pentecostal, etc.) for common goals.
 Areas of cooperation:
1. Joint evangelism and missionary activities.
2. Ecumenical services and prayer meetings.
3. Establishment of schools, hospitals, and charities.
4. Speaking out on social, political, and moral issues.
5. Providing relief materials during crises.
 Benefits: reduces division, fosters love, presents a united front to the world, and
strengthens the witness of the Church.
CHRISTIAN GIVING (PHIL. 4:14–20; 2 COR. 8:3–5; 9:6–15)
Key Teachings:
 Giving should be voluntary, cheerful, and sacrificial.
 Christians should support ministers of the gospel and those in need.
 God rewards cheerful givers with spiritual and material blessings.
 Giving demonstrates love, unity, and obedience to God.
 Paul commended the Philippians for supporting him financially (Phil. 4:14–20).
65
 The Macedonian Christians gave beyond their ability to support others (2 Cor. 8:3–5).
 Giving is compared to sowing seed – the more one sows, the more one reaps (2 Cor. 9:6–
15).
TEACHINGS THAT FOSTER UNITY
 Love one another (John 13:34–35).
 Forgive and reconcile with one another (Matt. 18:21–22).
 Bear one another’s burdens (Gal. 6:2).
 Avoid divisions and unnecessary disputes (1 Cor. 1:10–13).
 Recognise that all believers are members of one body in Christ (Rom. 12:4–5).
 Work together in humility and peace (Eph. 4:1–6).
CHRISTIANS LIVING IN THE COMMUNITY
 Christians should be examples of good citizenship and moral behaviour.
 Show love and tolerance towards non-Christians.
 Contribute positively to community development.
 Promote peace, justice, and fairness in all dealings.
 Avoid discrimination, tribalism, and hatred.
 Live in such a way that non-Christians will glorify God.
CHRISTIAN ATTITUDE TO PERSECUTION
 Persecution means hostility, suffering, or unfair treatment because of one’s faith.
66
 Jesus taught that His followers will face persecution but should rejoice because their
reward is great in heaven (Matt. 5:10–12).
 Attitudes expected of Christians:
1. Endure persecution with faith and patience.
2. Do not retaliate with evil (Rom. 12:17–21).
3. Pray for those who persecute you (Matt. 5:44).
4. Remain steadfast in faith even in trials.
5. Take comfort that persecution strengthens the Church.
