JSS 2 HORTICULTURE AND CROP PRODUCTION LESSON NOTES
FIRST TERM
WEEK 1: REVIEW OF JSS 1 WORK
Topic: Review of JSS1 Work
Content: Recap of farmland preparation and nursery practices; Discussion on importance of soil
and crop selection.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define horticulture and crop production.
2. List the five main steps of farmland preparation in the correct order.
3. Define "nursery" and state two reasons for its importance.
4. Explain the importance of soil as a medium for plant growth.
5. List three factors to consider when selecting a crop to plant.
2
Lesson Content:
1. Recap of Basic Definitions
 Horticulture: The art and science of cultivating garden crops, especially fruits,
vegetables, flowers, and ornamental plants.
 Crop Production: The large-scale practice of growing agricultural crops (like maize,
rice, cassava, yam) for food, feed, or industrial use.
 Soil: The upper layer of the earth's crust, which serves as a natural medium for plant
growth. It provides anchorage, water, and nutrients.
2. Recap of Farmland Preparation This involves all the activities a farmer performs on the
land before planting. The goal is to make the soil suitable for seed germination and plant growth.
 A. Site Selection: Choosing the best piece of land based on factors like:
o Topography: The land should be flat or gently sloping to prevent erosion.
o Soil Type: The soil must be suitable for the chosen crop (e.g., sandy-loam for
vegetables).
o Water Access: Must be near a reliable source of water (river, stream, well).
 B. Land Clearing: Removing unwanted vegetation (trees, bushes, grasses) from the site.
This can be done manually (cutlass, axe) or mechanically (bulldozer).
 C. Stumping: The removal of tree stumps (the roots left in the ground after clearing).
This is essential if the farmer plans to use machines like tractors.
 D. Tillage (Ploughing, Harrowing):
o Ploughing: The first "breaking" of the soil to dig it up, loosen it, and bury weeds.
o Harrowing: The second "breaking" of the soil to break the large soil clods into a
finer, smoother, and more level seedbed.
 E. Ridging/Bed Making: Gathering the tilled soil into mounds (ridges) or flat-topped
beds.
o Importance: Provides deeper loose soil for root growth, controls erosion, and
allows for easy water management.
3. Recap of Nursery Practices
3
 Definition: A nursery is a special, small plot of land where young plants (seedlings) are
raised with extra care before they are moved to the permanent farm.
 Crops that require a nursery: Tomato, Pepper, Cabbage, Lettuce, Onion, Cocoa, Oil
Palm.
 Importance of a Nursery:
1. Good Germination: Provides the best conditions for tiny seeds to sprout.
2. Care of Seedlings: It is easier to water, weed, and protect a small nursery than a
large farm.
3. Selection of the Fittest: Allows the farmer to select only the healthy, strong
seedlings for transplanting, which ensures a good yield.
4. Saves Space: The main farm can be used for other crops while the seedlings are
growing in the nursery.
4. Importance of Soil and Crop Selection
 Why Soil is Important:
o Anchorage: Holds the plant's roots firmly in the ground.
o Nutrients: Supplies all the essential minerals (Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium)
for growth.
o Water: Holds the water that plants absorb through their roots.
o Air: Provides oxygen for the roots to breathe.
 Why Crop Selection is Important:
o Climate: You must plant crops that suit your climate (e.g., don't plant apples in
the hot tropics).
o Soil Type: Plant crops that match the soil (e.g., plant cassava in sandy soil, rice in
clay/loamy soil).
o Economic Value: Plant crops that are in high demand and will make a profit.
o Pest/Disease Resistance: Choose crop varieties that are known to resist common
diseases.

WEEK 2: FARM TOOLS AND MACHINES
Topic: Farm Tools and Machines
Content: Types and examples of farm machinery; Functions of simple and complex machines;
Safety and maintenance practices.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Differentiate between simple farm tools, complex machines, and farm machinery.
2. List at least three examples of simple, complex, and machine-powered implements.
3. State the specific function of a tractor, plough, harrow, and planter.
4. List five general maintenance practices for farm tools and machines.
5. State five safety rules to observe when using farm machines.
6
Lesson Content:
1. Classification of Farm Equipment
 A. Simple Farm Tools:
o Definition: Simple hand-held tools that are not powered by animals or engines.
They are powered by human energy.
o Examples: Cutlass (clearing), Hoe (weeding, making ridges), Rake (levelling,
gathering trash), Shovel/Spade (lifting soil), Hand Fork (loosening soil), Hand
Trowel (transplanting), Watering Can (watering).
 B. Complex Machines (Animal-Drawn):
o Definition: Implements that are more complex than hand tools and are designed
to be pulled by farm animals (e.g., donkeys, oxen, camels).
o Examples: Animal-drawn Plough, Cart, Ridger.
 C. Farm Machinery (Engine-Powered):
o Definition: Heavy-duty, engine-powered machines that perform farm operations
on a large, commercial scale.
o The Tractor: This is the prime mover. It is the "engine on wheels" that provides
the power to pull or operate other machines (implements).
o Tractor-Coupled Implements:
 Plough: Used for the primary tillage (first breaking) of hard soil.
 Harrow: Used for secondary tillage (breaking soil clods into a fine
seedbed).
 Ridger: Used for making large ridges.
 Planter: Used for planting seeds in rows automatically.
 Harvester: Used for harvesting crops (e.g., combine harvester for
maize/rice).
 Sprayer: Used for the large-scale application of fertilizers, herbicides, or
pesticides.
2. Functions of Key Farm Machinery
7
1. Tractor: Provides the pulling power (called "traction") and engine power (via a Power
Take-Off or "PTO" shaft) to run other machines.
2. Plough (Mouldboard Plough): Its large curved blade cuts deep into the soil and inverts
it (turns it upside down) to bury weeds and loosen the soil.
3. Harrow (Disc Harrow): A set of sharp, spinning discs that cut the large clods left by the
plough into smaller, finer pieces, making the land smooth for planting.
4. Planter (Seed Drill): A machine that automatically:
o Opens a small furrow (hole) in the soil.
o "Drills" or drops seeds at a specific spacing.
o Covers the seeds with soil.
3. Safety and Maintenance Practices
 A. Safety Rules (Personal and Operational):
1. Training: Never operate a machine (especially a tractor) without proper training.
2. PPE (Personal Protective Equipment): Always wear safety boots, gloves, and
ear protection when operating loud machinery.
3. No Passengers: Never allow people to ride on the tractor or implements unless
there is a proper seat.
4. Moving Parts: Keep hands, feet, and loose clothing away from all moving parts
(belts, chains, shafts).
5. Refuelling: Never refuel a hot engine or while smoking.
6. Guards: Ensure all safety guards and shields are in place before starting.
 B. Maintenance Practices (Keeping Tools in Good Condition):
1. Cleaning: Clean all tools and machines after each use to remove soil and
moisture.
2. Sharpening: Regularly sharpen the cutting edges of tools (cutlass, hoe, plough
blades).
3. Oiling/Greasing: Apply oil or grease to all moving parts and metal surfaces to
prevent rust and reduce friction.
4. Proper Storage: Store tools in a cool, dry, and secure tool shed.
8
5. Check Engine (for machinery): Regularly check the engine oil, water (coolant),
and fuel levels before starting a tractor.

5. List three maintenance practices for farm tools.

WEEK 3: SOIL FERTILITY AND IMPROVEMENT
Topic: Soil Fertility and Improvement Content: Meaning of soil fertility; Causes of loss of
fertility; Methods of improving soil fertility (organic/inorganic); Compost making.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "soil fertility."
2. List at least five essential plant nutrients.
3. Explain three ways soil fertility is lost.
4. Differentiate between organic manures and inorganic (chemical) fertilizers.
5. Describe the process of making compost.
10
Lesson Content:
1. Meaning of Soil Fertility
 Definition: Soil fertility is the ability of the soil to provide all the essential nutrients in
the right amounts and proportions, and support plant growth to produce a high yield.
 A fertile soil is not just "rich in nutrients." It must also have:
o Good texture and structure (e.g., loamy)
o Good water-holding capacity.
o Good aeration (air spaces for roots).
o A healthy population of soil organisms (like earthworms and bacteria).
 Plant Nutrients: Plants are like humans; they need food. These "foods" are called
nutrients.
o Macronutrients (needed in large amounts): Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P),
Potassium (K).
o Secondary Nutrients: Calcium (Ca), Magnesium (Mg), Sulphur (S).
o Micronutrients (needed in tiny amounts): Iron (Fe), Zinc (Zn), Boron (B), etc.
2. Causes of Loss of Soil Fertility Soil can "lose its power" or become infertile in several ways:
1. Leaching: This is the "washing down" of soluble nutrients (especially Nitrogen) deep
into the soil by heavy rainfall, moving them beyond the reach of plant roots.
2. Soil Erosion: The "washing away" or "blowing away" of the fertile topsoil by water or
wind. This is the worst form of fertility loss.
3. Over-cropping / Continuous Cropping: Planting the same crop on the same land year
after year. The crop will "use up" all of its specific favourite nutrients (e.g., maize uses a
lot of Nitrogen).
4. Overgrazing: Allowing too many animals (cows, goats) to graze on a piece of land.
They eat all the grass, leaving the soil bare and exposed to erosion.
5. Bush Burning: Burning the farm after clearing. This kills valuable soil micro-organisms
and releases nutrients (like Nitrogen) into the air as gas.
3. Methods of Improving Soil Fertility We can "feed" the soil to make it fertile again.
11
 A. Organic Manures (Natural):
o Definition: Fertilizers made from natural, living (or once-living) materials. They
improve both nutrient content and soil structure.
o Examples:
 Animal Manure: (Poultry droppings, cow dung, goat manure).
 Green Manure: Planting specific leafy plants (like legumes) and then
ploughing them into the soil while they are still green.
 Compost: Decomposed (rotted) mixture of plants and animal waste.
 B. Inorganic/Chemical Fertilizers (Artificial):
o Definition: Man-made chemicals that are produced in a factory to supply specific
nutrients. They are fast-acting but do not improve soil structure.
o Examples:
 NPK 15:15:15: A "complete" fertilizer containing Nitrogen, Phosphorus,
and Potassium.
 Urea: Provides only Nitrogen.
 SSP (Single Superphosphate): Provides only Phosphorus.
 C. Cultural Practices:
o Crop Rotation: Planting different types of crops on the same land in a specific
order (e.g., Maize -> Beans -> Yam). This helps to replace nutrients and control
pests.
o Cover Cropping: Planting "cover crops" (like legumes) to cover the soil and
prevent erosion.
4. How to Make Compost (The Indore Method)
 Definition: Compost is a dark, crumbly, nutrient-rich soil-like material made from the
decomposition (rotting) of organic waste.
 Materials:
o Greens (Nitrogen-rich): Kitchen waste (fruit/veg scraps), fresh grass clippings,
animal manure.
o Browns (Carbon-rich): Dry leaves, dry grass, paper, small twigs.
o Other: Water, soil (to add micro-organisms).
12
 Process:
1. Site: Choose a shaded, well-drained spot.
2. Layer 1: Start with a 15cm layer of "Browns" (twigs, dry leaves) for air flow.
3. Layer 2: Add a 15cm layer of "Greens" (kitchen waste, fresh grass).
4. Layer 3: Add a thin layer of "Browns" (dry leaves).
5. Layer 4: Add a thin layer of animal manure or soil (this adds the bacteria to start
the decomposition).
6. Water: Sprinkle water on the pile (it should be damp, not soaking).
7. Repeat: Repeat layers (Browns -> Greens -> Manure/Soil -> Water) until the pile
is about 1 meter high.
8. Turn: Use a shovel to turn or mix the pile every 2-3 weeks. This provides the
oxygen needed for fast decomposition.
9. Ready: The compost is ready in 2-4 months when it is dark, crumbly, and smells
like fresh earth.
Evaluation:
1. What is "soil fertility"?
2. What are the three "macronutrients" (N, P, K)?
3. Explain two ways soil can lose its fertility.
4. What is the difference between organic manure and inorganic fertilizer?
5. What are the "Greens" and "Browns" used for in compost making?
Assignment:
1. Define Leaching, Soil Erosion, and Over-cropping.
2. What is "green manure"?
3. What is "NPK 15:15:15"?
4. Why is it important to "turn" a compost pile?
5. (Practical) Start a small compost pile or bucket in the school garden. Write down the (1)
Greens and (2) Browns that you added to it.

WEEK 4: LAND PREPARATION TECHNIQUES
Content: Clearing, tilling, and leveling; Irrigation and drainage systems; Crop rotation practices.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Review the steps of land preparation (clearing, tilling, levelling).
2. Define "irrigation" and list two common methods.
3. Define "drainage" and state its importance.
4. Define "crop rotation" and give an example of a 3-course rotation.
5. Explain two reasons why crop rotation is beneficial.
14
Lesson Content:
1. Review of Land Preparation (from Week 1)
 Clearing: Removing vegetation (bushes, trees) using cutlasses, axes, or bulldozers.
 Tillage: The mechanical "working" of the soil to prepare it for planting.
o Primary Tillage (Ploughing): Deep breaking of the soil.
o Secondary Tillage (Harrowing): Breaking soil clods into a fine, level seedbed.
 Levelling: Making the soil surface flat and smooth to ensure uniform planting and water
distribution.
 Ridging/Bed Making: Forming the soil into raised mounds (ridges) or beds.
2. Water Management: Irrigation and Drainage Water is essential, but too much or too little
is bad.
 A. Irrigation:
o Definition: The artificial application of water to farmland in order to supply
crops with the water they need during periods of low rainfall (the dry season).
o Methods:
1. Surface Irrigation (Flooding): Allowing water to flow over the surface
of the land. (Common in rice paddies).
2. Sprinkler Irrigation: Spraying water over the crops like "artificial rain,"
using sprinklers.
3. Drip/Trickle Irrigation: Using pipes with small holes (emitters) that drip
water slowly, directly onto the base of each plant. This is the most
efficient method as it wastes very little water.
4. Manual Irrigation: Using a watering can (for small gardens/nurseries).
 B. Drainage:
o Definition: The removal of excess or stagnant water (waterlogging) from
farmland.
o Importance: Most plant roots cannot survive in waterlogged soil because they
cannot get oxygen and will rot.
15
o Methods:
1. Surface Drainage: Digging open ditches or channels (furrows) to allow
excess water to flow away by gravity.
2. Sub-Surface Drainage: (Advanced) Placing perforated pipes
underground to collect and drain the water.
3. Crop Rotation Practices
 Definition: Crop rotation is the practice of planting different types of crops in a planned
sequence on the same piece of land over several years.
 It is the opposite of continuous cropping (planting maize on the same land every single
year).
 Principle: Different crops have different needs and effects on the soil.
o Legumes (e.g., Beans, Cowpea, Groundnut): Add Nitrogen to the soil (via root
nodules).
o Cereals (e.g., Maize, Rice): Use a lot of Nitrogen.
o Root Crops (e.g., Yam, Cassava): Use a lot of Potassium and open up the soil.
 Example of a 3-Course Rotation (3 Years):
o Year 1: Plant Maize (uses lots of Nitrogen).
o Year 2: Plant Beans (adds Nitrogen back to the soil).
o Year 3: Plant Yam (uses Potassium and benefits from the Nitrogen left by the
beans).
o Year 4: Repeat the cycle (back to Maize).
4. Benefits of Crop Rotation
1. Soil Fertility Improvement: The rotation (especially with legumes) naturally replaces
soil nutrients, reducing the need for expensive chemical fertilizers.
2. Pest and Disease Control: Many pests and diseases attack only one type of crop. By
changing the crop, the pest's life cycle is broken (they starve or die off).
3. Weed Control: Different crops have different planting times and canopies, which helps
to disrupt the growth cycles of specific weeds.
16
4. Improved Soil Structure: Rotating deep-rooted crops (like yam) with shallow-rooted
crops (like vegetables) helps to improve soil structure and water absorption.
Evaluation:
1. What is the difference between primary tillage and secondary tillage?
2. Define "irrigation."
3. What is "drip irrigation," and why is it efficient?
4. What is "drainage," and why is it important?
5. What is "crop rotation"?
Assignment:
1. Define Irrigation, Drainage, and Crop Rotation.
2. List two methods of irrigation and one method of drainage.
3. Give two major benefits of practicing crop rotation.
4. Design a simple 3-course crop rotation for a farm, using crops common in your area.
5. Why is it bad to plant maize on the same piece of land every single year? (This is

WEEK 6: SITE SELECTION FOR CROP PRODUCTION
Topic: Site Selection for Crop Production
Content: Factors affecting crop site selection; Land use and suitability; Environmental and
economic factors.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "site selection."
2. List three climatic factors to consider during site selection.
3. List three soil (edaphic) factors to consider.
4. List three economic factors to consider.
5. Explain how "land use" affects site selection.
18
Lesson Content:
1. What is Site Selection?
 Definition: Site selection is the critical process of evaluating and choosing the best
possible piece of land on which to establish a farm for growing specific crops.
 Importance: A good site = high yield and high profit. A bad site = low yield, crop failure,
and loss of money.
2. Factors Affecting Crop Site Selection There are three main categories of factors to consider:
 A. Climatic Factors (The Weather):
1. Rainfall: Does the area receive enough rainfall for the crop? Is the rainfall
reliable? If not, is there a source for irrigation?
2. Temperature: Is the area too hot or too cold for the crop? (e.g., Cabbage prefers
cooler temperatures, while yams need heat).
3. Sunlight: Does the land receive at least 6-8 hours of direct sunlight per day?
(Most crops need full sun). Is the land shaded by mountains or large buildings?
4. Wind: Is the area prone to very strong winds that could damage crops?
 B. Soil (Edaphic) Factors:
1. Soil Type (Texture): Is the soil sandy (drains too fast), clayey (holds too much
water), or loamy (the best, a balanced mix)?
2. Soil Fertility: Is the soil rich in nutrients? (A soil test can determine this).
3. Soil Depth: Is the soil deep enough for roots to grow? (e.g., You can't grow yams
on shallow, rocky soil).
4. Topography (Slope): The land should be flat or gently sloping. Very steep land is
bad because it leads to rapid soil erosion.
 C. Biotic Factors:
1. Pests and Diseases: Is the area known to have a high population of specific pests
(like stem borers) or diseases (like cassava mosaic virus)?
2. Weeds: Is the land covered in very difficult-to-remove weeds (like spear grass)?
19
 D. Economic and Social Factors (The "Business" Factors):
1. Proximity to Market: How far is the farm from the market where you will sell
your crops? (If it's too far, transport costs will "eat" all your profit).
2. Transportation: Is there a good road to the farm, or is it a bush path?
3. Labour: Is there a good supply of skilled farm workers in the local village or
town?
4. Security: Is the area safe from thieves (who steal crops) or stray animals (cows,
goats) that can destroy the farm?
3. Land Use and Suitability
 Land Use: What is the land currently being used for?
 Suitability: The farmer must check if the previous land use has damaged the land (e.t., if
it was a mechanic workshop, the soil might be polluted with oil and unsuitable for
vegetables). The land must be suitable for agriculture.
 Government policies (e.g., "This land is for residential use only") can also affect site
selection.
Evaluation:
1. What is "site selection"?
2. List two climatic factors and two soil factors that affect site selection.
3. What does "topography" mean, and why is it important?
4. Why is "proximity to market" an important economic factor?
5. Why is a very steep slope bad for farming?
Assignment:
1. List the four main categories of factors for site selection.
2. Explain "proximity to market" and "labour supply" as economic factors.
3. What is a "soil test," and how can it help in site selection?
4. (Practical) Imagine you have to start a school vegetable garden. Walk around your school.
o (a) Choose the best site.

WEEK 8: CROP SPACING AND PLANTING METHODS
Topic: Crop Spacing and Planting Methods
Content: Meaning and importance of crop spacing; Methods of planting (direct, transplanting);
Plant population and density.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "crop spacing" and "plant population."
2. State three reasons why correct crop spacing is important.
3. Differentiate between "direct planting" and "transplanting."
4. Give two examples of crops for each planting method.
5. Explain the relationship between spacing and plant population.
22
Lesson Content:
1. Meaning of Crop Spacing
 Crop Spacing: This is the specific distance kept between one plant and another on a
farm, both along the row (intra-row) and between the rows (inter-row).
 Examples:
o Maize: 75cm between rows, 25cm along the row.
o Yam: 1m x 1m (1m between rows, 1m along the row).
 The spacing is different for every crop.
2. Importance of Correct Crop Spacing Giving plants the "correct space" is crucial.
 If spacing is too wide:
1. Wastes Land: You will have a low plant population and a low total yield.
2. Encourages Weeds: The open, sunny spaces will be quickly covered by weeds.
 If spacing is too close (overcrowding):
1. Competition: Plants will "fight" each other for limited resources (sunlight, water,
nutrients).
2. Weak, Lanky Plants: Plants will grow very tall and thin (lanky) trying to reach
for sunlight, and will be weak.
3. Low Yield: The individual plants will be stressed and produce very small fruits or
seeds.
4. Spreads Disease: Air cannot move freely between plants, so fungal diseases
spread easily in the damp, crowded conditions.
 Correct spacing ensures that every plant gets enough resources to grow strong and
produce a maximum yield.
3. Plant Population and Density
 Plant Population: The total number of plants in a given area (e.g., one hectare).
23
 Formula: Plant Population = (Total Farm Area) / (Spacing Area)
o Example: How many maize plants in a 100m x 100m (10,000 m²) field, using a
1m x 1m spacing?
o Plant Population = 10,000 m² / (1m x 1m) = 10,000 plants.
 Plant Density: How crowded the plants are. This is controlled by spacing.
4. Methods of Planting There are two main ways to put a plant in the field.
 A. Direct Planting (or Direct Sowing):
o Definition: This is when the seeds are planted directly into the permanent
farmland where they will grow and mature.
o Methods:
 Broadcasting: Scattering the seeds randomly by hand (e.g., rice, some
vegetables). It is fast but wasteful.
 Drilling: Planting the seeds in a straight, continuous line (furrow).
 Row Planting: Planting the seeds at specific, spaced-out positions along a
row (the best method).
o Crops: Maize, Cowpea (Beans), Yam, Cassava, Groundnut, Okra.
 B. Transplanting:
o Definition: This is a two-stage process. The seeds are first planted in a nursery
(see Week 1). After the seedlings are strong (e.g., 3-4 weeks), they are carefully
uprooted and transplanted (moved) to the permanent farm.
o Crops: Tomato, Pepper, Cabbage, Onion, Lettuce, Rice (often).
 Hardening Off: The process of gradually acclimatizing nursery seedlings to the harsh
conditions of the open field (e.g., by reducing watering) before transplanting. This
reduces "transplanting shock."
Evaluation:
1. What is "crop spacing"?
2. Give two reasons why "overcrowding" (spacing too close) is bad for crops.
3. What is "direct planting"?
24
4. What is "transplanting"?
5. Give one example of a crop that is transplanted.
Assignment:

2. A farmer plants yams with a spacing of 1m x 1m. How many yam plants can fit on a
piece of land that is 10m x 10m? (Show your calculation).
3. What is the main advantage of "transplanting" over "direct planting"? (Hint: Nursery).
4. What is "broadcasting," and what is its main disadvantage?

WEEK 9: WEED MANAGEMENT
Topic: Weed Management
Content: Meaning and types of weeds; Effects of weeds; Methods of weed control (manual,
chemical, biological).
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "weed" in an agricultural context.
2. List three common examples of weeds.
3. Explain three harmful effects of weeds on crops.
4. Differentiate between manual, chemical, and biological weed control.
5. Give one example of each control method.
26
Lesson Content:
1. Meaning and Types of Weeds
 Definition: A weed is any plant growing where it is not wanted.
o A maize plant in a yam farm is a weed.
o A tomato plant in a rice paddy is a weed.
 Weeds are plants that are very successful at competing with farm crops.
 Common Examples:
o Spear Grass (Imperata cylindrica)
o Goat Weed (Ageratum conyzoides)
o Pigweed (Amaranthus species)
o Water Leaf (Talinum triangulare)
o Tridax (Tridax procumbens)
2. Harmful Effects of Weeds (Why Weeds are Bad) Weeds are a major problem for farmers
because they reduce crop yield.
1. Competition: Weeds compete with crops for all essential resources:
o Sunlight
o Water
o Soil Nutrients
o Space
o (Weeds are usually better at competing than the crops).
2. Alternate Hosts: Weeds can serve as alternative hosts (a "hiding place") for crop pests
and diseases. (e.g., Insects hide in the weeds and then attack the crops).
3. Reduced Crop Quality: The presence of weed seeds in harvested grain (like rice)

3. Methods of Weed Control
27
 A. Manual / Mechanical Control (Physical):
o Definition: Using physical labour or tools to remove or destroy weeds.
o Methods:
 Hand-Pulling: Physically pulling the weeds out by hand. (Effective in
gardens/nurseries).
 Hoeing: Using a simple hoe to cut the weeds. (Most common method in

 Slashing: Using a cutlass to cut the weeds down.
 Mulching: Covering the soil surface with a layer of material (like dry
grass, wood chips, or plastic sheets) to block sunlight so weed seeds
cannot germinate.
 B. Chemical Control:
o Definition: Using chemicals called herbicides to kill weeds.
o Types of Herbicides:
 Pre-emergence: Applied to the soil before the weeds or crops sprout.
 Post-emergence: Applied to the leaves of the weeds after they have
started growing.
o Advantages: Fast, effective on large farms, reduces labour.
o Disadvantages: Can be expensive, can pollute the environment, requires training
to use safely.
 C. Biological Control:
o Definition: Using a weed's natural enemies (living organisms) to control its
population.
o This is not about using cows or goats, as they will eat the crops too!
o Method: Scientists find a specific insect or disease that only eats the target weed
and does not eat the crop. They then release this insect into the farm.
o Advantage: Very safe for the environment, long-lasting.
o Disadvantage: Very slow, very complex to research.
 D. Cultural Control:
o Using good farming practices to prevent weeds from growing.

o Example: Planting at the correct spacing (see Week 8) so the crop leaves
(canopy) quickly cover the ground and shade out the weeds.

WEEK 1: NURSERY PRACTICES
Content: Preparation of nursery materials; Seed sowing and care; Transplanting techniques.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Lesson Content:
1. Review of Nursery
 Definition: A nursery is a special, well-prepared plot of land (or collection of containers)
where young plants (seedlings) are raised with intensive care before they are moved to
the permanent farm.
 Nursery Practices: These are all the activities involved in establishing and managing a
nursery to ensure the production of healthy, strong seedlings.
37
2. Preparation of Nursery Materials and Site
 Site Selection: The site for a nursery must:
o Be near a reliable water source.
o Be on a flat, well-drained piece of land.
o Be in a secure area, safe from pests and farm animals.
o Have fertile, loamy soil.
 Tools: Hand trowel, hand fork, rake, watering can, measuring tape, pegs, and ropes.
 Materials:
o Seeds: High-quality, viable seeds of the chosen crop (e.g., tomato, pepper).
o Soil: Rich, loamy soil, often mixed with well-decomposed manure (compost).
o Shade: Palm fronds, grass, or shade nets to protect seedlings from harsh sun.
o Water: Clean water.
 Bed Preparation:
1. Clear the chosen site of all weeds and stones.
2. Till the soil (using a hoe, hand fork) to a fine tilth (soft, crumbly texture).
3. Mark out the bed (e.g., 1m wide and any desired length).
4. Create a raised bed (about 15-20cm high) by raking the tilled soil. This ensures
good drainage.
5. Level the top of the bed with a rake.
3. Seed Sowing and Care
 Seed Sowing:
1. Drills: Use a small stick to create shallow, straight rows (drills) on the nursery
bed, about 10-15cm apart.
2. Sowing: Sow the tiny seeds thinly along the drills. Do not overcrowd them.
3. Covering: Cover the seeds lightly with a thin layer of fine soil.
 Post-Sowing Care (Routine Management):
38
1. Mulching: Cover the bed with a thin layer of dry grass or leaves. This keeps the
soil moist, prevents erosion, and stops the seeds from being splashed out.
(Remove the mulch immediately after germination).
2. Watering: Water the bed gently every day (morning and evening) using a
watering can with a "rose" head to prevent splashing.
3. Shading: Construct a shade (canopy) over the bed using palm fronds. This
protects the tender seedlings from direct, hot sun and heavy rain.
4. Weeding: Remove any weeds by hand as soon as they appear. Weeds compete
with the seedlings.
5. Thinning: If seedlings are overcrowded, pull out the weaker ones to give the
strong ones more space to grow.
6. Hardening Off: This is the process of gradually acclimatizing the seedlings to the
harsh conditions of the permanent farm.
 How: About 1-2 weeks before transplanting, gradually reduce the amount
of water and remove the shade for longer periods each day. This makes the
seedlings tough.
4. Transplanting Techniques
 Definition: Transplanting is the careful process of moving a seedling from the nursery
bed to its permanent position on the farm (ridge or field).
 Procedure:
1. Timing: Transplanting is best done in the late evening or on a cool, cloudy day to
reduce water loss (transpiration) and shock.
2. Watering: Water the nursery bed thoroughly about an hour before transplanting
to make the soil soft and easy to dig.
3. Uprooting: Use a hand trowel to dig around and under a seedling. Lift it out
carefully with a ball of earth around its roots. Never pull a seedling by its stem.
4. Planting: Plant the seedling in the prepared hole on the permanent ridge. The
hole should be deep enough to cover the ball of earth.
5. Firming: Press the soil gently but firmly around the base of the seedling to
remove air pockets and ensure root-soil contact.
39
6. Watering: Water the newly transplanted seedling immediately.

WEEK 2: SEED GERMINATION
Topic: Seed Germination
Content: Meaning and process; Conditions necessary for germination; Experiment on
germination.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "seed" and "germination."
2. Draw and label the parts of a seed (e.g., a bean seed).
3. List the three essential conditions necessary for germination.
4. Describe the process of germination, step by step.
5. Set up a simple experiment to prove the conditions for germination.
41
Lesson Content:
1. Meaning and Process
 Seed: A seed is a small embryonic plant enclosed in a protective outer covering called a
seed coat, usually with some stored food. It is the unit of reproduction for flowering
plants.
 Germination: This is the process by which a dormant (sleeping) seed begins to grow and
develop into a young plant (seedling) when all the right conditions are met.
2. Parts of a Seed (Example: Bean Seed)
 Testa (Seed Coat): The tough, protective outer layer.
 Micropyle: A tiny pore (hole) in the seed coat through which water enters.
 Cotyledon(s): The "seed leaves." They store food (starch, protein) for the embryo.
(Beans have two, maize has one).
 Embryo: The "baby plant" itself, which consists of:
o Plumule: Develops into the shoot (stem and leaves).
o Radicle: Develops into the root.
3. Conditions Necessary for Germination For a viable (living) seed to germinate, it must have
three external conditions:
1. Water (Moisture):
o Water enters through the micropyle and softens the testa.
o It "wakes up" the dormant embryo and activates the enzymes.
o It dissolves the stored food in the cotyledons so the embryo can use it.
2. Oxygen (Air):
o Once "awake," the embryo begins to respire (breathe).
o Respiration is the process of "burning" the stored food (like a tiny engine) to
produce energy for growth.
o Food + Oxygen → Energy + Carbon Dioxide + Water
42
o If a seed is buried too deep or is in waterlogged soil, it gets no oxygen and will
"drown" or rot.
3. Warmth (Suitable Temperature):
o Germination is a chemical process. All chemical reactions need a suitable
temperature to work.
o Most crops (like maize, beans) prefer a warm temperature.
o If it is too cold or too hot, the seed will not germinate.
(Note: Light is not needed for germination. In fact, most seeds germinate best in the dark,
underground.)
4. The Process of Germination (in a Bean Seed)
1. The seed absorbs water through the micropyle.
2. The testa softens and swells.
3. The embryo is activated and uses oxygen to respire.
4. The radicle (root) grows downwards into the soil to anchor the seedling and absorb more
water.
5. The plumule (shoot) grows upwards, pulling the cotyledons with it, towards the light.
6. Once in the light, the cotyledons open up, and the first true leaves appear.
7. The seedling now starts photosynthesis (making its own food), and the cotyledons shrivel
and fall off as their food store is used up.
5. Experiment: To Show the Conditions Necessary for Germination
 Aim: To prove that seeds need Water, Oxygen, and Warmth.
 Apparatus: 4 glass jars/cups, cotton wool, viable seeds (e.g., beans, maize).
 Procedure: Set up 4 jars as follows:
o Jar A (Control): Contains damp cotton wool + seeds. Kept in a warm place
(room temperature). (Has Water, Oxygen, Warmth).
o Jar B (No Water): Contains dry cotton wool + seeds. Kept in a warm place. (Has
Oxygen, Warmth; No Water).
43
o Jar C (No Oxygen): Contains damp cotton wool + seeds. Pour boiled, cooled
water on top and cover with a layer of oil (to stop air from dissolving). Kept in a
warm place. (Has Water, Warmth; No Oxygen).
o Jar D (No Warmth): Contains damp cotton wool + seeds. Kept in a very cold
place (e.g., a refrigerator). (Has Water, Oxygen; No Warmth).
 Observation: After 3-4 days, you will observe...
 Result:
o Jar A: Seeds germinate.
o Jar B, C, D: Seeds do not germinate.
 Conclusion: This proves that all three conditions (Water, Oxygen, and Warmth) are all
necessary for germination.
Evaluation:
1. What is "germination"?
2. What is the function of the cotyledon?
3. What is the function of the radicle?
4. List the three essential conditions for germination.
5. Why is oxygen needed for germination?
Assignment:

2. Why is light not necessary for germination?
3. Describe the entire process of germination, from water absorption to the seedling stage.

Lesson Content:
1. Review of Fertilizer Types (from Term 1)
 Organic Fertilizers (Manures):
o Source: Natural (animal waste, decomposed plants - compost).
o Action: Slow-release of nutrients.
o Benefit: Improves soil structure, water-holding capacity, and nutrients.
o Examples: Poultry manure, cow dung, compost.
 Inorganic Fertilizers (Chemical):
o Source: Man-made, factory-produced chemicals.
o Action: Fast-release of specific nutrients.
o Benefit: Provides a quick "boost" to plants. Does not improve soil structure.
o Examples: NPK 15:15:15, Urea (provides Nitrogen).
2. Timing of Fertilizer Application
 When to Apply: Fertilizer is not applied during planting. The seed has its own food
(cotyledon).
 First Application (Top-Dressing): Applied to the soil around the young crop, usually 2-
4 weeks after germination (e.g., for maize, tomato). This gives the young plant a boost
for vegetative (leafy) growth.
 Second Application: Often applied just before the flowering or fruiting stage to provide
energy for producing fruits/seeds.
 Key Rule: Apply fertilizer when the soil is moist (e.g., after rain) so the nutrients can
dissolve and be absorbed. Never apply to dry, hard soil.
3. Methods of Fertilizer Application
 1. Broadcasting:
o Definition: The scattering of fertilizer (usually granular) evenly over the entire
surface of the farm.
46
o When to Use: Best for crops that are planted very close together (e.g., rice, wheat,
lawns) or for applying manure before ploughing.
o Disadvantage: Can be wasteful, as much of it falls where there are no roots.
 2. Ring Application (or Spot Application):
o Definition: Applying fertilizer in a circular band (ring) around the base of an
individual plant.
o Procedure:
1. Make a small, shallow trench (ring) about 5-10cm away from the plant's
stem.
2. Sprinkle the correct dose of fertilizer into the ring.
3. Cover the fertilizer lightly with soil.
o Why? Placing it away from the stem prevents the "burning" (scorching) of the
plant. Covering it prevents the sun from destroying the nutrients (especially
Nitrogen).
o When to Use: Best for row crops like maize, tomato, pepper, and fruit trees.
 3. Foliar Application:
o Definition: Mixing a liquid fertilizer with water and spraying it directly onto the
leaves of the plant.
o Action: The leaves absorb the nutrients (especially micronutrients) very quickly.
o When to Use: This is used as a quick-fix to correct a nutrient deficiency, not as
the main feeding method.
o Tool: Knapsack sprayer.
 4. Band Application: Placing the fertilizer in a "band" or "row" beside the row of seeds
during planting (done by a mechanical planter).
4. Safety Precautions (for Chemical Fertilizers)
1. Read the Label: Always read and follow the manufacturer's instructions for dosage and
safety.
2. Wear PPE: Wear gloves, boots, and a simple nose/mouth mask to avoid skin contact and
inhaling the dust.
3. No Eating/Smoking: Never eat, drink, or smoke while handling fertilizers.
47
4. Wash Hands: Wash your hands and face thoroughly with soap and water after
application.
5. Wind Direction: Apply in the direction of the wind (so it blows away from you, not into
your face).
6. Storage: Store fertilizers in a cool, dry, locked shed, away from food, water, and children.

WEEK 4: VEGETATIVE PROPAGATION
Topic: Vegetative Propagation
Content: Meaning and importance; Methods of propagation: grafting, layering, cutting; Crops
that use each method.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "vegetative propagation."
2. Differentiate between sexual propagation (seeds) and asexual (vegetative) propagation.
3. List three main advantages of vegetative propagation.
4. Define and describe "cutting" and give one crop example.
5. Define and describe "grafting" and "layering" and give crop examples.
Lesson Content:
1. Meaning of Vegetative Propagation
 Sexual Propagation: This is the normal way plants reproduce, using seeds. This
involves the fusion of male and female gametes, so the offspring is a mix of two parents.
49
 Asexual (Vegetative) Propagation: This is the artificial method of growing a new plant
from a vegetative part (like a stem, root, or leaf) of a single parent plant.
 The new plant is an exact copy (a "clone") of the parent plant.
2. Importance (Advantages) of Vegetative Propagation
1. Genetic Uniformity: The new plant is an exact clone of the parent. This means a sweet
orange tree will produce only sweet oranges. (With seeds, you might get a sour orange).
2. Faster Maturity: Plants grown from cuttings or grafts will produce fruit much faster
(e.g., 2-3 years) than plants grown from seed (e.g., 7-10 years).
3. Propagation of Seedless Fruits: This is the only way to grow fruits that do not have
seeds (e.g., some types of oranges, grapes, bananas).
4. Pest/Disease Resistance: You can combine a strong plant with a tasty plant (see
Grafting).
3. Methods of Vegetative Propagation
 A. Cutting:
o Definition: This is the simplest method. A piece of the parent plant's stem, root,
or leaf is cut off and planted in moist soil. It develops its own roots and grows into
a new plant.
o Example (Stem Cutting):
1. Cut a 15-20cm piece of healthy stem from the parent plant (e.g., Cassava).
2. Plant it directly in the field, often at an angle.
o Crops: Cassava (most common), Sugarcane, Yam (cuttings of the tuber),
Pineapple (the crown), Sweet Potato (vines).
 B. Layering:
o Definition: This is the method of forcing a stem to grow roots while it is still
attached to the parent plant.
o Methods:
50
 Simple Layering: A low-growing, flexible branch is bent down, pegged
into a small hole in the soil, and covered with soil (leaving the tip
exposed).
 Air Layering (Marcotting): Used for branches that are too high to bend.
A small ring of bark is removed from the branch. This "wound" is covered
with a ball of moist soil or moss and wrapped tightly with a plastic sheet.
o Result: Roots will grow from the "wounded" part of the stem. Once the new roots
are strong, the branch is cut off from the parent and planted.
o Crops: Citrus (Orange, Lemon), Mango, Guava, Cocoa.
 C. Grafting:
o Definition: This is the advanced technique of joining the parts of two different
plants so they grow as one single plant.
o Parts:
 Stock (Rootstock): The bottom part (the rooted stem). It is chosen for its
strong roots and disease resistance.
 Scion: The top part (a small bud or shoot). It is chosen for its high-quality
fruit (e.g., sweet taste).

2. Key Maintenance Practices

3. Increases Fruit Size: By removing some excess flowers or small fruits,

o Pests: Insects, rats, birds.
o Diseases: Fungi, bacteria, viruses.

 Physical: Hand-picking pests, traps, fences.
 Chemical: Spraying insecticides (for insects) or fungicides (for fungi).
 IPM: Using a smart combination of all methods.

WEEK 8: HARVESTING AND STORAGE
Topic: Harvesting and Storage
Content: Techniques of harvesting; Post-harvest handling and preservation.

Lesson Content:
1. Harvesting
 Definition: Harvesting is the process of gathering or collecting mature crops from the
farm.
 Maturity: This is the stage when the crop is ready to be harvested.
o Physiological Maturity: The plant has completed its growth (e.g., grain is hard,
fruit is sweet).
o Harvest Maturity: The specific time the crop is harvested to meet market
demands (e.g., some bananas are harvested green for shipping).
 Timing: Harvesting must be done at the right time.
o Harvesting too early (immature): Crop quality is low, it won't store well.
o Harvesting too late (over-mature): Crop may rot, get eaten by pests, or spoil.
 Harvesting Techniques (Methods):
o Manual (by hand):
 Picking: For fruits and vegetables (e.g., tomato, pepper, okra, oranges).
 Digging (Lifting): For root/tuber crops (e.g., yam, cassava, potato) using

 Cutting: For cereals or vegetables (e.g., rice, sugarcane, cabbage) using a

o Mechanical (by machine):
 Using Harvesters (e.g., Combine Harvester for maize/rice, Tomato

2. Post-Harvest Handling
 Definition: This includes all the activities that happen immediately after harvesting,
before the crop is stored or sold.
 Process:
1. Gathering: Bringing the crops from the farm to one central point.
58
2. Cleaning: Washing (e.g., carrots) or "threshing" (e.g., beating beans to remove
pods) to remove dirt, stones, and unwanted parts.
3. Sorting & Grading: Separating the crops based on quality, size, or ripeness (e.g.,
Grade 1 tomatoes are big and red; Grade 2 are small).
4. Packaging: Putting the crops into bags, crates, or baskets for easy transport.
 Post-Harvest Losses: A large amount of food (30-50%) is lost in Nigeria after
harvesting.
 Causes of Loss:
o Mechanical Injury: Bruising, cutting, or breaking the crop during careless
harvesting and transport.
o Pest/Disease: Pests (weevils) and fungi (mould) attacking the harvested crop.
o Environmental: Rotting or sprouting due to high heat and humidity.
3. Storage and Preservation
 Storage: The process of keeping farm produce safe for future use, in a way that maintains
its quality.
 Preservation: The specific treatment or process used to stop food from spoiling (rotting,
decaying).
 Methods of Preservation (Traditional and Modern):
1. Drying (Dehydration):
 Method: Removing water from the food. (Micro-organisms cannot grow
without water).
 Examples: Sun-drying of maize, beans, pepper, okra; smoking of
fish/meat.
2. Refrigeration / Freezing (Cold Storage):
 Method: Using low temperatures to slow down the growth of bacteria and
fungi.
 Examples: Storing fresh tomatoes, fruits, and vegetables in a refrigerator
or cold room.
3. Canning / Bottling:
59
 Method: Sealing the food in an airtight container (can or bottle) and then
heating it to kill all micro-organisms.
 Examples: Canned tomatoes, canned sweet corn, bottled fruit juices.
4. Use of Chemicals:
 Method: Adding preservatives like salt, sugar, or vinegar.
 Examples: Salting fish; sugaring to make fruit jams; pickling with vinegar.
5. Barns and Silos:
 Method: Large, specialized structures for storing grain (silos) or tubers
(yam barns). They are designed to be cool, dry, and rodent-proof.

Lesson Content:
1. Meaning and Importance of Marketing
 Definition: Agricultural Marketing includes all the business activities involved in
moving a farm product (like tomatoes) from the farm to the final consumer.
 It is not just "selling." It includes:
o Harvesting
o Grading and Sorting
o Packaging
o Transportation
o Storage
o Processing (e.g., turning oranges into juice)
o Advertising and Sales
 Importance of Marketing:
1. Provides Income: It is the only way a farmer gets paid for their hard work.
2. Prevents Spoilage: An efficient marketing system (with good storage and
transport) moves perishable crops (like tomatoes) to the consumer before they
spoil.
3. Links Producer and Consumer: It bridges the gap between the farmer in the
village and the consumer in the city.
4. Creates Jobs: Marketing provides jobs for drivers, packagers, wholesalers,
retailers, and advertisers.
2. Channels of Distribution
 Definition: A channel of distribution is the path or route that a product takes to get from
the producer (farmer) to the final consumer.
 Key "Middlemen" (Intermediaries):
o Wholesaler (Distributor): Buys in very large quantities from many farmers,
stores the produce, and sells in large quantities to retailers.
62
o Retailer: Buys in large quantities from the wholesaler and sells in small
quantities (e.g., 1kg, 5 tomatoes) directly to the final consumer. (e.g., The local
market woman).
 Common Channels:
o Channel 1 (Shortest):
 Producer (Farmer) → Final Consumer
 (e.g., A farmer at a roadside stall).
o Channel 2 (Most Common):
 Producer (Farmer) → Retailer → Final Consumer
o Channel 3 (Longest):
 Producer (Farmer) → Wholesaler → Retailer → Final Consumer
3. Problems of Agricultural Marketing in Nigeria
1. Poor Transportation: Bad roads damage crops during transport, increase costs, and
cause delays, leading to spoilage.
2. Lack of Storage Facilities: Farmers have no cold rooms or proper silos. This forces
them to sell immediately at harvest time when prices are low. This leads to "seasonal
scarcity" (food is cheap at harvest, very expensive later).
3. Poor Packaging: Using rough baskets and raffia crushes and bruises crops (especially
tomatoes).
4. Too Many Middlemen: In Channel 3, every middleman adds their own profit. This
makes the food very expensive for the final consumer, but the farmer still gets paid very
little.
5. Lack of Market Information: Many farmers in villages don't know the real price of
their crops in the city, so middlemen cheat them.
4. Solutions to Marketing Problems
1. Government: Repairing rural roads.
63
2. Cooperatives: Farmers should form cooperatives (groups) to buy their own storage
(silos) and transport (trucks), so they can "cut out" the middlemen and sell directly to
retailers, keeping more profit.
3. Technology: Using mobile phones to check market prices before selling.
4. Improved Packaging: Using plastic or wooden crates for transport.

1. Importance and Types of Vegetables
 Definition: Vegetables are the edible parts of plants, such as the leaves, stems, roots,
tubers, or fruits, that are commonly consumed by humans. Horticulture is the branch of
agriculture that deals with the intensive cultivation of vegetables, fruits, and flowers.
 Importance of Vegetables:
o Nutritional Importance: They are a primary source of essential vitamins (e.g.,
Vitamin A for sight, Vitamin C for skin/immunity) and minerals (e.g., Iron for
blood, Calcium for bones). They also provide dietary fibre (roughage), which is

 Examples: Spinach (Efo, Amunututu), Fluted Pumpkin (Ugu), Waterleaf,

72
1. Soil: They need deep, fertile, well-drained loamy soil that is rich in organic matter
(compost or manure). Heavy clay soil or pure sandy soil is not ideal.
2. Climate (Temperature & Sunlight): Most vegetables require warm temperatures and
full, direct sunlight (at least 6-8 hours a day) for photosynthesis.
3. Water: Vegetables have shallow roots and need a consistent, regular, and reliable
supply of water. They cannot tolerate drought. Access to irrigation (drip, sprinkler, or
manual) is essential.
4. Site: The land must be flat or gently sloping (to prevent erosion) and located in a secure
area (to prevent theft or damage by animals).
3. Production Stages (Example: Tomato Production)
1. Site Selection: Choose a sunny, well-drained site with fertile soil, near a water source.
2. Land Preparation: Clear the land, till the soil (plough and harrow) to a fine tilth, and
make raised beds or ridges. Mix well-rotted manure or compost into the soil.
3. Propagation (Planting):
o Tomatoes are first planted in a nursery (See Term 2, Week 1).
o Seeds are sown in drills in the nursery, watered, and shaded.
o Seedlings are "hardened off" after 3-4 weeks.
o Transplanting: Seedlings are transplanted to the permanent beds in the evening,
at a spacing of about 60cm x 60cm.
4. Maintenance Practices:
o Watering: Water every 1-2 days if there is no rain.
o Weeding: Remove weeds regularly, either by hand or with a hoe.
o Fertilizer Application: Apply NPK fertilizer using the "ring method" (See Term
2, Week 3) about 2-3 weeks after transplanting.
o Staking: Provide a 1-2m stake (stick) for each plant to climb on. This keeps the
fruits off the ground, prevents disease, and makes harvesting easy (See Term 2,
Week 6).
o Pruning: (Optional) Removing some of the side shoots ("suckers") to encourage
the plant to produce fewer, but larger, fruits.
73
o Pest/Disease Control: Watch for insects (like fruit borers) and diseases (like
blight). Spray with appropriate (safe) insecticides or fungicides if necessary.
5. Harvesting:
o Tomatoes are ready for harvest about 2-3 months after transplanting.
o They are harvested by hand-picking the mature fruits (which can be green-mature
or fully red, depending on the market).
o Harvesting is done every 3-5 days for several weeks.

2. Sorting, Grading, and Packaging
 A. Sorting:
o Definition: This is the process of removing unwanted materials from the
harvested produce.
o Process: It involves separating the good produce from the bad produce.
o Items Removed:
 Diseased, rotten, or pest-damaged produce.
 Immature or over-ripe produce.
 Damaged or bruised produce (from harvesting).
 Foreign materials (e.g., dirt, stones, leaves, weeds).
 B. Grading:
o Definition: This is the next step after sorting. It involves categorizing the good
produce into different "grades" or classes based on quality.
o Criteria for Grading:
1. Size: e.g., Grade 1 (Large), Grade 2 (Medium), Grade 3 (Small).
2. Colour: e.g., Fully red tomatoes vs. partially red tomatoes.
3. Firmness/Texture: e.g., Hard, firm fruits vs. slightly soft ones.
4. Freedom from Blemishes: e.g., Perfect skin vs. skin with small spots.
o Importance: Grading increases the value of the produce. A farmer can sell
"Grade 1" tomatoes at a much higher price to hotels, while selling "Grade 2" at a
lower price in the open market.
 C. Packaging:
o Definition: The process of enclosing the graded produce in a container for
protection, handling, transport, and marketing.
o Importance: Good packaging protects the produce from being crushed, bruised,
or contaminated.
o Packaging Materials:
 Traditional (Bad): Raffia baskets, woven bags. They are cheap but offer
poor protection; fruits are easily crushed.
76
 Modern (Good):
 Plastic Crates (Perforated): Strong, stackable, and allow for air
circulation (ventilation). Ideal for tomatoes, peppers.
 Wooden Crates: Strong, good for transporting yams or citrus.
 Net Bags: Used for onions, potatoes, cabbage (allows ventilation).
 Cardboard Cartons: Used for high-value or export crops.
3. Storage Methods
 Definition: Storage is the process of keeping farm produce in a safe and controlled
environment to preserve its quality and prevent spoilage until it is needed.
 A. Short-Term Storage (Days to Weeks):
1. Cool, Dark, Ventilated Place: Many vegetables (like yams, potatoes, onions) can
be stored for a few weeks in a cool, dry, and dark room with good air movement.
2. Refrigeration (Cold Storage): Using refrigerators or "cold rooms" (at 0-10°C).
This is the best method for highly perishable leafy greens, tomatoes, and fruits. It
slows down respiration and the growth of micro-organisms.
 B. Long-Term Storage (Months to Years) (Processing/Preservation):
1. Drying (Dehydration): Removing water to stop microbes from growing.
 Examples: Sun-drying of peppers, okra, or leafy vegetables (to make
"Gbure").
2. Canning / Bottling: Sealing the produce (e.g., tomatoes, sweetcorn) in an airtight
can or jar and heating it to kill all bacteria.
3. Processing: Changing the form of the produce.
 Examples: Processing tomatoes into paste/puree; processing peppers into
dry chili powder.

1. Meaning of Entrepreneur and Entrepreneurship
 Entrepreneur: An entrepreneur is a person who identifies a business opportunity (a
problem to solve or a need to fill), gathers the resources (money, people, materials), and
starts a business to provide a product or service, taking on financial risks in the hope of
making a profit.

 Entrepreneurship: This is the process or act of being an entrepreneur—the act of setting

 Agripreneur: A modern term for an entrepreneur in agribusiness.
2. Qualities (Characteristics) of a Good Entrepreneur What makes someone a successful
entrepreneur?
1. Risk-Taker: They are willing to risk their time, money, and energy on an idea that might
fail.
2. Innovative/Creative: They think of new ideas, new products, or new ways of doing
things. (e.g., "Instead of just selling raw tomatoes, I will process them into chili sauce!").

employees.

powder!"

3. Importance of Entrepreneurship in Agriculture Why does a country need agripreneurs?
1. Job Creation: When an entrepreneur starts a new farm or food processing company, they
hire people (farm managers, drivers, factory workers, sales staff). This reduces
unemployment.
2. Food Security: Agripreneurs produce food on a large scale, ensuring that there is enough
food for the nation's population.
3. Development of Rural Areas: Most agricultural businesses are in rural areas. When a
factory or farm is set up, it brings roads, electricity, and development to that village.
4. Increases Gross Domestic Product (GDP): New businesses create new wealth, which
makes the entire country richer.
5. Reduces Post-Harvest Losses: Entrepreneurs (processors) buy raw produce from
farmers and turn it into products with a long shelf-life (e.g., fruit juice, yam flour), thus
saving food that would have rotted.
6. Introduction of New Technology: Entrepreneurs are often the first to adopt new
technologies (like drip irrigation or modern silos) to make farming more efficient.

Lesson Content:
1. What is Financial Management?
 Definition: Financial management is the process of planning, organizing, controlling,
and monitoring the financial resources (the money) of a business, in order to achieve its
goals.
 In simple terms: It is "smart money management" for your business.
2. The First Rule: Separate Business and Personal Money
 This is the most important rule for a small entrepreneur.
 Problem: A farmer gets N5,000 from selling vegetables. He uses N1,000 for his child's
school fees, N2,000 for food, and N500 for airtime. He only has N1,500 left. He thinks he
is "making money," but he has no money to buy fertilizer for the next season. His
business will die.
 Solution: The business must have its own money.
1. Open a separate bank account for the business (or use a separate "savings box").
2. All N5,000 from sales goes into the business account.
3. The farmer pays himself a small "salary" (e.g., N1,000) from the business account
to his personal pocket.
4. The remaining N4,000 stays in the business account, ready to buy seeds, feed, and
fertilizer for the next cycle. This is called reinvestment.
3. Budgeting and Planning
 Income: All money that comes into the business (e.g., sales).
 Expenditure: All money that goes out of the business (e.g., costs, expenses).
 Budget: A budget is a plan for your income and expenditure before you spend the
money. It is a financial "roadmap" for a specific period (e.g., a month, a planting season).
 Budgeting: The process of creating this plan.
92
4. How to Create a Simple Farm Budget (Example: Budget for 1 poultry cycle - 6 weeks for
broilers)
 Goal: To raise 20 broiler chicks.
 1. Estimate Your Expenditures (Costs):
o Capital Costs (One-time):
 Feeder (1): N1,000
 Drinker (1): N800
o Operating Costs (Running):
 Day-old chicks (20 x N500): N10,000
 Feed (Starter, Finisher): N15,000
 Medication/Vaccines: N1,000
 Wood shavings: N500
o TOTAL ESTIMATED EXPENDITURE = N28,300
 2. Estimate Your Income (Sales):
o Goal: Raise 18 chicks successfully (assume 2 die).
o Sale Price: N3,500 per chicken (at 6 weeks).
o TOTAL ESTIMATED INCOME = 18 x N3,500 = N63,000
 3. Plan (Calculate Estimated Profit):
o Estimated Income: N63,000
o Estimated Expenditure: (N28,300)
o ESTIMATED PROFIT = N34,700
 Why is this budget useful?
1. It tells the farmer they need N28,300 before they can even start.
2. It shows the potential profit, making it a good business.
3. It acts as a guide for spending.
