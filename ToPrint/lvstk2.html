WEEK 1Introduction to Livestock Farming

Content: Meaning and importance of livestock farming; Scope; Subsistence vs. Commercial systems.

Performance Objectives

  1. Define livestock farming and explain its scope.
  2. Discuss the importance of livestock farming to Nigeria.
  3. Differentiate between subsistence and commercial livestock farming.

1.1 Meaning of Livestock Farming

Livestock farming is the scientific practice of breeding, raising, and managing domestic animals for food (meat, milk, eggs), fiber (wool, hides), labor (draught), and by-products (manure).

1.2 Importance of Livestock Farming

AreaImportance
NutritionalHigh-biological-value protein (meat, milk, eggs); vitamins (B12, A, D); minerals (iron, calcium).
EconomicIncome, employment, foreign exchange (hides/skins), raw materials for industry.
AgriculturalDraught power for crop farming; manure for soil fertility.
Social/CulturalSign of wealth; used for festivals, ceremonies, dowries, religious sacrifices.

1.3 Subsistence vs. Commercial Livestock Farming

FeatureSUBSISTENCECOMMERCIAL
GoalFeed the familyMaximize profit
ScaleSmall (5–20 animals)Large (hundreds–thousands)
TechnologyTraditional, free-rangeModern, intensive
BreedsLocal, hardyExotic, high-yielding
OutputLow, household consumptionHigh, market-oriented

WEEK 2History and Development of Livestock Farming in Nigeria

Content: Traditional systems; Modern methods; Government roles.

Performance Objectives

  1. Describe traditional livestock systems.
  2. Explain the shift to modern methods.
  3. State the roles of government in livestock development.

2.1 Traditional Systems

  • Nomadic Pastoralism: Fulani herders move cattle (Zebu) in search of pasture and water. Transhumance: seasonal movement from dry North to greener South.
  • Village Free-Range: Poultry, goats, sheep scavenge by day; simple shed at night. Low input, low output.

2.2 Modern Methods

  • Exotic Breeds: Holstein-Friesian (milk), Broilers/Layers (poultry), Large White/Landrace (pigs).
  • Intensive Housing: Battery cages, deep litter, concrete piggeries, raised slatted floors.
  • Veterinary Medicine: Mass production of vaccines (Newcastle, Gumboro, PPR).
  • Feed Formulation: Scientifically balanced rations.
  • Artificial Insemination (AI): Rapid genetic improvement.

2.3 Government Roles

RoleExamples
Research InstitutesNAPRI (Shika, Zaria) — animal production. NVRI (Vom, Plateau) — vaccines and disease research. KLDB (Kainji) — fisheries.
Development ProgramsADPs, Operation Feed the Nation (OFN).
Policy & InfrastructureGrazing reserves, import bans on frozen poultry, border quarantine.

Evaluation

  1. Describe nomadic pastoralism and transhumance.
  2. List three disadvantages of the free-range system.
  3. What are the functions of NAPRI and NVRI?

Assignment

  1. Write a 250-word essay on the challenges of nomadic pastoralism in Nigeria.
  2. Compare traditional and modern livestock systems in a table (Breeds, Housing, Feed, Disease Control).

WEEK 3Classification of Livestock

Content: Ruminants and non-ruminants; Examples and characteristics.

Performance Objectives

  1. Classify livestock based on digestive system.
  2. Describe the four compartments of the ruminant stomach.
  3. State the characteristics of ruminants and non-ruminants.

3.1 Ruminants

Definition: Mammals with a complex four-compartment stomach (polygastric). Examples: Cattle, goats, sheep.

CompartmentFunction
RumenFermentation vat; microbes break down cellulose.
ReticulumTraps heavy objects; aids rumination.
OmasumAbsorbs water; filters food.
Abomasum"True stomach"; secretes acid and enzymes.

Key Characteristics: Chew cud (rumination); digest cellulose; prone to bloat.

3.2 Non-Ruminants

Definition: Mammals with a simple, single-compartment stomach (monogastric). Examples: Pigs, poultry, rabbits.

Key Characteristics: Simple stomach; cannot digest cellulose; compete with humans for grains.

3.3 Pseudo-Ruminants

  • Rabbits and horses are non-ruminants but can digest fiber.
  • Rabbits have a large cecum that acts like a small rumen.
  • Rabbits practice coprophagy (eating soft night feces) to re-digest food.

Evaluation

  1. What does "polygastric" mean?
  2. List the four compartments of the ruminant stomach in order.
  3. Why can a cow survive on grass but a pig cannot?
  4. What is coprophagy?

Assignment

  1. Draw and label the four compartments of the ruminant stomach.
  2. Classify the following as ruminant or non-ruminant: cattle, pig, goat, chicken, rabbit, sheep, turkey.

WEEK 4Livestock Housing

Content: Types of housing for poultry, goats, sheep, cattle, pigs; Principles of good housing.

Performance Objectives

  1. State the principles of good livestock housing.
  2. Describe poultry housing systems (deep litter, battery cage).
  3. Explain housing for cattle, pigs, goats, and sheep.

4.1 Principles of Good Housing

  1. Orientation (East-West): Minimizes direct sun, keeping house cool.
  2. Ventilation: Removes ammonia and heat; provides oxygen.
  3. Space: Prevents overcrowding, stress, disease spread.
  4. Drainage: Sloped floor prevents water-logging.
  5. Security: Protects against predators and theft.
  6. Hygiene: Smooth concrete for easy cleaning.

4.2 Poultry Housing

  • Deep Litter: For broilers/growers; wood shavings litter; must be kept dry.
  • Battery Cage: For layers; sloped floor for egg collection; waste drops through wire.

4.3 Cattle Housing

  • Kraal: Traditional fenced enclosure.
  • Paddock: Fenced pasture for grazing.
  • Feedlot: Intensive outdoor pens for beef fattening.
  • Dairy Barn (Cubicle): Individual stalls for dairy cows.

4.4 Pig Housing (Piggery)

  • Strong concrete walls and floors.
  • Farrowing Pen: Contains a farrowing crate to prevent crushing of piglets.
  • Boar Pen: Individual pen for adult male.
  • Wallow: Shallow pool for cooling (pigs lack sweat glands).

4.5 Goat and Sheep Housing

  • Raised, Slatted Floor: House on stilts with gapped floor; waste falls through, preventing parasite contact.

Evaluation

  1. Why is East-West orientation important?
  2. Describe the deep litter system.
  3. What is a farrowing crate and why is it used?
  4. What is the advantage of a raised slatted floor?

Assignment

  1. Draw a labeled diagram of a deep litter house.
  2. Draw a labeled diagram of a battery cage system.
  3. Explain the importance of ventilation and drainage.

WEEK 6Animal Nutrition I: Classes of Feed

Content: Classes of feed (Energy, Protein, Minerals, Vitamins, Water); Examples of feed ingredients.

Performance Objectives

  1. Classify animal feed into Roughages, Concentrates, and Supplements.
  2. Define roughages and give examples.
  3. Distinguish between energy concentrates and protein concentrates.
  4. List the six classes of nutrients and their functions.
  5. Identify feed ingredients for each class.

6.1 Classes of Feed

Animal feeds are practically classified into three main groups based on their nutrient composition and fiber content.

Class 1: Roughages (Forages)

Definition: Feeds that are high in crude fiber (above 18%) and low in digestible energy.

Characteristics:

  • Bulky; low nutrient density.
  • Require microbial fermentation for digestion.
  • Mainly fed to ruminants (cattle, goats, sheep).

Examples: Fresh grass (Guinea, Elephant), Hay (dried grass), Silage (fermented grass), Straw, Legumes (e.g., centrosema), Tree leaves.

Importance: Provides fiber for rumen health; cheap; prevents bloat.

Class 2: Concentrates

Definition: Feeds that are low in crude fiber (below 18%) and high in digestible nutrients (energy or protein).

Sub-classes:

Sub-classFunctionExamples
Energy ConcentratesProvide high energy for growth, fattening, and work.Maize, Sorghum, Millet, Cassava, Wheat Offal.
Protein ConcentratesProvide protein for growth, milk, and egg production.Plant: Groundnut Cake (GNC), Soybean Meal, Palm Kernel Cake (PKC).
Animal: Fish Meal, Blood Meal.

Class 3: Supplements

Definition: Micro-ingredients added to feed in small quantities to supply essential minerals, vitamins, and other additives.

Examples:

  • Mineral Supplements: Bone Meal (Calcium, Phosphorus), Oyster Shell (Calcium), Salt Licks (Sodium).
  • Vitamin Supplements: Vitamin Premixes (A, D, E, K, B-complex).
  • Additives: Antibiotics, coccidiostats, antioxidants.

6.2 The Six Classes of Nutrients (within feed)

All feed classes supply these six essential nutrients in varying proportions.

NutrientFunctionMain Source
CarbohydratesPrimary energy source.Energy concentrates (maize), roughages (grass).
ProteinsGrowth, tissue repair, egg/milk production.Protein concentrates (GNC, fish meal).
Fats & OilsConcentrated energy (2.25× carbs); vitamin absorption.Palm oil, groundnut oil, tallow.
VitaminsRegulate body processes; "spark plugs."Green plants, synthetic premixes.
MineralsBone formation, blood, metabolism.Bone meal (Ca, P), salt licks (Na), premixes (Fe, I, Zn).
WaterTemperature regulation, nutrient transport, digestion.Clean water, ad libitum.

WEEK 8Animal Nutrition II: Balanced Ration, Feed Formulation & Storage

Content: Balanced ration; Factors affecting feed formulation; Feed storage.

Performance Objectives

  1. Define a balanced ration and explain why it is "specific."
  2. Explain the roughage-to-concentrate ratio for different animals.
  3. List factors affecting feed formulation.
  4. State the rules of proper feed storage.
  5. Explain the danger of Aflatoxin.

8.1 Balanced Ration

Definition: A ration is the total feed an animal receives in 24 hours. A balanced ration contains all six nutrients in the correct proportions for the animal's specific age and purpose.

Roughage-to-Concentrate Ratio (Important):

  • Ruminants (Cattle): 60:40 roughage:concentrate (high fiber needed for rumen).
  • Poultry & Pigs: Mostly concentrate; roughage is minimal (they cannot digest fiber).
  • Rabbits: 40:60 roughage:concentrate (they need fiber for cecum health).

Examples of specific rations:

  • Broiler Starter: High protein (23%) for rapid growth.
  • Broiler Finisher: Lower protein (19%), high energy for fattening.
  • Layer's Mash: Moderate protein, very high calcium for eggshells.

8.2 Factors Affecting Feed Formulation

FactorExplanation
Animal's SpeciesRuminant vs. non-ruminant; cow feed ≠ chicken feed.
Animal's AgeYoung need more protein; adults need more energy.
Purpose (Physiological State)Lactation (high protein, calcium); fattening (high energy); pregnancy (extra nutrients); work (high energy).
Cost & AvailabilityBusiness decision; substitute expensive ingredients with cheaper alternatives.
Health StatusSick animals may need medicated or highly digestible feed.

8.3 Feed Storage

Goal: Protect feed from moisture, pests, and sunlight.

Ideal Store: Dry, cool, well-ventilated, pest-proof.

Rules of Storage

  • Use Pallets: Never stack bags directly on the floor.
  • FIFO: First-In, First-Out.
  • Pest Control: Traps and seal holes.
  • Separate: Keep away from chemicals, drugs, fuel.

Aflatoxin

A toxic mold (Aspergillus) that grows on damp feed (especially GNC). It destroys the liver, stunts growth, and causes death. Major economic loss.

  3. List five factors affecting feed formulation.
  4. What is a pallet and why is it essential?
  5. What is Aflatoxin and why is it dangerous?

Assignment

  2. What does FIFO stand for and how is it implemented?

WEEK 9Animal Breeding I

Content: Natural breeding; Artificial insemination; Selection methods.

Performance Objectives

9.1 Selection Methods

MethodDescriptionPros/Cons
Mass SelectionSelect best-looking animals (phenotype).Simple; cannot select for hidden traits.
Pedigree SelectionSelect based on ancestors' records.Early selection; records may be inaccurate.
Progeny TestingSelect based on offspring performance.Most accurate; slow and expensive.

9.2 Natural Breeding

Definition: Male physically mates with female in heat.

Advantages: Simple, high conception rate.

Disadvantages: Disease transmission, injury risk, cost of keeping male, limited genetics.

9.3 Artificial Insemination (AI)

Definition: Semen collected from superior male, frozen, and manually inserted into female.

Advantages: Rapid genetic improvement, disease control, safety, access to global genetics.

Disadvantages: Skilled technician required, accurate heat detection, lower conception rate if mistimed.

WEEK 10Animal Breeding II

Content: Crossbreeding, inbreeding, line breeding; Advantages and disadvantages.

Performance Objectives

  1. Define a breeding system.
  2. Explain inbreeding and its dangers.
  3. Describe line breeding.
  4. Explain crossbreeding and hybrid vigor.

10.1 Inbreeding

Definition: Mating of very closely related animals (brother-sister, parent-offspring).

Purpose: Create purebreds; increase homozygosity.

Danger: Inbreeding Depression — low fertility, weak offspring, high mortality, deformities.

10.2 Line Breeding

Definition: Milder inbreeding (cousins, grandsire-granddaughter).

Purpose: Maintain influence of an outstanding ancestor without severe depression.

10.3 Crossbreeding

Definition: Mating of animals from two different breeds.

Result: Hybrid Vigor (Heterosis) — offspring better than average of parents.

Example: Muturu (hardy, disease-resistant) × Holstein-Friesian (high milk) = Hybrid that survives in Nigeria and produces good milk.

Evaluation

  2. What is inbreeding depression?
  3. Difference between inbreeding and line breeding.
  4. Define crossbreeding and hybrid vigor.

Assignment

  1. Write a 250-word essay comparing inbreeding depression to hybrid vigor.
  2. Draw family trees for inbreeding and line breeding.
  3. Name one crossbreed animal and explain its advantage.

Exam Strategy

  • Read the question carefully: Identify the key verb — "Define," "Explain," "Discuss," "Compare," "List." Your answer must match the command word.
  • Plan your answer: For essay questions, take a minute to jot down the main points you want to make before you start writing.
  • Use the "Define, Explain, Example" method: For "explain" or "discuss" questions, start with a clear definition, provide a detailed explanation, and then give a specific example to support your point.
  • Be specific: Use names, dates, and specific events (e.g., "NAPRI," "Transhumance," "Aflatoxin," "Farrowing Crate") to show depth of knowledge.
  • Structure your essay: Use an introduction, a body with clearly numbered points, and a brief conclusion.
