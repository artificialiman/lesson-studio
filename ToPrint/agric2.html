1.1 Definition of Farm Surveying

Farm surveying is the process of measuring and mapping out the physical features of a farmland, including its boundaries, elevations, slopes, and existing structures, to obtain data useful for planning and proper land use.

1.2 Definition of Farm Planning

Farm planning refers to the systematic arrangement and scheduling of farm resources—land, labour, capital, and equipment—to achieve set production goals efficiently.

1.3 Importance of Farm Surveying

  1. Boundary Determination: Establishes true boundaries, prevents encroachment and land disputes.
  2. Farm Layout: Provides data for designing roads, irrigation channels, fences, and drainage systems.
  3. Soil Conservation: Analyzes slope and elevation to implement terracing or contour plowing.
  4. Water Management: Reveals natural water flow patterns for irrigation and drainage design.
  5. Infrastructure Placement: Guides strategic placement of barns, silos, poultry houses, fish ponds.
  6. Resource Allocation: Assists in allocating land to various enterprises based on suitability.

1.4 Types of Farm Surveying

TypeDescription
a. Chain SurveyingMeasures linear distances using chains or tapes. Suitable for small, level plots. Tools: chain (20 or 30 m), arrows, ranging rods, pegs.
b. Compass SurveyingMeasures directions or bearings using a compass. Useful for uneven, vegetated, or irregular terrain. More accurate than chain surveying.
c. Plane Table SurveyingGraphical method where field observations are directly plotted on a drawing board. Uses alidade, compass, drawing tools. Reduces data transfer errors.
d. Theodolite SurveyingHighly accurate method using a theodolite to measure horizontal and vertical angles. Used for large-scale farm development (irrigation, terraces, roads).
e. Aerial SurveyingModern method using drones, aircraft, or satellites. Processed with GIS and photogrammetry. Ideal for large-scale commercial farms and precision agriculture.

1.6 Procedures in Conducting a Farm Survey

  1. Reconnaissance Survey: Preliminary inspection of the land to understand its physical characteristics and plan the survey strategy.
  2. Setting of Control Points: Establish fixed reference points marked with pegs or poles.
  3. Measurement: Collect actual data on distances between points using chains, tapes, or digital meters.
  4. Angle and Bearing Determination: Measure angles or bearings using compass or theodolite.
  5. Levelling: Determine relative heights or elevations of different points using dumpy level, Abney level.
  6. Recording and Plotting: Record all observations in a field book; plot data onto a chart or drawing paper.
  7. Preparation of Survey Plan: Prepare a scaled, detailed map showing boundaries, control points, structures, and elevation markers.

1.7 Farm Planning

Importance of Farm Planning:

  • Maximises land use.
  • Reduces waste (duplication of efforts, unnecessary overlap).
  • Enhances productivity (smoother operations, labour efficiency).
  • Facilitates mechanisation (adequate space and access roads).
  • Promotes safety (hazardous areas sited away from residential zones).
  • Environmental conservation (drainage, windbreaks, contour farming).

Factors to Consider in Farm Planning:

  1. Topography: Slope, elevation, drainage.
  2. Soil Type and Fertility: Sandy, loamy, clay; nutrient levels.
  3. Climate: Rainfall, temperature, humidity, wind direction.
  4. Water Availability: Rivers, streams, boreholes.
  5. Accessibility: Access roads and transportation networks.
  6. Security: Safety of crops, animals, and infrastructure.
  7. Land Tenure System: Ownership and legal rights over land use.

Components of a Farm Plan:

  • Residential Area
  • Cropland
  • Pasture/Grazing Area
  • Animal Pens
  • Storage Facilities
  • Irrigation and Drainage Systems
  • Access Roads
  • Processing Unit
  • Fencing

Types of Farm Layouts:

  • Ridge Layout: Raised ridges for root crops (yam, cassava, potatoes).
  • Bed Layout: Flat, raised sections for vegetables (onions, lettuce, carrots).
  • Contour Layout: Follows natural contours on sloping land to reduce erosion.
  • Block Layout: Farm divided into blocks for different enterprises (crops, livestock, agroforestry).

Types of Farm Plans:

  • Operational Plan: Short-term; daily, weekly, or seasonal activities.
  • Strategic Plan: Long-term; future development, expansion, new enterprises.
  • Budget Plan: Financial plan; expected income and expenditures.
  • Enterprise Plan: Specific to one enterprise (poultry, aquaculture).

2.1 Meaning of Farm Planning

Farm planning is the systematic process of arranging and allocating farm resources such as land, labour, capital, and equipment to maximise production and profit while ensuring sustainability. It involves setting clear goals, evaluating existing resources, choosing suitable enterprises, and making well-informed decisions.

2.2 Objectives of Farm Planning

  • Ensure effective and efficient use of available resources.
  • Guide the farmer in achieving higher productivity by choosing suitable enterprises.
  • Maximise profit through proper scheduling of farm operations.
  • Ensure sustainability—preserving natural resources for long-term use.
  • Support better financial management and decision-making.

2.3 Importance of Farm Planning

  1. Organised Farm Operations: Helps avoid confusion and duplication of efforts.
  2. Efficient Use of Resources: Ensures land, labour, and capital are allocated to areas where they will be most productive.
  3. Proper Budgeting and Record Keeping: Allows the farmer to track expenses, income, and productivity.
  4. Attraction of Credit Facilities: A good farm plan reflects seriousness and feasibility, helping farmers access loans.
  5. Integration of Modern Practices: Allows the integration of mechanisation, disease control, irrigation, and sustainable practices.

2.4 Factors to Consider in Farm Planning

FactorExplanation
i. Nature of the SoilTexture, structure, fertility, drainage, pH level influence crop type and farming system.
ii. Topography of the LandSlopes, hills, valleys, plains influence farm layout and choice of enterprises.
iii. ClimateRainfall, temperature, humidity, sunlight duration determine suitable crops and animals.
iv. Availability of CapitalDetermines scale of farming, inputs to purchase, and infrastructure.
v. Accessibility and TransportationFarms should be accessible by roads for movement of produce and inputs.
vi. Water SupplyReliable water source (rivers, boreholes, dams) for irrigation and livestock.
vii. Land Tenure SystemSecure land tenure encourages investment; uncertain rights discourage long-term planning.
viii. Labour AvailabilityNumber and type of workers available influence operations.
ix. Market DemandUnderstanding demand helps produce what is needed, reducing overproduction and losses.

2.5 Components of a Farm Plan

  1. Farmstead or Residential Area: Farmer's house and workers' quarters; centrally located for supervision.
  2. Crop Plots: Designated fields for crop cultivation based on soil fertility, crop rotation, accessibility.
  3. Livestock Pens: Housing for animals, designed for welfare, waste management, biosecurity; located downwind.
  4. Pasture Land: Reserved grazing areas; rotational grazing for sustainability.
  5. Water Sources and Irrigation Systems: Water storage tanks, irrigation pipes, pumps.
  6. Storage and Processing Facilities: Warehouses, silos, barns, processing units to reduce post-harvest losses.
  7. Access Roads: Internal and external roads for movement of people, inputs, and produce.
  8. Drainage and Erosion Control Structures: Drainage ditches, contour ridges.
  9. Waste Disposal Area: Composting for organic waste; proper disposal for non-biodegradable waste.
  10. Fencing: Demarcates boundaries and protects from intruders or stray animals.

2.6 Types of Farm Plans

  • Operational Farm Plan: Daily and seasonal activities (planting, weeding, fertilising, harvesting).
  • Strategic Farm Plan: Long-term (3–5 years); vision of the farm, major investments, expansion.
  • Financial or Budget Plan: Income, expenditures, profitability; crucial for loans.
  • Enterprise Plan: Specific to each crop or animal enterprise; inputs, expected output, timing, profitability.

2.7 Farm Layout versus Farm Planning

AspectFarm PlanningFarm Layout
DefinitionBroader process of deciding what to do and how to do it.Physical arrangement of farm structures and fields.
FocusDecision-making and strategy.Design and structure (spatial arrangement).
ScopeEncompasses all resources and activities.Deals with location of each component.

2.8 Steps in Farm Planning

  1. Survey the land to assess topography, soil type, drainage, boundaries.
  2. Set goals and objectives for the farm (profit targets, crop yields).
  3. Assess available resources (land, capital, labour, machinery).
  4. Select enterprises (crops/livestock) based on suitability and market demand.
  5. Design a layout of the farm indicating where each component will be located.
  6. Prepare a budget showing expected income and expenditure.
  7. Create a timetable of operations to guide farming activities.
  8. Implement the plan and begin operations.
  9. Monitor progress and make adjustments when necessary.

2.9 Problems of Farm Planning in Nigeria

  • Inadequate Capital: Limits ability to implement well-designed plans.
  • Lack of Formal Training: Many farmers lack technical know-how.
  • Unclear Land Tenure: Discourages long-term investment.
  • Poor Rural Roads: Complicates planning and implementation.
  • Limited Access to Extension Services: Farmers lack guidance.
  • Climate Variability: Unreliable weather data increases uncertainty.
  • Absence of Reliable Market Data: Reduces planning effectiveness.

2.10 Remedies to Planning Challenges

  • Governments and financial institutions should offer accessible credit and grants.
  • Strengthen extension services to educate farmers on planning techniques.
  • Reform land tenure policies to guarantee security and encourage investment.
  • Invest in rural infrastructure (roads, water supply).
  • Promote the use of ICT tools for planning, data analysis, and weather forecasting.

3.1 Meaning of Farmstead

A farmstead refers to the central hub of farm activities, usually comprising the residential area of the farmer, farm buildings, processing and storage facilities, equipment sheds, livestock pens, and other structures essential for farm operations.

3.2 Importance of Farmstead Planning

  • Improves efficiency and reduces cost.
  • Saves time and fuel by minimising unnecessary movement.
  • Ensures adequate drainage and reduces disease transmission.
  • Helps prevent environmental pollution.
  • Enhances security and facilitates future expansion.

3.3 Principles of Farmstead Planning

PrincipleExplanation
a. AccessibilityFarmstead should be easy to reach by road or footpath; all buildings connected by roads/footpaths.
b. CentralisationFarmstead placed centrally in relation to operational units; reduces distance and time.
c. Proximity to Water SupplyLocated close to a reliable water source (river, borehole, stream, well).
d. SecurityFenced or hedged; proper lighting, gates, security personnel.
e. Proper DrainageSited on slightly elevated, well-drained land; open drains, contour bunds, gravel walkways.
f. Future ExpansionPlan should include spaces for possible additions (more pens, processing facilities).
g. Separation of Noisy and Smelly UnitsPig pens, poultry houses, compost pits located away from residential and processing areas.
h. Grouping of Similar ActivitiesRelated functions located near one another (equipment sheds near workshops; storage barns near threshing areas).
i. Orientation to Sun and WindResidential and livestock buildings sited to receive adequate sunlight and cross ventilation.

3.4 Location of Buildings and Structures

Building/StructureLocation Considerations
a. Farmhouse/Residential BuildingSited on slightly raised ground; centrally located; close to main access road but away from noisy/polluted areas.
b. Livestock Pens and HousingDownwind of residential area; close to feed storage and water supply; adequate spacing for light, ventilation, cleaning.
c. Machinery and Equipment ShedClose to crop fields and farmhouse; firm, dry ground; workshops nearby for repairs.
d. Processing and Storage FacilitiesNear production areas but far from animal pens and waste disposal; accessible for trucks and carts.
e. Drainage ChannelsBuilt around all major buildings; connected to natural drainage lines or constructed ditches.
f. Roads and PathwaysConnect major buildings and fields; properly planned; well-compacted surface.
g. Fencing and BoundariesEntire farmstead fenced; gates at appropriate positions; hedges as natural barriers.

4.1 Meaning of Forest

A forest is a large expanse of land covered predominantly with trees, undergrowth, shrubs, and a wide variety of plant and animal species. Forests may be natural (virgin) or man-made (plantation).

4.2 Meaning of Forest Management

Forest management refers to the application of scientific, economic, and social principles in the administration, care, and use of forests to meet specific objectives—sustainable timber production, biodiversity conservation, water cycle regulation, or recreation.

4.3 Importance of Forest Management

  1. Conservation of Natural Resources: Conserves water, soil, biodiversity.
  2. Sustainable Production of Timber and Non-Timber Products: Ensures resources are harvested sustainably.
  3. Control of Climate and Air Quality: Carbon sequestration; mitigates climate change.
  4. Prevention of Flooding and Desertification: Forests absorb rainfall; act as barriers to desert encroachment.
  5. Employment and Revenue Generation: Timber processing, paper manufacturing, herbal medicine.
  6. Tourism and Recreation: Game reserves and national parks attract tourists.

4.4 Methods of Forest Management

MethodDescription
a. Selective ExploitationCutting only mature and economically valuable trees; leaves younger trees and non-commercial species.
b. AfforestationPlanting trees on land not previously forested; increases forest cover.
c. ReforestationPlanting trees to replace those removed by logging, farming, or natural disasters.
d. Taungya SystemCombines forestry with agriculture; farmers cultivate food crops between rows of young forest trees.
e. Forest ReservationSetting aside specific areas as protected forest reserves.
f. Controlled GrazingRestricting the number and movement of livestock within forest areas.
g. Regulation of Tree FellingEnforcing rules on when, where, and how trees may be felled; permits and replanting.

4.5 Roles of Government in Forest Management

  • Legislation and Regulation: Enacts forest laws to control land use, tree felling, and forest exploitation.
  • Establishment of Forest Reserves and Parks: Creates protected forest areas for conservation, tourism, research.
  • Public Awareness and Education: Sponsors educational programmes; includes environmental education in school curricula.
  • Support for Research and Development: Funds research institutes such as Forestry Research Institute of Nigeria (FRIN).
  • Provision of Incentives: Tax reliefs, grants, or land allocation to individuals and organisations engaged in afforestation and conservation.

5.1 Meaning of Agro-Forestry

Agro-forestry is an integrated land-use system where trees and shrubs are deliberately planted or retained on the same plot of land as crops and/or animals. The components interact positively to enhance productivity and sustainability.

5.2 Types of Agro-Forestry Systems in Nigeria

SystemDescription
a. Agrisilvicultural SystemAgricultural crops cultivated together with trees on the same piece of land. Trees may be planted in rows; crops grown between rows. Examples: Leucaena leucocephala, Gliricidia sepium, mango, guava.
b. Silvopastoral SystemTrees and pasture or animals integrated on the same land. Trees provide shade and fodder; grasses serve as grazing land. Suitable in northern Nigeria; prevents desertification and soil erosion.
c. Agrosilvopastoral SystemCombines crops, trees, and livestock on the same plot. Practised where land is limited. Example: cassava + maize + goats + neem/moringa.
d. Alley CroppingCrops planted in alleys between rows of leguminous trees/shrubs that are regularly pruned. Prunings serve as green manure or mulch. Trees: Gliricidia, Leucaena.
e. Taungya SystemTemporary cultivation of food crops alongside young forest trees during early years of a plantation. Practised in government forest reserves.

5.3 Importance of Agro-Forestry in Nigeria

  1. Improvement of Soil Fertility: Leguminous trees fix nitrogen; fallen leaves decompose to form humus.
  2. Erosion Control: Tree roots hold soil; canopies reduce raindrop impact.
  3. Biodiversity Conservation: Maintains tree cover; creates mini-ecosystems for birds, insects, microorganisms.
  4. Climate Change Mitigation: Trees absorb carbon dioxide; combat global warming.
  5. Economic Diversification and Food Security: Multiple income sources (timber, fruits, medicinal plants, fuelwood, food crops).
  6. Employment Creation: Jobs in tree nursery operations, pruning, harvesting, processing, livestock rearing.
  7. Sustainable Land Use: Maintains ecological balance; combats land degradation.

5.4 Challenges of Agro-Forestry in Nigeria

  • Lack of Awareness: Many farmers are not informed about agro-forestry practices.
  • Land Tenure Issues: Farmers reluctant to plant trees on land they do not legally own.
  • Inadequate Government Support: Limited funding, training, and extension services.
  • Competition Between Trees and Crops: Trees may compete for light, water, and nutrients.
  • Bush Burning and Overgrazing: Destroy young trees; make it hard to establish systems.

5.5 Solutions and Way Forward

  • Promote awareness through extension services and media.
  • Reform land tenure policies to encourage tree planting.
  • Provide government support (funding, training, inputs).
  • Proper planning to minimise tree-crop competition.
  • Enforce laws against bush burning and uncontrolled grazing.

6.1 Meaning of Ornamental Plants

Ornamental plants are cultivated primarily for decorative and aesthetic purposes rather than for food or other agricultural uses. They enhance the beauty of homes, gardens, parks, offices, schools, and public spaces. They include flowering plants, shrubs, trees, climbers, grasses, and groundcovers.

6.2 Importance of Ornamental Plants

ImportanceExplanation
1. Aesthetic and Beautification ValueImproves visual appeal of surroundings. Flowering plants (roses, lilies, marigolds) bring vibrant colours; foliage plants add greenery.
2. Environmental BenefitsAbsorb carbon dioxide, release oxygen; reduce noise pollution; act as windbreaks; stabilise soil; reduce urban heat island effect.
3. Psychological and Health BenefitsCreate serene atmospheres; reduce stress and anxiety; improve mood and productivity; promote faster recovery in patients.
4. Economic ValueHorticulture and landscaping industries provide employment (nursery operators, landscape designers, gardeners, florists). Ornamental plants are sold in nurseries.
5. Cultural and Religious SignificanceFlowers used in religious rituals, weddings, naming ceremonies, festivals. Hibiscus used in Nigerian communities.
6. Habitat for WildlifeShrubs and flowering trees provide food and shelter for birds, insects, bees, butterflies.
7. Education and RecreationGardens and parks for leisure, learning, nature appreciation. Botanical gardens visited by students, researchers, tourists.

WEEK 7Common Types of Ornamental Plants

Content: Detailed classification and examples of ornamental plants.

Performance Objectives

  1. List and explain seven types of ornamental plants.
  2. Give examples of each type.
  3. Differentiate between flowering plants and foliage plants.

7.1 Flowering Ornamental Plants

Grown for colourful, fragrant, or uniquely shaped flowers. Can be seasonal (annuals/biennials) or perennial.

Examples: Roses (Rosa spp.), Hibiscus (Hibiscus rosa-sinensis), Ixora (Ixora coccinea), Bougainvillea, Allamanda.

Uses: Flower beds, borders, fences, pots.

7.2 Foliage Ornamental Plants

Grown mainly for attractive leaves (colour, shape, texture, pattern). Suited for indoor decoration and shade gardens.

Examples: Croton (Codiaeum variegatum), Caladium, Dracaena, Dieffenbachia, Aglaonema.

7.3 Shrubs

Woody ornamental plants smaller than trees; multiple stems branching from the base. Used for hedges, screens, foundation plantings.

Examples: Duranta, Euphorbia, Hibiscus, Golden Bells.

7.4 Trees

Cultivated for stature, attractive foliage, flowers, fruits, or overall form. Provide shade, structure, and visual interest.

Examples: Royal Palm (Roystonea regia), Flamboyant Tree (Delonix regia), Jacaranda (Jacaranda mimosifolia), Neem (Azadirachta indica), Almond Tree (Terminalia catappa).

7.5 Climbers and Creepers

Climbers grow vertically using tendrils, twining stems, or adhesive roots. Creepers spread across the ground. Valuable for wall decoration, arches, trellises, ground covers.

Examples: Bougainvillea, Allamanda, Morning Glory (Ipomoea spp.), Money Plant (Epipremnum aureum), Ivy (Hedera spp.).

7.6 Grasses and Ground Covers

Low-growing plants that spread horizontally to cover soil. Prevent erosion, suppress weeds, improve visual appeal.

Examples: Carpet Grass (Axonopus compressus), Centipede Grass, Lippia, Dwarf Elephant Grass.

7.7 Indoor Ornamental Plants

Suited for growing inside homes, offices, and enclosed spaces. Require indirect sunlight; improve air quality.

Examples: Peace Lily (Spathiphyllum spp.), Snake Plant (Sansevieria spp.), Spider Plant (Chlorophytum comosum), ZZ Plant (Zamioculcas zamiifolia), Philodendron.

WEEK 8Settings and Locations for Planting Ornamental Plants

Content: Suitable settings and locations for each type of ornamental plant.

Performance Objectives

  1. Identify suitable settings for planting different types of ornamental plants.
  2. Explain why each location is appropriate for each plant type.
  3. Give examples of plants suitable for each setting.

8.1 Flowering Plants – Suitable Settings

Flourish where they receive adequate sunlight, air circulation, and space for blooming.

  • Front yards and flower beds: Colourful welcome around homes.
  • Borderlines and walkways: Edging gardens and paths with vibrant colour.
  • Around patios and porches: Aesthetic value and natural fragrance.
  • Public parks and roundabouts: Mass planting for dramatic displays.

Examples: Roses in front gardens; Bougainvillea along fences; Hibiscus in hedges.

8.2 Foliage Plants – Suitable Settings

Ideal for creating a soothing green backdrop or adding texture. Tolerate shade or low light.

  • Indoor spaces: Living rooms, hallways, offices; act as air purifiers.
  • Shaded parts of the garden: Under trees or along shaded walls.
  • Verandas and balconies: Potted foliage plants with indirect sunlight.
  • Background of flower beds: Enhance contrast and complement flowering plants.

Examples: Caladiums along shaded paths; Dracaena and Croton in pots; Aglaonema and Dieffenbachia indoors.

8.3 Shrubs – Suitable Settings

Versatile; used for decorative or functional purposes (privacy, fencing, partitioning).

  • Perimeter of residential compounds: Ornamental hedges or natural fences.
  • Garden partitions: Separate different garden zones.
  • Under windows and near walls: Height won't obstruct views.
  • Road medians and institutional landscapes: Low-maintenance landscaping.

Examples: Hibiscus for hedging; Duranta along pathways; Golden Bells in home gardens.

8.4 Trees – Suitable Settings

Require ample space due to height and canopy spread. Planted for beauty, shade, structure, and environmental benefits.

  • Avenues and streets: Shade and decorative alignment.
  • Parks and public gardens: Centrepieces or shade providers.
  • School compounds and playgrounds: Shade and recreation.
  • Lawn centres or open fields: Focal points in the landscape.

Examples: Flamboyant Tree along roadsides; Royal Palm in resort entrances; Jacaranda in civic parks.

8.5 Climbers and Creepers – Suitable Settings

Need physical support or ground space to spread. Add vertical interest; cover walls or fences.

  • Garden fences and gates: Soft green edge.
  • Walls and pergolas: Natural wall covering or shading.
  • Gazebos and arches: Romantic or cool shaded spaces.
  • Ground level (for creepers): Cover bare soil or decorate rock gardens.

Examples: Bougainvillea on trellises; Allamanda on fences; Ivy on brick walls; Morning Glory at arch entrances.

8.6 Ground Covers and Grasses – Suitable Settings

Protect and beautify ground surface. Control erosion, suppress weeds, provide uniform green coverage.

  • Lawns and playgrounds: Soft, even surface.
  • Sloping lands and embankments: Prevent soil erosion.
  • Around large trees: Where sunlight is limited, grass can thrive.
  • In between paving stones: Soften hard surfaces.

Examples: Carpet Grass for sports fields; Lippia for sloping ground; Centipede Grass in shaded and sunlit areas.

8.7 Indoor Ornamental Plants – Suitable Settings

Selected for ability to survive in low-light and confined environments. Decorative; purify air.

  • Living rooms and bedrooms: Relaxation and freshness.
  • Office desks and corridors: Reduce stress, improve aesthetics.
  • Reception areas in hotels or institutions: Welcome guests with greenery.
  • Indoor corners and staircases: Fill empty spaces with life.

Examples: Peace Lily in low-light indoor spaces; Snake Plant in bedrooms; Spider Plant hanging near windows; Philodendron on shelves.

WEEK 9Methods of Cultivating Ornamental Plants

Content: Seven methods of propagating and cultivating ornamental plants.

Performance Objectives

  1. Describe seven methods of cultivating ornamental plants.
  2. Give examples of plants suitable for each method.
  3. State one advantage of each method.

9.1 Direct Seeding (Sowing)

Planting seeds directly into the soil or container where the plant will grow permanently. Soil must be rich, loose, and well-drained. Seeds sown at appropriate depths and spacing.

Examples: Zinnias, Sunflowers, Cosmos, Marigolds.

Advantage: Cost-effective; ideal for fast-growing annuals that do not tolerate transplanting.

9.2 Transplanting

Raising seedlings in a nursery or container, then transferring them to their permanent location once strong enough.

Examples: Petunias, Impatiens, Begonias.

Advantages: Allows close monitoring of early growth; reduces competition; ensures even spacing.

9.3 Use of Cuttings

Vegetative method where a portion of a plant (stem, leaf, or root) is cut and planted to grow into a new plant. Common type is stem cutting.

Types: Stem cuttings (Hibiscus, Croton, Bougainvillea); Leaf cuttings (Sansevieria); Root cuttings (some perennials).

Advantage: Economical; new plant retains traits of parent plant.

9.4 Grafting and Budding

Advanced vegetative propagation combining desirable qualities from two plants. In grafting, a scion is joined to a rootstock. In budding, a single bud is inserted under the bark of the rootstock.

Examples: Roses, Flamboyant Trees.

Uses: Improving flower colour and size; increasing disease resistance; enhancing growth vigour.

9.5 Layering

A branch or stem of a parent plant is bent down to touch the soil, and roots develop while still attached to the parent. Once roots form, the new plant is cut off and transplanted.

Types: Simple layering, Mound layering, Air layering.

Examples: Bougainvillea, Hibiscus, Allamanda.

Advantage: Produces stronger plants; avoids transplant shock.

9.6 Division

Separating a mature plant into several parts, each with roots and shoots, which are then replanted to grow independently. Used for plants that grow in clumps or have rhizomes, bulbs, or tubers.

Examples: Lily, Chrysanthemum, Daylilies, Agapanthus.

Advantage: Rejuvenates old plants; helps control overcrowding.

9.7 Use of Bulbs, Corms, and Tubers

Many ornamental plants grow from underground storage organs. Bulbs, corms, and tubers are planted directly into the soil, where they sprout into new plants during their growing season.

Examples: Tulips and Lilies (from bulbs); Gladiolus (from corms); Dahlia and Begonia (from tubers).

Advantage: Store food; survive dormancy; ideal for seasonal planting.

  2. Explain the difference between transplanting and layering as methods of cultivating ornamental plants.
  3. Outline five methods of cultivating ornamental plants and state one advantage of each.

Performance Objectives

  2. State the importance of each practice.

10.1 Maintenance Practices

PracticeImportance/Description
1. WateringMaintains turgidity and freshness; supports flowering. Must be regular, especially in dry seasons. Avoid overwatering to prevent root rot.
2. WeedingReduces competition for nutrients, water, and space; reduces pests and diseases; enhances aesthetic appeal.
3. Pruning and TrimmingImproves air circulation; reduces disease infestation; encourages bushier growth; maintains desired shape (hedges, topiary).
4. Fertiliser ApplicationSupplements soil nutrients. Organic (compost, manure) and inorganic (NPK 15:15:15). Nitrogen for leafy growth; phosphorus for blooming; potassium for stems/roots.
5. Pest and Disease ControlEnsures plant remains healthy and attractive. Cultural practices (spacing, sanitation); chemical (insecticides, fungicides); biological (beneficial insects).
6. MulchingConserves soil moisture; suppresses weed growth; regulates soil temperature. Organic mulches (sawdust, grass clippings) decompose and improve soil.
7. Staking and SupportingProvides physical support for climbers and weak-stemmed plants. Prevents lodging; improves plant health and display aesthetics.
8. Deadheading and Flower RemovalEncourages more flowers rather than seed formation. Improves visual appearance; prevents fungal growth on decaying flowers.
9. Re-potting (For Potted Ornamentals)Provides more room for root growth; prevents root binding; rejuvenates the plant. Done during the plant's dormant period.
10. Lawn Care (For Ornamental Grass)Regular mowing, irrigation, fertilisation, and aeration. Keeps lawns healthy and enhances overall landscape beauty.
