1.1 What a Machine Is

A machine is any device that makes work easier by changing the magnitude or direction of a force. It does not create energy — it only redirects or reshapes it. This is the single governing fact of the whole topic.

Three functions of a machine:

FunctionMeaningExample
Force multiplierIncreases the applied effort so a smaller force moves a larger loadCrowbar, lever, pulley block
Speed multiplierIncreases the distance or speed of the outputBicycle gears, fishing rod
Direction changerRedirects force to a more convenient directionFixed pulley, block and tackle

1.3 The Master Relationship

Work output = work input × efficiency, therefore:

MA = η × VR

Equivalently: η = MA / VR × 100%

This one relation governs every machine problem. When efficiency is ideal (η = 1), then MA = VR. When efficiency is real (η < 1), then MA < VR. In practice, MA is always less than VR because friction consumes part of the input.

1.4 Efficiency and Losses

Efficiency is always less than 100% in real machines because:

  • Friction between moving parts converts work into heat.
  • Some energy is used to move parts of the machine itself (e.g. the weight of a pulley block).
  • Deformation or vibration absorbs work.

A machine with efficiency above 100% does not exist — it would violate the conservation of energy.

1.5 Levers

A lever is a rigid bar that rotates about a fixed point called the fulcrum (pivot). The three orders differ by what lies between what.

OrderArrangementExampleMA
First orderFulcrum between effort and loadCrowbar, seesaw, scissorsMay be >1 or <1
Second orderLoad between fulcrum and effortWheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle openerAlways > 1
Third orderEffort between fulcrum and loadFishing rod, forearm, tweezersAlways < 1

Law of moments for a lever in equilibrium:

E × a = L × b

(a = effort arm, b = load arm)

Velocity ratio of a lever: VR = a / b

Exceptions and traps:
  • A lever's own weight acts as a load at its centre of gravity, reducing effective MA.
  • The order of a lever depends on the arrangement of L, E, F — not on the shape. Scissors are first order despite looking like a cutter.
  • Second-order levers always have MA > 1. Third-order levers always have MA < 1. Structural, not stylistic.

2.1 Fixed Pulley

A pulley whose axle is fixed to a support. The rope passes over it, and the load hangs on one side while effort is applied on the other.

  • Effect: changes only the direction of the effort. No MA gained.
  • Ideal VR = 1 (effort and load move equal distances).
  • MA < 1 in practice, because friction in the pulley groove consumes some effort.

2.2 Movable Pulley

The pulley itself is free to move with the load. The rope is anchored at one end and pulled at the other.

  • Effect: the load is supported by two segments of rope, so the effort is half the load.
  • Ideal VR = 2.
  • In practice MA < 2 because the pulley's own weight adds to the load.

2.3 Block and Tackle

A system of two or more pulleys (one fixed block, one movable block) connected by a single rope. This is where pulleys become powerful.

Velocity Ratio of a block and tackle:

VR = n

where n = number of rope segments supporting the movable block

Worked rule: how to count n

  • Count only the rope segments that pull upwards on the movable block.
  • Do not count the segment being pulled by the effort (the "tail"), because it pulls downwards.
  • If in doubt, count segments between the fixed and movable blocks.

2.4 Efficiency of Pulley Systems

The main losses in a pulley system are:

  • Weight of the movable block — it must be lifted along with the load.
  • Friction in the pulley grooves and bearings.
  • Stiffness of the rope — it resists bending around the pulley.

Efficiency with a block of weight w:

η = LL + w × 100%   (ideal, frictionless)

Exceptions and traps:
  • A fixed pulley always has MA < 1. It is a direction changer, not a force multiplier.
  • VR is a geometric fact — it depends only on the rope arrangement, not on friction or load.
  • In a block and tackle, pulling the free end downward lifts the load upward. The effort distance is always n times the load distance.

◻ DIAGRAM OMITTED — A three-panel plate: inclined plane with slope length l, height h, and angle θ; wedge with length L and thickness t; screw jack with pitch p, handle radius r, and one full turn indicated. Each labelled with VR derivation. Not embedded in the source.

3.1 Inclined Plane

A flat surface set at an angle to the horizontal. A load is pushed up the slope rather than lifted vertically. The mechanical advantage comes from trading distance for force.

Velocity Ratio:

VR = lh = 1sin θ

l = slope length, h = vertical height, θ = angle to horizontal

  • Ideal MA = l / h (when frictionless).
  • Real MA is less because of friction between the load and the slope.
  • The shallower the slope, the greater the VR, and the greater the ideal MA.

3.2 The Wedge

Two inclined planes placed back-to-back. A wedge converts a downward push into an outward splitting force.

Velocity Ratio:

VR = Lt

L = length of the wedge, t = thickness of the wide end

Applications: axe, chisel, knife, nail, splitting maul, pin.

3.3 The Screw and Screw Jack

A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. A screw jack uses this principle to lift heavy loads (e.g. raising a car to change a tyre).

Velocity Ratio of a screw jack:

VR = 2πrp

r = length of the handle (radius of effort circle), p = pitch of the screw

Pitch (p): the distance the screw advances in one complete turn. Also, p = 1 / (number of threads per metre).

Exceptions and traps:
  • Screw jacks have very high VR but very low efficiency (often under 30%) because of massive friction in the threads. The high friction is deliberately kept — it stops the jack from unwinding under load.
  • A wedge is often used as a "friction-locked" tool. It does not slip out easily because the friction between its faces and the material is high.
  • The formula VR = 2πr / p assumes one thread. Multiple-start threads have VR divided by the number of starts.

4.1 Wheel and Axle

A wheel of large radius rigidly attached to a smaller cylinder (the axle). Turning the wheel turns the axle. Effort is applied to the wheel; load is attached to the axle (or vice versa).

Velocity Ratio:

VR = Rr

R = radius of the wheel, r = radius of the axle

Applications: winch, steering wheel, screwdriver, door knob, windlass, capstan.

4.2 Gears

Gears are toothed wheels that mesh to transmit motion and force. Adjacent gears rotate in opposite directions. The number of teeth determines the speed and torque change.

Velocity Ratio of a gear pair:

VR = NdrivenNdriver = teeth on driven gearteeth on driver gear

Rule: If the driven gear has more teeth than the driver, speed decreases and torque increases. If the driven gear has fewer teeth, speed increases and torque decreases.

Gear Trains (compound gears)

When three or more gears mesh, the overall VR is the product of each pair:

VRtotal = VR1 × VR2 × VR3 × …

Alternatively for a chain of gears: VR = Nlast / Nfirst (intermediate gears cancel).

4.3 Bicycle as a Compound Machine

A bicycle combines several simple machines:

  • Pedal crank and chainwheel — a wheel and axle.
  • Chain and sprocket — a gear pair (or chain drive, functionally equivalent).
  • Wheels — reduce friction against the ground; increase distance per pedal turn.
  • Brake levers — levers (second order).
  • Handlebars and frame — steering through a fulcrum arrangement.
Exceptions and traps:
  • Wheel and axle VR depends only on the radii, not on the direction of effort. Whether you drive the wheel or the axle determines whether you get force multiplication or speed multiplication.
  • Meshed gears always rotate in opposite directions. Idler gears reverse the direction again without changing total VR.
  • Bicycle gear ratios use chainwheel and rear-sprocket tooth counts, not physical radius, since the chain rides on teeth.

6.1 Where the Work Goes

For any real machine:

Win = Wout + Wlost

Wlost = energy degraded to heat by friction and to lifting the machine's own parts

Source of lossHow it appears
Friction in bearings and groovesHeat, wear, noise
Weight of the movable blockEffort must lift the block along with the load
Stiffness of rope or beltResists bending, so effort is wasted
Deformation of machine partsEnergy stored briefly and lost as heat
Air resistanceSignificant at high speed

6.2 Effort versus Load Graph

When you plot effort on the y-axis against load on the x-axis for a real machine, the graph is a straight line of the form:

E = a L + b

  • Slope a = 1 / (ideal MA). This is the reciprocal of the ideal mechanical advantage.
  • Intercept b = the effort required to overcome friction and move the machine's own parts, even when the load is zero. Sometimes called the "dead effort" or "friction effort".
  • Maximum MA occurs at the largest load on the graph, because the intercept b becomes a smaller fraction of the total effort.

6.3 Why Efficiency Rises with Load

Because the intercept b is fixed, adding more load spreads the fixed loss over a larger useful work output. Hence:

  • Efficiency increases as load increases (up to the machine's safe limit).
  • A machine working near its maximum rated load is more efficient than one working lightly loaded.
Exceptions and traps:
  • MA can be greater than VR only if the graph or the measurement is wrong; η would exceed 100%.
  • The intercept b is not the weight of the load, and not the weight of the machine. It is only the effort needed to overcome internal resistance.
  • An ideal (frictionless) machine would have b = 0, giving a straight line through the origin.
  • Efficiency can rise to a practical maximum but never reaches 100% because friction cannot be completely eliminated.

7.1 Pascal's Principle

Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of the fluid and to the walls of the container.

7.2 Hydraulic Press

A small force applied to a small piston creates a pressure in the fluid, which acts on a large piston to lift a heavy load.

Pressure equality:

EA1 = LA2

Mechanical advantage:

MA = LE = A2A1 = r22r12

Note on VR: Because the fluid is incompressible, the volume pushed down equals the volume pushed up: A1 d1 = A2 d2. Therefore VR = d1 / d2 = A2 / A1.

Ideal case: In a frictionless hydraulic press, MA = VR and efficiency = 100%. Real presses lose a few percent to piston friction.

7.3 Hydraulic Brakes

A hydraulic brake system uses Pascal's principle to transmit force from the brake pedal to the wheels.

  1. Driver presses the brake pedal.
  2. Pedal pushes a small piston in the master cylinder.
  3. Pressure is transmitted through brake fluid (incompressible) to each wheel.
  4. At each wheel, a larger slave cylinder pushes the brake shoes (or pads) against the drum (or disc).
  5. Friction slows the wheel.

7.4 Applications of Hydraulics

  • Car braking systems
  • Garage lifts and jacks
  • Excavators and bulldozers
  • Aircraft control surfaces
  • Hydraulic presses in factories
  • Dams and sluice gates
Exceptions and traps:
  • Pascal's principle requires an enclosed, incompressible fluid. If air is present, the system becomes "spongy" and braking is reduced.
  • A hydraulic press multiplies force but at the cost of distance: the small piston moves much further than the large one.
  • Because pressure in a fluid acts in all directions, a hydraulic system does not need a straight pipe to work — the fluid transmits pressure around bends without loss (ignoring friction).

8.1 What a Compound Machine Is

A compound machine is a machine made of two or more simple machines working together. Almost every real-world machine is compound.

8.2 Overall VR of a Compound Machine

VRoverall = VR1 × VR2 × VR3 × …

Overall MA: obtained from the overall load and overall effort: MA = Ltotal / Etotal.

Overall efficiency: η = (MA / VR) × 100%.

8.3 Worked Analysis — A Simple Crane

A crane lifts a load by combining:

  • A motor driving a gearbox (gear train).
  • A cable drum (wheel and axle).
  • A pulley block on the hook (block and tackle).
StageVREffect
GearboxNdriven / NdriverChanges speed and torque
Cable drumR / rMultiplies torque, reduces speed
Pulley blockn (number of strings)Multiplies force, changes direction

Each stage reduces the speed further but increases the force further. The overall VR is the product.

8.4 Why Machines Are Combined

  • Each simple machine provides only limited MA (or a trade of distance for force).
  • Combining them multiplies MA, allowing heavy loads to be moved with small efforts.
  • Different stages also serve different purposes: e.g. change direction, change speed, change location.
Exceptions and traps:
  • Overall efficiency is always less than or equal to the efficiency of the least efficient stage.
  • VRoverall is a geometric quantity — it depends only on the ratios of the machine parts, not on the load.
  • Compound machines can multiply force enormously but never make work easier in the sense of reducing total energy needed; work is only reshaped.

10.1 Advantages of Machines

  • Force multiplication — heavy loads can be lifted by small efforts (crowbar, jack).
  • Speed multiplication — fast motion with small input (bicycle, gears).
  • Change of direction — makes work more convenient (fixed pulley, lever).
  • Precision and consistency — machines do repetitive work without tiring.
  • Increased productivity — work is done faster and on a larger scale.
  • Safety for humans — hazardous tasks can be done by machines (cranes lifting loads, hydraulic presses).

10.2 Limitations of Machines

  • No machine creates energy. Output work is always less than or equal to input work.
  • Friction is unavoidable — efficiency is always below 100%.
  • Mechanical advantage comes with distance penalty. A high-MA machine moves the load slowly.
  • Maintenance required — lubrication, replacement of worn parts.
  • Cost — initial purchase and upkeep.
  • Failure modes — machines can break, jam, or cause injury if misused.

10.3 Why 100% Efficiency Is Impossible

Efficiency requires all input work to become useful output work. But in any real machine:

  • Friction between moving surfaces converts work to heat.
  • The machine's own parts need to be lifted or moved.
  • Energy escapes as sound and vibration.

The Second Law of Thermodynamics formalises this: heat cannot be completely converted back to useful work. Every energy transfer degrades some of the energy to a less useful form.

10.4 Safety in Machine Use

HazardPrecaution
Moving parts (gears, belts, pulleys)Fit guards; keep loose clothing and hair away
High-tension ropes and cablesInspect regularly; stand clear of snap-back zone
Hydraulic systems under pressureNever open fittings under pressure; use correct-rated hoses
Heavy loads suspendedNever stand under; use correct-rated slings and hooks
Sharp wedges (axes, chisels)Cut away from body; use proper grip and stance
Electrical drive motorsIsolate before maintenance; use insulated tools
Safety principle: a machine designed with high MA is not inherently safer — a jack that can lift 3 tonnes can also crush a foot. Guards, procedures and training matter more than the machine's power rating.
