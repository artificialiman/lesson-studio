Waves — General Principles

Definitions • Wave Equation • Types • Superposition • Applications
What is a wave? A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy through a medium (or through space) without transferring matter. When you drop a stone into a pond, the water bobs up and down — but the water itself does not travel to the edge. What travels is the disturbance, carrying energy outward. Waves are the universe's way of moving energy and information without moving the material that carries them: light from the Sun, sound from a speaker, radio from an antenna, seismic shaking from an earthquake. This lesson covers the universal vocabulary and laws of waves — the tools you will apply to sound, light, water, and electromagnetic waves in later topics.
Section A

Core Vocabulary & Definitions

Every wave problem uses the same six quantities. Learn what each is, its symbol, its SI unit, and what it means physically — not just its formula.

1. Amplitude (A)

The maximum displacement of a particle or field from its equilibrium (rest) position. Amplitude measures how big the wave is — how far the water rises, how far the pendulum swings, how bright the light is.

A — measured in metres (m) for mechanical waves Physical meaning: amplitude relates to energy. For a wave, energy is proportional to A2. Double the amplitude, quadruple the energy.

2. Wavelength (λ)

The distance between two consecutive points in phase — crest to crest, trough to trough, or any point to the next identical point. Wavelength measures the spatial period of the wave.

λ — measured in metres (m) Memory aid: λ is the Greek letter lambda. Think "length" of one complete cycle in space.

3. Period (T)

The time taken for one complete oscillation — for one full wavelength to pass a fixed point. Period measures the temporal length of one cycle.

T — measured in seconds (s) Example: if a wave completes 5 cycles in 2 s, then T = 2/5 = 0.4 s.

4. Frequency (f)

The number of complete oscillations per second. Frequency is the reciprocal of period — how often a cycle repeats.

f = 1T   measured in hertz (Hz) = s−1 Example: a wave with period T = 0.4 s has f = 1 / 0.4 = 2.5 Hz.

5. Wave Speed (v)

The speed at which the disturbance (energy) travels through the medium. Wave speed depends on the medium, not the wave's amplitude or frequency.

v = f λ   measured in m s−1 Fundamental relationship: speed = frequency × wavelength. This single equation drives almost every wave calculation you will meet.

6. Angular Frequency (ω) & Wave Number (k)

For mathematical work, frequency and wavelength are often expressed in angular form. Angular frequency ω is the rate of change of phase; wave number k is the spatial equivalent.

ω = 2πf (rad s−1)   •   k = 2πλ (rad m−1) Used in: the wave function y = A sin(kx − ωt), and in interference calculations.
The master equation: v = f λ. From it, everything else follows: f = v / λ, λ = v / f. If a wave passes from one medium to another, frequency stays constant (the source determines it), so speed and wavelength change together.
Section B

Classification of Waves

B.1 By Medium Requirement

Mechanical Waves

Waves that require a physical medium (solid, liquid, or gas) to propagate. Without particles to vibrate, they cannot exist.

Examples: sound, water waves, seismic (earthquake) waves, waves on a stretched string, ultrasound.

Electromagnetic Waves

Waves that do not require a medium — they propagate through vacuum. They consist of oscillating electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel.

Examples: radio, microwave, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays. All travel at c = 3 × 108 m s−1 in vacuum.

Common misconception: water waves are not purely transverse. Real water waves are a combination of transverse and longitudinal motion — particles move in circles or ellipses, not straight lines. Only the idealised "wave on a string" is purely transverse.

Section C

Universal Wave Behaviour

Every wave, whatever its type, exhibits the same six behaviours. These are the phenomena you will be tested on in topic-specific lessons.

1. Reflection

A wave bounces back when it meets a boundary between two media. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection (measured from the normal).

θi = θr Examples: echo (sound), mirror image (light), ripple tank reflection (water).

2. Refraction

A wave changes direction when it passes from one medium to another because its speed changes. Frequency is unchanged; wavelength and speed change.

sin θ1sin θ2 = v1v2 = λ1λ2 = n Rule of thumb: entering a slower medium, the wave bends toward the normal; entering a faster medium, it bends away.

3. Diffraction

A wave spreads out when it passes through a gap or around an obstacle. Diffraction is most noticeable when the gap size is comparable to the wavelength.

Significant diffraction when   gap ≈ λ Examples: hearing sound around a corner; radio waves bending over hills; the spreading of ripples through a narrow slit.

4. Interference

When two waves meet, their displacements add (superposition). If crests meet crests, we get constructive interference; if crest meets trough, we get destructive interference.

Constructive:  Δd = nλ  •  Destructive:  Δd = (n + ½)λ Principle of superposition: the resultant displacement at any point is the vector sum of the displacements due to each wave.

5. Dispersion

The separation of a wave into its component frequencies as it travels through a medium, because wave speed varies with frequency.

Examples: a prism splitting white light into a spectrum; different colours of light travelling at slightly different speeds in glass.

6. Polarisation

Restricting a transverse wave's oscillations to a single plane. Only transverse waves can be polarised; longitudinal waves cannot.

Applications: polaroid sunglasses reduce glare; LCD screens use polarised light; radio antennas respond to specific polarisations.
Test for a transverse wave: try to polarise it. If you can filter out half the wave by rotating a filter, it is transverse. If polarisation has no effect, it is longitudinal. This is the definitive experiment.
Section D

The Wave Equation & Graphical Analysis

D.1 Displacement–Distance vs. Displacement–Time Graphs

This is the single most common point of confusion in wave physics. The same wave looks different depending on which axis you plot. Learn to read both.

Graph x-axis What a full cycle gives you
Displacement–distance position along the wave the wavelength λ
Displacement–time time the period T (and hence f = 1/T)
Classic exam trap: a displacement–time graph does not show the shape of the wave. It shows how one point moves over time. To find wavelength, you need a displacement–distance graph (or "snapshot").

D.2 The General Wave Function

Progressive Wave Equation

For a wave travelling in the +x direction, the displacement y of a particle at position x and time t is given by:

y = A sin(kx − ωt)  or  y = A sin 2π(tT − xλ) Sign rule: (kx − ωt) means travelling in +x; (kx + ωt) means travelling in −x.

D.3 Standing Waves (Stationary Waves)

How Standing Waves Form

When a wave reflects and meets its own reflection travelling the opposite way, they superimpose. At certain frequencies, the result appears to stand still, with fixed points of zero displacement (nodes) and fixed points of maximum displacement (antinodes).

Node spacing = λ2  •  Antinode spacing = λ2  •  Node to antinode = λ4 On a string fixed at both ends: the two ends must be nodes, so L = nλ/2 where n = 1, 2, 3, … Each n gives a harmonic.
Progressive vs. standing — the key distinction: a progressive wave transfers energy from place to place. A standing wave stores energy in place; net energy transfer is zero. All points in a standing wave between two adjacent nodes oscillate in phase.

Practice Questions

Questions 1–25
  1. A wave has frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 4 m. Calculate its speed.
  2. A sound wave travels at 340 m s−1 with a frequency of 170 Hz. Find its wavelength.
  3. A wave has period 0.02 s and speed 300 m s−1. Find its wavelength.
  4. A wave completes 12 oscillations in 3 s. Find its frequency and period.
  5. An electromagnetic wave in vacuum has wavelength 600 nm. Find its frequency. (c = 3 × 108 m s−1)
  6. Define amplitude and state how it relates to the energy of a wave.
  7. Distinguish between a mechanical wave and an electromagnetic wave, giving one example of each.
  8. State two differences between transverse and longitudinal waves.
  9. Which type of wave can be polarised, and why?
  10. Explain why sound waves cannot travel through a vacuum.
  11. A wave travels from air into water. State what happens to its (a) frequency, (b) speed, and (c) wavelength.
  12. Define reflection and state the law of reflection.
  13. Explain why a wave changes direction when it enters a new medium.
  14. A ripple tank produces waves with wavelength 2 cm. The waves pass through a gap of 2 cm. Describe what is observed and name the phenomenon.
  15. State the principle of superposition.
  16. Two waves with the same frequency travel to a point. Their path difference is 1.5 λ. State whether interference is constructive or destructive, and justify your answer.
  17. Explain what is meant by dispersion and give one example.
  18. A string of length 0.8 m is fixed at both ends and vibrates in its fundamental mode. Find the wavelength.
  19. In a standing wave, the distance between two consecutive nodes is 0.25 m. Find the wavelength.
  20. State two differences between a progressive wave and a standing wave.
  21. A wave is described by y = 0.05 sin(4x − 200t) (SI units). State the amplitude, wave number, and angular frequency.
  22. Using the wave in Q21, find its wavelength, frequency, and speed.
  23. Light of wavelength 500 nm in air enters glass of refractive index 1.5. Find its speed and wavelength in the glass.
  24. A wave on a string has tension 80 N and mass per unit length 0.02 kg m−1. Find the wave speed.
  25. Explain the difference between a displacement–distance graph and a displacement–time graph, and state what quantity each gives you directly.
