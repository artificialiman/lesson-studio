1.1 Meaning of Livestock Farming

Livestock farming, a major branch of agriculture, is the scientific and purposeful practice of breeding, raising, and managing domestic animals (livestock). These animals are kept for the primary purpose of producing valuable resources, which include:

  • Food: Meat (beef, pork, chicken), milk, eggs.
  • Fiber: Wool (from sheep), hides and skin (for leather).
  • Labor: Draught animals (e.g., cattle, donkeys) used for plowing and transportation.
  • Other Products: Manure (for fertilizer), bone meal, etc.

It is distinct from simply keeping pets, as it is an economic activity aimed at production and profit.

1.2 Importance of Livestock Farming

1. Nutritional Importance

  • High-Quality Protein: Livestock products (meat, milk, eggs) are the best sources of high-biological-value protein, which contains all the essential amino acids required for human growth, tissue repair, and immune system function.
  • Vitamins and Minerals: They provide vital nutrients that are difficult to find in plant-based diets, such as Vitamin B12 (for nerve function) and Haem-iron (easily absorbed iron to prevent anemia). Milk is a primary source of Calcium for bone health.

2. Economic Importance

  • Income and Livelihood: It provides a direct source of income for millions of people, from rural nomadic pastoralists to commercial poultry farmers.
  • Employment: It creates a vast number of jobs, not just on the farm (farmhands, veterinarians) but also in related industries (feed mills, abattoirs, transportation, marketing).
  • Foreign Exchange: The export of hides and skin (especially "Morocco" leather from Nigerian goats) is a major source of foreign currency for the country.
  • Raw Materials: It supplies raw materials to many industries, such as leather (for shoes, bags), textiles (wool), and animal feed (bone meal, blood meal).

3. Agricultural and Environmental Importance

  • Draught Power: In many rural communities, cattle (especially bulls) are used as draught animals for plowing land for crop farming, which is cheaper than buying a tractor.
  • Manure (Fertilizer): Animal waste (dung, poultry droppings) is an excellent, nutrient-rich organic fertilizer that improves soil structure and fertility, reducing the need for chemical fertilizers.

4. Social and Cultural Importance

  • Status Symbol: In many traditional Nigerian societies, the number of animals a person owns (especially cattle) is a direct measure of their wealth and social standing.
  • Cultural Use: Livestock (goats, chickens, rams) are essential for festivals, ceremonies, marriages (as part of the dowry), and religious sacrifices.

2.1 Traditional Systems

1. Nomadic Pastoralism

This is the most well-known traditional system, primarily associated with the Fulani people and their Zebu cattle. It is a mobile system where herders move their animals over large distances in search of pasture (grass) and water.

  • Transhumance: This is a specific type of pastoralism where the movement is seasonal. The herders move their cattle from the dry, savanna North during the rainy season to the greener South (Middle Belt) during the dry season.

Characteristics:

  • Animals (e.g., Zebu) are extremely hardy, disease-resistant, and can walk long distances.
  • Production (milk, meat) is low.
  • Animals are a sign of wealth and culture, not just profit.
  • This system is now under severe pressure due to population growth, climate change, and conflict with crop farmers.

2. Village/Backyard (Free-Range) System

This is the most common system for poultry, goats, and sheep in rural and semi-urban areas.

Characteristics:

  • Animals are not confined. They "free-range" during the day, scavenging for their own food (insects, household scraps, grass).
  • They are given minimal housing (e.g., a small shed) at night.
  • Input (cost) is almost zero.
  • Output is low, and animals are vulnerable to theft, predators, and diseases (like Newcastle disease in poultry).

2.2 Modern Methods and Development

The development of modern livestock farming in Nigeria was a conscious effort to move from low-output traditional systems to high-output commercial systems.

1. Introduction of Exotic (Foreign) Breeds

This was a major turning point. Scientists and policymakers realized that local breeds were hardy but produced very little.

  • Cattle: Holstein-Friesian (for high milk yield), Hereford (for high beef yield).
  • Poultry: Broilers (for meat, grow in 6-8 weeks) and Layers (e.g., Leghorn, for high egg production).
  • Pigs: Large White, Landrace (for high-quality pork).

Problem: These breeds are not adapted to Nigeria's climate or diseases (e.g., Trypanosomiasis from tsetse flies). They require intensive management.

2. Intensive Housing Systems

  • Battery Cages: For laying hens. This confines birds in small cages, which protects them from disease, prevents egg-eating, and makes management easy.
  • Deep Litter System: For broilers. Thousands of birds are raised on a sawdust-covered floor in a large, ventilated "broiler house."
  • Piggeries: Concrete pens for raising pigs, separating them from mud and waste.

3. Advancements in Veterinary Medicine

The establishment of veterinary colleges and research centers allowed for the mass production of vaccines (e.g., against Newcastle, Gumboro) and drugs (antibiotics, dewormers), making it possible to keep thousands of animals in one place without them all dying.

4. Feed Formulation

The shift from scavenging to scientific feed formulation (creating balanced rations from maize, soybean, etc.) allowed animals to reach their full genetic potential. This created the massive "animal feed" industry.

5. Artificial Insemination (AI)

The (limited) introduction of AI, especially in cattle, allowed farmers to get calves from a superior bull without owning the bull.

2.3 Government Roles in Livestock Development

The Nigerian government has historically played a major role in trying to boost livestock production.

1. Research Institutes

  • NAPRI (National Animal Production Research Institute): Located in Shika, Zaria. This is the primary institute for research into animal breeding, nutrition, and production systems in Nigeria.
  • NVRI (National Veterinary Research Institute): Located in Vom, Plateau State. This is the primary institute for animal disease research and the production of most animal vaccines used in the country.
  • KLDB (Kainji Lake Research Institute): Researches freshwater fisheries.

2. National Development Programs

  • Agricultural Development Projects (ADPs): State-level programs (funded by the World Bank) that provided farmers with extension services, inputs, and technology.
  • Operation Feed the Nation (OFN): A past military government initiative to boost all forms of agriculture, including backyard poultry.

3. Policy and Infrastructure

  • Grazing Reserves: Establishing designated areas for pastoralists to graze, in an attempt to reduce farmer-herder conflict.
  • Import Bans: Banning the import of frozen poultry (chicken, turkey) to protect and stimulate the local poultry industry.
  • Border Control: Setting up quarantine stations at borders to check animals for diseases.

3.1 Introduction

A more fundamental way to classify farm animals is by their digestive system (their stomach). This is the most important classification because it determines what you can feed them and how they must be managed. All livestock are divided into two main groups: Ruminants and Non-Ruminants.

3.2 Ruminants

Definition: A ruminant is a mammal that has a complex, four-compartment stomach. They are "polygastric" (Poly = many; gastric = stomach).

Examples: Cattle, Goats, Sheep.

The Four Compartments

CompartmentDescription and Function
a. RumenThis is the first and largest compartment. It is not a "true" stomach. It is a massive fermentation vat. It contains billions of microbes (bacteria, fungi, protozoa). These microbes do what the cow cannot: they break down cellulose (the tough fiber in grass) through fermentation.
b. ReticulumThis is connected to the rumen (often called the "rumen-reticulum"). It has a honeycomb lining. Its job is to trap heavy, non-food items (like stones or nails) and to help with rumination.
c. OmasumThis is a globe-shaped organ with many folds of tissue ("many-plies"). Its main job is to absorb water and filter the food.
d. AbomasumThis is the "True Stomach." It functions exactly like a human stomach, secreting acid (HCl) and digestive enzymes (like pepsin) to digest protein.

Key Characteristics of Ruminants

  • They Chew the Cud (Rumination): This is their defining feature. The animal eats grass, swallows it into the rumen, and then later regurgitates the partially digested food (the "cud") back into its mouth to be chewed again. This (1) adds saliva and (2) breaks the food into smaller pieces, giving the microbes more surface area to work on.
  • They Digest Cellulose: They can survive and thrive on high-fiber, low-quality feeds (like grass, hay, silage, straw) that humans and other animals cannot. They do not compete with humans for food (grains).
  • Bloat: They are prone to a dangerous condition called "bloat," where fermentation gas gets trapped in the rumen.

3.3 Non-Ruminants

Definition: A non-ruminant is an animal that has a simple, single-compartment stomach. They are "monogastric" (Mono = one).

Examples: Pigs, Poultry (Chickens, Turkeys), Rabbits. (Humans are also monogastric).

Key Characteristics of Non-Ruminants

  • Simple Stomach: Their stomach produces acid and enzymes to digest food, just like the abomasum.
  • Cannot Digest Cellulose: They have no rumen and cannot break down high-fiber grass. If you feed a chicken only grass, it will starve.
  • They Compete with Humans: Because they cannot eat grass, their diet is based on high-energy, low-fiber foods like grains (maize, sorghum) and protein meals (soybean, groundnut cake). These are all foods that humans can also eat.
  • Efficient Feed Conversion: Many (like pigs and broilers) are extremely efficient at converting high-quality feed into meat.

3.4 Special Cases (Pseudo-Ruminants)

  • Some animals (like Rabbits and Horses) are non-ruminant but can digest fiber.
  • Rabbits have a very large cecum (a pouch in the intestine) that acts like a small "rumen," fermenting fiber.
  • Rabbits also practice coprophagy (eating their own soft, night-time feces) to re-digest the food and absorb the nutrients.

4.1 Introduction

In modern livestock farming (especially commercial systems), animals are not just left to wander. Housing is a critical management tool. A livestock house is any structure built to confine, protect, and manage animals.

4.2 Principles of Good Livestock Housing

PrincipleExplanation
1. Orientation (Siting)The building should be sited on high, well-drained ground. East-West Orientation: The long axis of the building should face East-West. This minimizes the amount of hot, direct sun that hits the long walls, keeping the inside cooler.
2. VentilationCritical. The building must have excellent cross-ventilation (e.g., open sides with wire mesh). Purpose: To remove hot air, harmful gases (ammonia), reduce humidity, and bring in fresh oxygen. Poor ventilation leads to respiratory diseases.
3. Space (Stocking Density)The house must be large enough for the number of animals. Overstocking leads to stress, competition, faster disease spread, and vices (like feather-pecking in chickens).
4. DrainageThe floor must be sloped (e.g., 1:20 gradient) so that water (from cleaning or urine) flows out. This prevents "water-logging" and dampness, which breed disease.
5. SecurityThe house must be strong enough to prevent predators (e.g., snakes, foxes) from entering and secure enough to prevent theft.
6. HygieneThe floor and walls (especially in a piggery) should be made of smooth, durable concrete. This makes them non-porous and easy to wash, clean, and disinfect.
7. AccessibilityThe house must be accessible to bring in feed and water, and to remove waste and animals.

4.3 Poultry Housing

  • Extensive (Free-Range): Traditional. Birds scavenge by day and may have a simple shed for night.
  • Semi-Intensive: A fixed house is provided, along with an outside, fenced-in "run" or "paddock."
  • Intensive (Fully Confined):
    • Deep Litter System: For Broilers and young layers ("pallets"). A large, well-ventilated house with a concrete floor covered with a thick (6-8 inch) layer of absorbent "litter" (e.g., wood shavings). Drinkers, feeders, and heaters are all inside.
    • Battery Cage System: For adult Laying Hens. Birds are kept in small wire cages, stacked in long rows. Feed and water are provided in troughs outside the cage. The cage floor is sloped, so when an egg is laid, it rolls forward out of the cage. Waste drops through the wire floor, separating the bird from its manure. Pros: Very high production, clean eggs, easy to manage, high stocking density. Cons: Low animal welfare, high initial cost.

4.4 Cattle Housing

In Nigeria, cattle are often kept in simple fenced-in areas called paddocks (for grazing) or kraals (a traditional fenced enclosure). Modern systems include:

  • Feedlot: An intensive system for beef fattening. Cattle are kept in outdoor pens and are brought a high-energy diet.
  • Dairy Barns (Cubicle Housing): A large, well-ventilated building for dairy cows. Each cow has its own "cubicle" or stall for resting. Designed for hygiene and comfort to maximize milk production.

4.5 Pig Housing (Piggery / Pigsty)

Key Feature: Pigs are very strong, destructive, and produce a lot of wet manure. The house must be made of strong concrete blocks and concrete floors.

Specialized Pens:

  • Farrowing Pen: For the pregnant/mother sow. It contains a "farrowing crate," which is a special cage that stops the mother from accidentally crushing her piglets.
  • Boar Pen: A strong, individual pen for the adult male.
  • Finishing Pen: A larger group pen for growing pigs.

Features: Must have a water wallow (a small pool) for cooling and a good drainage gutter.

4.6 Goat and Sheep Housing

These are simpler. A basic, well-ventilated shed is often sufficient. A common modern design is a raised-floor house. The house is built on stilts (e.g., 1-2 meters off the ground) with a slatted (gapped) wooden or plastic floor.

Advantage: The feces and urine fall through the floor, keeping the animals clean, dry, and far away from parasites (like worms).

6.1 Introduction

Nutrition is the foundation of livestock production. Feed accounts for 60-70% of the total cost of production in most livestock enterprises. A well-fed animal will grow faster, produce more milk or eggs, and resist disease better than a poorly-fed one.

6.2 The Six Classes of Nutrients

NutrientFunctionSource (Ingredients)
1. Carbohydrates The primary energy source. They are the "fuel" that the animal burns to walk, breathe, and stay warm. Maize (corn), Sorghum, Millet, Wheat (in wheat offal), Cassava, Yams.
2. Proteins The "building blocks" of the body. Essential for growth (building muscle), repairing tissue, and production (making eggs, milk, and developing a fetus). Plant-Based: Groundnut Cake (GNC), Soybean Meal, Palm Kernel Cake (PKC).
Animal-Based: Fish Meal, Blood Meal.
3. Fats and Oils (Lipids) A concentrated energy source. They provide 2.25 times more energy per gram than carbohydrates. They also help in the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins. Palm Oil, Groundnut Oil, Tallow (animal fat).
4. Vitamins "Protective" nutrients needed in very small amounts. They regulate body processes and act as "spark plugs" for chemical reactions. Fat-Soluble (A, D, E, K): Green leafy plants, synthetic Vitamin Premixes.
Water-Soluble (B-complex, C): Synthetic Vitamin Premixes.
5. Minerals Structural and regulatory. Needed for bones, blood, and metabolic processes. Macro-minerals: Calcium & Phosphorus (Bone Meal, Oyster Shell, Limestone Flour), Sodium (Salt Licks).
Micro-minerals: Iron, Iodine, Zinc (Mineral Premixes).
6. Water The "forgotten nutrient," but the most essential. Regulates body temperature, transports nutrients, essential for digestion and milk/egg production. Must be provided ad libitum (freely available at all times) and must be clean.

8.1 Balanced Ration

Definition: A ration is the total amount of feed an animal receives in a 24-hour period. A balanced ration is a ration that contains all 6 essential nutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, water) in the correct proportions and amounts to meet the animal's specific needs.

The Ration is "Specific": There is no single "perfect feed." The "correct proportion" changes depending on the animal.

  • Example (Poultry):
    • Broiler Starter Mash: For a 1-week-old broiler (growth). Needs very high protein (e.g., 23%).
    • Broiler Finisher Mash: For a 6-week-old broiler (fattening). Needs lower protein (e.g., 19%) but high energy (carbs).
    • Layer's Mash: For an egg-laying hen. Needs moderate protein but very high Calcium (for eggshells).

8.2 Factors Affecting Feed Formulation

Feed Formulation is the science of mixing different ingredients (e.g., maize, GNC, bone meal) to create a balanced ration. A farmer or nutritionist must consider these factors:

FactorExplanation
1. The Animal's SpeciesA cow (ruminant) needs a high-fiber ration (grass). A chicken (non-ruminant) needs a low-fiber, high-grain ration. You cannot feed cow feed to a chicken.
2. The Animal's AgeYoung animals (growth): Need more protein (e.g., Broiler Starter). Adult animals (maintenance): Need less protein and more energy.
3. The Animal's Purpose (Physiological State)Egg/Milk Production (Lactation): Needs high protein, high calcium, and high energy. Meat Production (Fattening): Needs very high energy. Pregnancy: Needs extra nutrients. Work: A draught animal needs a very high-energy ration.
4. Cost and Availability of IngredientsThis is a business decision. Maize might be the best energy source, but if it's too expensive, the farmer must reformulate using a cheaper alternative (like cassava).
5. Health StatusA sick or recovering animal may need a special, highly digestible ration or medicated feed.

8.3 Feed Storage

This is a critical management task. You can buy the best feed in the world, but if you store it incorrectly, it will be wasted or become poisonous.

The Goal: To protect the feed from Moisture, Pests, and Sunlight.

The Ideal Feed Store

  • DRY: Must be 100% waterproof.
  • COOL: Heat (from sunlight) can degrade vitamins.
  • WELL-VENTILATED: Good airflow prevents dampness and mold.
  • PEST-PROOF: Must be sealed to keep out rats, mice, weevils, and cockroaches.

Rules of Storage

  • USE PALLETS: Never stack feed bags directly on the bare floor (especially concrete). The floor is cold and wicks moisture, causing the bottom bags to mold. Bags must be stacked on wooden pallets (platforms) to allow air to circulate underneath.
  • FIFO: "First-In, First-Out." Always use the oldest feed bags before opening the new ones.
  • PEST CONTROL: Set traps and seal holes.
  • SEPARATE: Keep feeds separate from chemicals like drugs, pesticides, or fuel.

The Danger of Poor Storage (Aflatoxins)

  • If feed (especially Groundnut Cake) gets damp, a toxic mold (Aspergillus) will grow on it.
  • This mold produces a poison called Aflatoxin.
  • Aflatoxin is a silent killer in livestock. It destroys the liver, stunts growth, and causes death. It is a major economic loss.

9.1 Introduction

Animal Breeding is the applied science of using the principles of genetics to improve livestock. Breeding is the process of mating a selected male and female to produce offspring. The goal of all breeding is to ensure that the next generation is better than the current generation.

9.2 Selection

Selection is the process of choosing which animals will be allowed to become parents. This is the first and most important step in any breeding program. If you breed with poor-quality animals, you will get poor-quality offspring.

MethodDescriptionPros and Cons
1. Mass Selection The oldest and simplest method. The farmer simply looks at all their animals and selects the "best-looking" ones (the ones with the best phenotype) to be parents. Pro: Simple, easy, and effective for highly heritable traits.
Con: You cannot select for traits you can't see (like disease resistance) or traits in the opposite sex (like milk production in a bull).
2. Pedigree Selection Selecting an animal based on the performance records of its ancestors (its "pedigree" or "family tree"). Pro: Allows you to select for traits before the animal is old enough to show them.
Con: The records might be inaccurate, and the animal may not have inherited the good genes.
3. Progeny Testing The most accurate and most complex method. An animal (especially a male) is selected based on the performance of his offspring (his "progeny"). Pro: The most reliable method, as it tests the actual genes the male is passing on.
Con: Very slow (takes years to get results) and very expensive. Used by large breeding companies.

9.3 Mating Systems

1. Natural Breeding (Natural Mating)

Definition: The traditional method of allowing a male animal (e.g., bull, boar, ram) to physically mount and copulate with a female in "heat" (estrus).

Advantages:

  • Simple and Low-Tech: Requires no special equipment or training.
  • High Conception Rate: The male is an expert at detecting the exact time the female is fertile.

Disadvantages:

  • Disease Transmission: A male can easily pass Sexually Transmitted Diseases (STDs) (e.g., Brucellosis) to all females.
  • Risk of Injury: A large, heavy male (like a bull) can injure a small female (a heifer).
  • Cost and Risk of Keeping Males: Large male animals are often aggressive, dangerous to the farmer, and expensive to feed and house all year.
  • Limited Genetics: One bull can only service a few cows in his local area.

2. Artificial Insemination (AI)

Definition: This is a high-tech breeding method. It involves:

  1. Collecting semen (sperm) from a genetically superior male.
  2. Processing, diluting, and freezing the semen in liquid nitrogen (in "semen straws").
  3. A trained technician manually inserts the thawed semen into the female's reproductive tract (uterus) at the correct time of her heat cycle.

Advantages:

  • RAPID GENETIC IMPROVEMENT: The semen from one prize-winning bull can be frozen and used to sire thousands of calves all over the world, rapidly improving the genetic quality of the entire population.
  • DISEASE CONTROL: Semen is tested for STDs before being frozen. This prevents the spread of diseases.
  • SAFETY AND COST: The farmer doesn't need to own a dangerous, expensive bull. They just buy a straw of semen.
  • BREEDING ACCESS: A small farmer in Nigeria can use AI to get a calf from the best bull in Holland or America.

Disadvantages:

  • Requires a Skilled Technician: You must hire a trained AI technician.
  • Heat Detection: The farmer must be able to accurately detect when the cow is in "heat." If the timing is wrong, the AI will fail.
  • Lower Conception Rate: Can be less successful than natural mating if not timed perfectly.

10.1 Introduction

A Breeding System is a planned strategy for mating animals to achieve a specific goal. The system is defined by how genetically related the two parents are.

10.2 Inbreeding

Definition: Inbreeding is the mating of very closely related animals.

Relationship: Father to daughter, son to mother, or full brother to sister.

Purpose (Advantage):

  • To Create "Purebreds": This is how all "pure" breeds (like Holstein, German Shepherd) were created. By intensely inbreeding, you "fix" the traits.
  • To Increase Homozygosity: It makes the genes "pure" (e.g., AA or aa). This makes the animals very uniform and predictable.

Disadvantages (Critical):

  • INBREEDING DEPRESSION: This is the biggest problem. Inbreeding exposes hidden, harmful recessive genes.
  • Effects of Inbreeding Depression:
    • Low Fertility/Sterility: The animals cannot produce offspring.
    • Low Vigor: Offspring are weak and small.
    • High Offspring Mortality: Many are born dead or die young.
    • Physical Deformities: e.g., extra legs, missing eyes.

In general, inbreeding is avoided by commercial farmers.

10.3 Line Breeding

Definition: Line breeding is a milder, more controlled form of inbreeding. It is the mating of animals that are related, but not as closely.

Relationship: Cousin to cousin, grandsire to granddaughter, half-brother to half-sister.

Purpose: To maintain the influence of a single, outstanding ancestor.

Example: A farmer has a champion, prize-winning bull. The bull dies. To keep his "good genes" in the herd, the farmer might mate the bull's son back to the bull's half-sisters.

Balance: It is a way to get the benefits of inbreeding (uniformity) without the severe risks of inbreeding depression.

10.4 Crossbreeding (Outcrossing)

Definition: Crossbreeding is the opposite of inbreeding. It is the mating of animals from two completely different breeds.

Purpose: To combine the best traits from two breeds into one animal.

The Key Advantage: Hybrid Vigor (Heterosis)

Definition: Hybrid Vigor is the phenomenon where the crossbred offspring (the "hybrid") is better than the average of its two parents. It is often bigger, stronger, more fertile, and healthier. It is the opposite of Inbreeding Depression.

Classic Nigerian Example:

  • Parent 1: Muturu (a local Nigerian cattle breed).
    • Pros: Extremely hardy, tolerant of heat, resistant to local diseases (like Trypanosomiasis).
    • Cons: Very small, grows slowly, produces almost no milk.
  • Parent 2: Holstein-Friesian (an exotic breed).
    • Pros: The world's best milk producer (e.g., 50L/day).
    • Cons: Very delicate, cannot handle heat, will die from local diseases.

THE HYBRID OFFSPRING: The crossbred calf gets the disease resistance from the Muturu and the high milk potential from the Holstein. It is an animal that survives in Nigeria and also produces good milk.

Other Examples:

  • Mating a "Broiler" (fast-growing) with a "Layer" (egg-laying) to create a good "dual-purpose" chicken.
