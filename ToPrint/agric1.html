1.1 Meaning of Agriculture

Agriculture is defined as the science, art, and business of cultivating the land, producing crops, and rearing animals for food, fiber, fuel, and other purposes. It involves all processes from soil preparation, planting, raising animals, harvesting, to processing, packaging, and marketing.

The word is derived from two Latin words: "Ager" (field) and "Culture" (cultivation). Literally, it means "field cultivation." Modern agriculture now includes livestock rearing, forestry, fishery, and agricultural economics.

Agriculture is a science (uses biological and chemical knowledge), an art (requires skills and experience), and a business (people earn a living from it).

1.2 Branches of Agriculture

BranchDescription
Crop ProductionCultivation of crops such as cereals (rice, maize), tubers (yam, cassava), legumes (beans, groundnut), vegetables, and fruits.
Animal HusbandryRearing of domesticated animals (goats, sheep, poultry, pigs, cattle) for meat, milk, eggs, hides, and skins.
ForestryManagement, planting, and conservation of forests and trees for timber, rubber, fruits, and medicinal plants.
FisheryFarming and harvesting of fish and other aquatic animals (shrimp, crayfish) in natural or man-made water bodies.
Agricultural Economics & ExtensionEconomic aspects of agriculture (cost, marketing, farm management) and education of farmers by extension officers.

1.3 Importance of Agriculture

  1. Provision of Food: Main source of carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, and minerals.
  2. Source of Raw Materials: Cocoa for chocolate, cotton for textiles, oil palm for soap and cooking oil.
  3. Employment Opportunities: Direct jobs (farmers, herdsmen) and indirect jobs (marketers, processors, veterinarians).
  4. Source of Income: Farming, fishing, livestock rearing, and processing generate income for families.
  5. Contribution to National Development: Supports trade, transport, and education; reduces food importation.
  6. Foreign Exchange Earnings: Exports like cocoa, sesame seed, rubber, and palm oil bring foreign currency.
  7. Rural Development: Leads to provision of roads, electricity, schools, and health centers in rural areas.
  8. Promotion of Industrial Growth: Supplies raw materials to agro-industries (food processing, packaging, textiles).

WEEK 3Meaning and Differences Between Subsistence and Commercial Agriculture

Content: Definition, characteristics, and differences between subsistence and commercial agriculture.

Performance Objectives

  1. Define subsistence agriculture and commercial agriculture.
  2. State the characteristics of each system.
  3. Differentiate between subsistence and commercial agriculture using at least ten points.
  4. Give Nigerian examples of each system.

3.1 Subsistence Agriculture

Definition: Farming in which the farmer produces food mainly for the consumption of themselves and their family, not for sale in large markets. A little surplus may be sold locally.

Characteristics: Small-scale; family labor; simple tools (hoe, cutlass); depends on rainfall; low output; not profit-oriented.

Example: Mr. and Mrs. Ubong in Uruan grow yam, cassava, maize, okra, and vegetables on a small plot for family consumption.

3.2 Commercial Agriculture

Definition: Farming done with the intention of producing crops or rearing animals for sale and profit. Practiced on a large scale with advanced technology.

Characteristics: Large-scale; hired labor; modern machinery (tractors, harvesters); uses fertilizers, improved seeds, irrigation; market-oriented; profit-driven.

Example: A company in Akwa Ibom State operates a large oil palm plantation covering hundreds of hectares, using modern machines and processing palm oil for national and international markets.

3.3 Detailed Differences

FeatureSubsistence AgricultureCommercial Agriculture
1. PurposeFeed the farmer and household.Produce for sale and profit.
2. ScaleSmall plot near home.Large area (hundreds of hectares).
3. ToolsSimple traditional tools (hoe, cutlass, baskets).Advanced machinery (tractors, harvesters, planters).
4. LabourFamily labor.Hired or paid labor; specialists.
5. Output/YieldLow due to traditional methods.High due to modern inputs and management.
6. Type of ProduceMixture of food crops for family.One or two crops/animals for large-scale distribution.
7. TechnologyLittle or no technology; knowledge passed down.Heavy use of technology (fertilizers, improved seeds, irrigation).
8. Risk & SustainabilityLow financial risk; sustainable due to local knowledge.High financial risk; depends on markets and weather.
9. Storage & MarketingSurplus stored locally or sold in village markets.Well-organized storage, transportation, and marketing systems.
10. Examples in NigeriaFamily growing yam and maize in Benue for home use.Large oil palm plantation in Akwa Ibom supplying factories and international buyers.

WEEK 6Role of Non-Governmental Organisations (NGOs) in Agricultural Development

6.1 Meaning of NGOs

Non-Governmental Organisations (NGOs) are non-profit, independent organisations that operate at local, national, or international levels. They work closely with rural farmers, communities, cooperatives, and agricultural groups to provide support, funding, and education. Their interventions are particularly valuable in areas neglected by government programmes.

6.2 Roles of NGOs

RoleDescription
1. Financial Assistance and GrantsProvide grants or soft loans to smallholder farmers, especially women and youth, who cannot access formal banking services.
2. Capacity Building and TrainingTrain farmers through workshops, seminars, and demonstrations on improved practices (crop rotation, compost making, pest control, irrigation, post-harvest handling).
3. Research and DevelopmentPartner with research institutions to conduct field-based research, test new seed varieties, and investigate organic farming methods.
4. Advocacy and Policy InfluenceCampaign for fair land laws, better market access, affordable inputs, and environmental protection; represent farmers at national and international platforms.
5. Promoting Gender Equality and InclusionEmpower women and disadvantaged groups by providing tools, training, credit, and sensitizing communities on inclusion and equity.
6. Support for Agro-Cooperative SocietiesMobilize farmers into cooperatives, provide technical and managerial support, and help them access finance and markets.
7. Disaster Relief and Emergency SupportProvide emergency supplies (seeds, food, livestock, shelter) during floods, droughts, pest infestations, or pandemics; support recovery.
8. Environmental Protection and Sustainable FarmingTeach farmers to protect soil fertility, conserve water, manage waste, and reduce harmful chemicals; encourage agroforestry, organic farming, mulching.
9. Market Access and Value Chain DevelopmentHelp farmers connect to local and international markets; offer training on packaging, pricing, negotiation, and value addition.
10. Monitoring, Evaluation, and FeedbackHelp communities track progress of development projects; assess impact and suggest improvements; ensure transparency and accountability.

Content: Meaning, objectives, key laws, effects, and agencies.

Performance Objectives

  4. Explain the effects of these laws on agricultural production.

8.1 Meaning of Agricultural Laws and Reforms

Agricultural laws are legal instruments enacted by the government to guide, regulate, and promote agricultural activities. Agricultural reforms are programmes or policies introduced to improve the agricultural sector.

8.2 Objectives of Agricultural Laws

  1. Ensure efficient use of agricultural land.
  2. Promote agricultural investment.
  3. Resolve land disputes.
  4. Protect the environment.
  5. Boost agricultural productivity.

8.3 Key Agricultural Laws and Reforms in Nigeria

Law/ReformDescription and Implication
1. Land Use Act of 1978All land vested in state governors; individuals acquire land through certificates of occupancy. Abolished traditional land ownership. Made land accessible for commercial farming. Limitation: Bureaucracy in obtaining land certificates.
2. Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme Act of 1977CBN guarantees up to 75% of loans granted to farmers by commercial banks. Increased access to credit. Limitation: Collateral requirements still a barrier.
3. River Basin Development Authorities Act (1976)Established RBDAs to provide irrigation, flood control, and water supply. Aimed at year-round farming. Limitation: Poor maintenance, corruption, mismanagement.
4. Agricultural (Control of Importation) ActRegulates importation of agricultural products to protect local farmers. Bans or tariffs on certain imports. Limitation: Smuggling and porous borders.
5. Operation Feed the Nation (OFN) – 1976Programme by General Olusegun Obasanjo to increase food production and encourage youth participation. Involved training, inputs, school farms. Limited success due to poor planning and lack of continuity.
6. Green Revolution Programme (1980)Initiated by President Shehu Shagari to boost food production and reduce importation. Distributed seeds, fertilizers, machinery. Limited success due to poor implementation.
7. Agricultural Promotion Policy (APP) – 2016 to 2020Also known as "The Green Alternative." Focused on food security, job creation, and value addition. Contributed to increased rice and cassava production. Challenges: Insecurity and infrastructure deficit.

8.4 Effects of Agricultural Laws and Reforms

  1. Improved Land Accessibility: Land Use Act made it easier for large-scale farmers to access land.
  2. Increased Credit Availability: ACGS improved farmers' access to loans.
  3. Boost in Food Production: Green Revolution and APP encouraged food production.
  4. Modernisation of Agriculture: Reforms facilitated mechanisation and value addition.
  5. Youth Engagement: OFN targeted youth and educated citizens.
  6. Policy Continuity Challenges: Frequent changes in government lead to abrupt termination of programmes.

8.5 Agencies Responsible for Agricultural Laws and Reforms

AgencyRole
Federal Ministry of Agriculture and Food SecurityOversees policy formulation, implementation, and supervision of national agricultural projects.
Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN)Manages agricultural credit schemes and provides financial support for agribusiness.
Nigerian Agricultural Insurance Corporation (NAIC)Provides insurance cover to farmers against natural disasters and risks.
National Agricultural Land Development Authority (NALDA)Facilitates land development and management for agricultural use.
River Basin Development Authorities (RBDAs)Responsible for irrigation and water supply for agriculture.
Nigerian Export Promotion Council (NEPC)Promotes export of Nigerian agricultural products and value chain development.

WEEK 9Land and Its Uses

Content: Meaning of land, characteristics, uses of land, and importance of land use planning.

Performance Objectives

  1. Define land and state its characteristics.
  2. Explain nine major uses of land.
  3. Discuss the importance of proper land use planning.

9.1 Meaning of Land

Land refers to the solid surface of the earth not covered by water, including the soil, minerals, water tables, and rocks beneath it. In agriculture, land is the portion of the earth's surface used for cultivating crops, rearing animals, and other farming activities. It is a fixed resource — cannot be increased or manufactured.

9.2 Characteristics of Land

  • Immobile: Cannot be moved from one place to another.
  • Inelastic in supply: Fixed in quantity.
  • Varies in fertility: Texture, slope, drainage, chemical composition differ.
  • Subject to degradation: Can be degraded by erosion, pollution, overuse.

9.3 Uses of Land

UseDescription
1. Agricultural UseCrop farming, livestock rearing, fish farming, horticulture. Cropland, pastureland, fish ponds.
2. ForestryGrowing trees in forests (natural or plantations) for timber, fuelwood, fruits, herbs, resins. Also protects watersheds, biodiversity.
3. Wildlife ConservationLand set aside for game reserves and national parks (e.g., Yankari Game Reserve, Cross River National Park).
4. Residential UseBuilding homes, villages, towns, cities. Urban expansion competes with agricultural land.
5. Industrial UseFactories, warehouses, business complexes. Processing plants for agricultural products.
6. Recreational UseParks, playgrounds, sports fields, tourism centres.
7. Transportation InfrastructureRoads, railways, airports, harbours. Roads connect rural farms to urban markets.
8. Religious and Cultural PurposesChurches, mosques, shrines, cemeteries. Traditional festivals require open land spaces.
9. Mining and ExtractionMinerals, crude oil, clay. Mining requires land for exploration, extraction, processing.

9.4 Importance of Proper Land Use Planning

  • Ensures efficient and sustainable use of limited land.
  • Prevents land disputes.
  • Reduces environmental degradation.
  • Promotes equitablemiddle development.
  • Protects agricultural lands from unnecessary conversion to urban or industrial areas.

WEEK 10Agricultural Ecology

Content: Definition, components, importance, and relationship between living and non-living components.

Performance Objectives

  1. Define agricultural ecology (agroecology).
  2. Explain the biotic and abiotic components of an agroecosystem.
  3. State the importance of agricultural ecology.
  4. Describe the relationship between living and non-living components.

10.1 Definition of Agricultural Ecology

Agricultural ecology (agroecology) is the branch of science that deals with the relationship between crops, livestock, and the surrounding environment. It explores how living organisms (plants, animals, microorganisms) interact with non-living elements (soil, water, air, climate) within farming systems. The aim is to produce food in a way that is economically viable and environmentally sustainable.

10.2 Components of an Agroecosystem

ComponentDescription
Biotic (Living)Crops, livestock, beneficial organisms (earthworms, bees), decomposers (fungi, bacteria), pests and parasites.
Abiotic (Non-Living)Soil, water, air, sunlight, temperature, rainfall, wind.

10.3 Importance of Agricultural Ecology

  1. Promotes Sustainable Agriculture: Encourages farming methods friendly to the environment; reduces reliance on artificial chemicals.
  2. Enhances Soil Fertility and Productivity: Teaches natural soil maintenance (crop rotation, cover cropping, organic manure).
  3. Supports Natural Pest and Disease Control: Embraces biodiversity (birds, frogs, predatory insects) and intercropping.
  4. Facilitates Efficient Use of Resources: Encourages rainwater harvesting, mulching, drip irrigation, agroforestry.
  5. Improves Climate Resilience: Mixed farming and organic soil management help farms withstand droughts, floods, and pest outbreaks.
  6. Encourages Integrated Farming Systems: Combines crops, livestock, aquaculture, and forestry; waste from one part becomes a resource for another.
  7. Reduces Environmental Pollution: Advocates organic inputs, recycling, composting, and green manure.
  8. Strengthens Food Security and Nutrition: Promotes cultivation of a wide variety of crops, including traditional and indigenous foods.

10.4 Relationship Between Living and Non-Living Components

In an agroecosystem, the interaction between living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) elements is continuous and essential:

  • Plants (living) depend on sunlight (non-living) for photosynthesis.
  • Soil (non-living) provides physical support and essential nutrients to plants.
  • Microorganisms in the soil (living) break down organic matter into forms plants can use.
  • Animals feed on crops (living), and their waste returns nutrients to the soil (non-living).

FactorExplanation
