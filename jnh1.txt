WEEK 1: INTRODUCTION TO HISTORY
MEANING OF HISTORY
History is the study of past human events, societies, and experiences. It involves the record of
how people lived, the challenges they faced, their achievements, and how these past events shape
the present and future. It explains the origin of communities, nations, and institutions.
In simple terms, history is the story of mankind’s past, written or passed down, that helps us to
understand who we are and where we are coming from.
IMPORTANCE OF STUDYING HISTORY
1. Understanding the past – History teaches us about the lives of our ancestors, their
struggles, successe, and failures.
3
2. Explains identity – It helps us know our origin, culture, and traditions, thereby building
a sense of belonging.
3. Promotes patriotism – By learning about Nigeria’s heroes, struggles for independence,
and cultural heritage, citizens develop love for the country.
4. Guides decision-making – Past mistakes and successes help leaders and individuals
make wiser decisions.
5. Appreciation of cultural diversity – History shows how different groups and regions
contributed to Nigeria’s development.
6. Encourages national unity – Knowing the shared history of Nigeria helps to strengthen
unity among ethnic groups.
7. Preservation of heritage – History preserves values, customs, and traditions for future
generations.
4
SOURCES OF NIGERIAN HISTORY
Historians use different sources to gather information about the past. These sources include:
1. Oral Sources – Stories, legends, folktales, proverbs, and accounts passed down by word
of mouth from one generation to another. For example, traditional griots or elders
narrating past events.
2. Written Sources – Documents, books, colonial records, newspapers, diaries, and
government publications that provide detailed accounts of historical events.
3. Archaeology – The scientific study of material remains of past human life and activities
such as bones, ruins, and fossils. Archaeologists dig and study remains to reconstruct the
past.
4. Artifacts – Man-made objects such as tools, pottery, weapons, ornaments, sculptures,
and household materials used in ancient times. They give us direct evidence of how
people lived.
5
5. Traditions and Customs – Practices, festivals, and cultural beliefs that have been
preserved and handed down over time. They tell us about values, governance, and
lifestyles of different Nigerian societies.
EVALUATION

2. State four importance of history.
3. Mention five sources of Nigerian history.
4. Differentiate between oral and written sources of history.
5. Why is history important for national unity?

3. Write short notes on two Nigerian cultural practices that serve as sources of history.

WEEK 2: NIGERIAN PEOPLES AND LANGUAGES
INTRODUCTION TO NIGERIAN PEOPLES AND LANGUAGES
 Nigeria is the most populous country in Africa with over 200 million people.
 It is made up of more than 250 ethnic groups and over 500 languages, making it one of
the most diverse nations in the world.
 These groups differ in language, culture, traditions, and way of life, yet all belong to one
nation—Nigeria.
7
MAJOR ETHNIC GROUPS AND THEIR LOCATIONS
Although Nigeria has many ethnic groups, the three largest groups are:
1. Hausa-Fulani
o Location: Northern Nigeria (Kano, Sokoto, Katsina, Kaduna, Borno, etc.).
o Language: Hausa and Fulfulde.
o Religion: Mostly Islam.
o Occupation: Farming, cattle rearing, trading, crafts, and politics.
2. Yoruba
8
o Location: Southwestern Nigeria (Lagos, Oyo, Ogun, Ondo, Osun, Ekiti, Kwara,
part of Kogi).
o Language: Yoruba.
o Religion: Christianity, Islam, and traditional religion.
o Occupation: Farming (cocoa, kola nut), trading, craftsmanship, and civil service.
3. Igbo
o Location: Southeastern Nigeria (Anambra, Imo, Enugu, Abia, Ebonyi, part of
Delta).
o Language: Igbo.
o Religion: Christianity and traditional religion.
o Occupation: Farming (yam, cassava, palm oil), trading, business, and crafts.
OTHER MAJOR ETHNIC GROUPS IN NIGERIA
 Kanuri – Found in Borno and Yobe States, mostly Muslims, known for farming and
cattle rearing.
 Tiv – Located in Benue State, famous for yam farming.
 Ijaw – Found in the Niger Delta region (Bayelsa, Rivers, Delta), fishermen and oil
workers.
 Ibibio/Efik/Annang – Located in Akwa Ibom and Cross River States, known for fishing,
trading, and rich culture.
 Nupe – Found in Niger State, engaged in farming and crafts.
 Gwari (Gbagyi) – Indigenous to the Middle Belt, especially Niger and Abuja, known for

 Edo (Bini) – Found in Edo State, known for their historical kingdom and arts.

 Jukun – Found in Taraba and parts of Benue, known for farming and hunting.
 Idoma – Found in Benue State, farmers and traders.

 Nigeria’s linguistic diversity reflects its cultural richness.
 There are over 500 distinct languages, with Hausa, Yoruba, and Igbo as the most widely

 English is the official language used for education, government, and business because of

 Language plays a major role in shaping identity, values, music, literature, and traditions.

6. Challenges of Nigeria’s Diversity
1. Ethnic rivalry and conflicts.
2. Language barriers in communication.

WEEK 3: EARLY SETTLEMENTS IN NIGERIA (NOK, IGBO-UKWU, IFE, BENIN)
INTRODUCTION
Long before Europeans came, Nigerians had great settlements and kingdoms. These places
showed that our ancestors were very skilled in farming, metal work, trade, and art. The four
important ones we will study are:
 Nok culture
 Igbo-Ukwu
 Ife (Ile-Ife)
 Benin Kingdom
Nok Culture (500 BC – 200 AD)
 Location: Found on the Jos Plateau area (Plateau, Kaduna, Niger States).
12
 Economy: They were farmers and hunters.
 Technology: First known people in West Africa to make iron tools. This helped them
farm better and defend themselves.
 Art: Famous for their terracotta (clay) sculptures of human heads and figures. They had
big eyes, detailed hairstyles, and ornaments.
 Importance: Nok shows that very early Nigerians were creative and advanced in art and
iron work.
Igbo-Ukwu (about 9th Century AD)
 Location: Near Onitsha, Anambra State (South-East Nigeria).
 Discovery: Archaeologists found amazing bronze and copper objects in 1938 and 1959.
 Technology: Used lost-wax method to make beautiful and detailed bronze works (bowls,
ornaments, ritual objects).
 Trade: Glass beads and foreign materials found show that they traded with distant places
across Africa and beyond.
13
 Importance: Igbo-Ukwu proves that people in Igboland were rich, organised, and had
skilled craftsmen many centuries ago.
Ife (12th – 15th Century AD)
 Location: South-West Nigeria, the sacred city of the Yoruba people.
 Belief: Ife is seen as the origin of the Yoruba race, where their ancestor Oduduwa first
ruled.
14
 Art: Known for natural-looking bronze and terracotta heads made by the lost-wax
method. They are very realistic, showing faces, scarifications, crowns and ornaments.
 Society: Ife was both a religious and political centre, with kings (Oonis), palaces,
markets, shrines, and skilled workers.
 Importance: Ife art shows that Nigerians could make world-class realistic sculptures long
before colonial times.
Benin Kingdom (13th – 19th Century AD)
 Location: Present Edo State.
 Leadership: Ruled by powerful kings called Obas.
 Government: Well organised with chiefs, warriors, and palace officials.
15
 Art: Produced famous Benin Bronzes (plaques, statues, and ivories) that decorated the
Oba’s palace. They showed scenes of royal life, battles, and ceremonies.
 Trade: Traded pepper, ivory, and later slaves with the Portuguese from the 15th century.
 History: In 1897, the British attacked Benin, looted thousands of bronzes and took them
to Europe. Today, some are being returned.
 Importance: Benin shows the strength of early Nigerian kingdoms in politics, trade, and
art.

WEEK 4: ORIGINS OF NIGERIAN SOCIETIES
CONTENT
Meaning of Origin
The term origin refers to the beginning or the way something or a group of people came into
existence. Every society has an account of its origin, which explains how the people believe they
started as a community or nation. In Nigerian history, origins are usually explained through
myths, legends, and oral traditions passed from one generation to another.
A myth of origin is a traditional story that explains how a group of people believe they came
into existence. Though not always scientifically proven, these myths provide valuable insight
into the worldview, values, and traditions of Nigerian societies.
MYTHS OF ORIGIN OF NIGERIAN SOCIETIES
17
1. Yoruba Myth of Origin
o The Yoruba people trace their origin to Oduduwa, regarded as the ancestor and
progenitor of the Yoruba race.
o According to the myth, Oduduwa descended from heaven with a chain and a cock,
carrying a handful of sand. He poured the sand on water and the cock spread it to
form land.
o He settled at Ile-Ife, which became the spiritual and cultural cradle of the Yoruba
people.
o Oduduwa had many children who later founded different Yoruba kingdoms such
as Oyo, Ife, Ijebu, Ondo, and Benin (influenced by Yoruba tradition).
o This myth emphasizes the sacredness of Ile-Ife as the origin of the Yoruba
civilization.
2. Hausa Myth of Origin
18
o The Hausa people trace their origin to the legend of Bayajidda, a prince from
Baghdad in the Middle East.
o According to the story, Bayajidda migrated to Borno and later to Daura, where he
killed a sacred snake (Sarki) that prevented the people from fetching water from
the well.
o In gratitude, he married the Queen of Daura (Daurama). Their union produced
children who became the founders of the Hausa Bakwai (the seven legitimate
Hausa states): Daura, Kano, Katsina, Zazzau (Zaria), Gobir, Rano, and Biram.
o The myth highlights foreign contact, migration, and the establishment of
centralized Hausa city-states.
3. Igbo Myth of Origin
o The Igbo people have diverse traditions of origin, as the Igbo society is
decentralized.
o One popular myth states that the Igbo originated from Nri (in present-day
Anambra State), where Eri, considered a sky being, was sent by God (Chukwu) to
establish the Igbo community.
o Another account says that the Igbo descended from Jewish or Hebrew ancestry,
though this is largely based on later interpretations.
o The Nri kingdom became a spiritual and cultural center for the Igbo, emphasizing
religion, taboos, and morality.
4. Other Nigerian Myths of Origin
o Benin People: The Benin trace their origin to the Ogiso dynasty, with the first
Ogiso, Igodo, ruling at Igodomigodo. Later, the people invited a son of Oduduwa
from Ife to rule as Oba, leading to the establishment of the Oba dynasty.
o Ijaw People: The Ijaw trace their origin to the Niger Delta region, where they
believe their ancestors emerged as one of the earliest settlers of the area,
depending on fishing and water transport.
19
o Nupe People: The Nupe have traditions that link their origin to Tsoede, a heroic
figure who is said to have united various groups into one kingdom.
IMPORTANCE OF MYTHS OF ORIGIN
1. They provide a sense of identity and belonging to different groups.
2. They strengthen unity and loyalty among members of a society.
3. They explain the migration patterns and settlement of various Nigerian peoples.
4. They help preserve cultural and traditional values for future generations.
5. They provide historians with clues about Nigeria’s early societies and cultural
development.
EVALUATION
1. Define myths of origin.
2. Explain the Yoruba myth of origin.
3. Who was Bayajidda, and why is he important in Hausa history?
4. State two accounts of Igbo origin.
5. Mention two other Nigerian ethnic groups and their myths of origin.

WEEK 6: TRADITIONAL POLITICAL SYSTEMS I
MEANING OF TRADITIONAL POLITICAL SYSTEM
 A traditional political system refers to the method by which pre-colonial Nigerian
societies were governed before the arrival of Europeans.
 These systems were based on the customs, traditions, and values of the people.
 They ensured law and order, promoted unity, and managed conflicts within the
community.
 Two good examples are the centralized Yoruba kingdoms and the acephalous
(stateless) Igbo village democracy.
21
YORUBA KINGDOMS (OYO AND IFE)
a. Oyo Empire
 System: Centralized monarchy with checks and balances.
 Ruler: The Alaafin of Oyo, who was regarded as the supreme ruler and semi-divine.
 Council of State (Oyo Mesi): A powerful council of seven kingmakers led by the
Bashorun. They advised the Alaafin and could compel him to commit suicide if he ruled
unjustly.
 Ogboni Cult: A secret society that represented the voice of the people and acted as a
check on both the Alaafin and Oyo Mesi.
 Army: Headed by the Are Ona Kakanfo, a powerful general in charge of defense and
expansion.
 Features of Oyo Political System:
1. Highly organized and centralized.
2. Clear separation of powers between king, council, people, and army.
3. Strong system of checks and balances.
4. A mix of monarchy, aristocracy, and popular participation.
b. Ife Kingdom
 Ruler: The Ooni of Ife, regarded as the spiritual head of the Yoruba and custodian of
traditions.
 Council of Chiefs: Assisted the Ooni in administration and governance.
 Religious Authority: Ife was considered the spiritual center of the Yoruba people; the
Ooni was more of a religious and cultural leader than a political conqueror.
 Features of Ife Political System:
1. More decentralized than Oyo.
22
2. Strong emphasis on religion, culture, and tradition.
3. Less military-oriented compared to Oyo.
IGBO VILLAGE DEMOCRACY
 The Igbo people practiced a decentralized or acephalous (headless) political system.
 There was no single king or centralized ruler; instead, governance was at the village and
community level.
 Key Institutions:
o Council of Elders (Ndichie): Made important decisions and resolved disputes.
o Age Grades: Organized groups of young men and women responsible for
community development, defense, and law enforcement.
o Title Holders (Ozo, Nze, etc.): Respected individuals who held influence in
decision-making.
o Village Assembly: All adult males participated in decision-making. This was a
form of direct democracy.
o Priests and Oracles: Spiritual leaders who played advisory roles, e.g., the Oracle
of Arochukwu.
 Features of Igbo Political System:
1. Decentralized and democratic.
2. Collective decision-making through consensus.
3. Strong community participation in governance.
4. Authority was spread across elders, title holders, age grades, and religious leaders.
23

WEEK 8: TRADITIONAL POLITICAL SYSTEMS II
THE HAUSA/FULANI EMIRATE SYSTEM
Origin and Development:
 The Hausa city-states (Kano, Katsina, Zaria, Gobir, Daura, etc.) existed before the Fulani
Jihad of 1804.
 The Jihad, led by Usman dan Fodio, established Islamic rule and created the Sokoto
Caliphate, the largest empire in 19th-century West Africa.
 The Fulani introduced a centralized emirate system under Islamic law (Sharia).
Structure of the Emirate System:
26
 Sultan/Caliph of Sokoto: Supreme religious and political head of the caliphate.
 Emirs: Governed different emirates (e.g., Kano, Zaria, Katsina) under the authority of
the Sultan.
 District Heads (Hakimai): Administered districts, collected taxes, and maintained law
and order.
 Village Heads (Dagatai/Mai-Unguwa): Controlled villages and reported to district
heads.
 Ward Heads (Masu Unguwa): Lowest officials responsible for families and compounds.
 Judicial System: Based on Sharia law, with judges (Alkali) handling cases.
Features of the Hausa/Fulani Emirate System:
27
1. Highly centralized and theocratic (religion + politics combined).
2. Power flowed from the Sultan down to emirs, district heads, and village heads.
3. Administration was based on Islamic principles.
4. Strong taxation system (cattle tax jangali, land tax kharaj, personal tax zakkat).
5. Large standing army loyal to the Emirate and Jihad leaders.
KANEM-BORNU EMPIRE
Origin and Location:
28
 One of the oldest kingdoms in Nigeria, existing from around the 9th century.
 Located in the Lake Chad region, covering present-day Borno, Yobe, Chad, and Niger.
 Originally Kanem, later merged with Bornu under Mai Ali Ghaji and reached its peak
under Mai Idris Alooma (1571–1603).
Structure of Government:
 Mai (King): Supreme ruler, both political and spiritual leader.
 Council of State: Senior officials and noblemen who advised the Mai.
 Galadima: Administrator of the capital.
 Yerima: In charge of the military and defense.
 Kashim: Treasurer who controlled finance.
 Other Officials: Controlled provinces, ensured law and order, and reported directly to
the Mai.
Features of Kanem-Bornu Political System:
1. Centralized monarchy headed by the Mai.
2. Islam played a significant role in administration, especially under Idris Alooma.
3. Strong military organization – Idris Alooma introduced guns and horses for cavalry.
4. Efficient taxation system supported by trade in slaves, horses, kola nuts, and salt.
5. Diplomatic relations with North Africa and the Middle East.
6. Stability through a blend of traditional customs and Islamic practices.

WEEK 9: NIGERIAN ART AND CULTURE
MEANING OF NIGERIAN ART AND CULTURE
 Art: The creative expression of human thought, imagination, and skill through various
forms such as painting, sculpture, music, dance, textiles, and crafts.
 Culture: The total way of life of a people, including their values, traditions, religion,
language, dressing, food, festivals, music, art, and social institutions.
 Nigerian art and culture reflect the diversity of the over 250 ethnic groups in the country
and serve as a means of identity and unity.
32
TRADITIONAL ART FORMS IN NIGERIA
Nigeria is blessed with ancient and diverse artistic traditions. Some major examples include:
1. Sculpture and Carving:
o Nok Culture (500 BC – 200 AD): Famous for terracotta (clay) figurines.
o Ife Art: Known for naturalistic bronze and terracotta heads.
o Benin Art: Renowned for bronze plaques, ivory carvings, and court art.
o Esie Soapstone Figures: Thousands of carved stone figures found in Kwara State.
2. Textiles and Weaving:
33
o Aso-Oke (Yoruba handwoven cloth).
o Akwete cloth (Igbo, woven by women).
o Adire (tie and dye cloth of Abeokuta).
3. Pottery and Ceramics:
o Practiced by the Gwari (Abuja), Nupe (Bida), and Igbo (Okigwe).
4. Beadwork and Jewelry:
o Extensively used by Yoruba, Benin, and Igbo royalty.
5. Architecture:
34
o Traditional mud houses, decorated walls, shrines, and palaces.
o Famous examples include the Kano city walls, Sukur cultural landscape, and
Zaria’s traditional architecture.
6. Performing Arts:
o Drumming, dancing, storytelling, masquerades, and chants.
o Traditional instruments include talking drums (dùndún), flutes, xylophones,
shekere, and gongs.
FESTIVALS IN NIGERIA
35
Festivals are cultural celebrations that unite communities and preserve tradition.
Types of Festivals:
 Traditional Festivals:
o Osun-Osogbo Festival (Osun State, Yoruba – for the river goddess).
o New Yam Festival (Igbo – thanksgiving for harvest).
o Durbar Festival (Hausa/Fulani – horse riding parades during Eid celebrations).
o Argungu Fishing Festival (Kebbi State – fishing competition).
o Eyo Festival (Lagos – unique Yoruba masquerade parade).
 Religious Festivals: Eid-el-Fitr, Eid-el-Kabir, Christmas, Easter.
 National/Cultural Festivals: Abuja Carnival, FESTAC ’77 (Festival of Arts and
Culture).
Importance of Festivals:
1. Preserve cultural heritage.
2. Strengthen community identity and unity.
3. Provide entertainment and tourism.
4. Serve as thanksgiving and spiritual renewal.
5. Promote Nigeria’s image internationally.
CULTURAL IDENTITY
 Definition: Cultural identity is the shared sense of belonging and recognition that comes
from one’s cultural background. It reflects the values, customs, language, dressing, and
traditions of a people.
36
Ways Nigerians Express Cultural Identity:
1. Traditional dressing (agbada, isiagu, babariga, iro and buba).
2. Native languages (Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, Tiv, Ibibio, etc.).
3. Food (amala, tuwo, pounded yam, fufu, suya, nkwobi).
4. Music, dance, and festivals.
5. Respect for elders and communal living.
Importance of Cultural Identity:
1. Promotes pride in one’s heritage.
2. Helps preserve indigenous knowledge and values.
3. Encourages unity in diversity.
4. Protects society from cultural erosion by foreign influences.
5. Enhances national development through cultural tourism.

WEEK 10: EARLY TRADE AND EXCHANGE
MEANING OF TRADE AND EXCHANGE
 Trade is the buying, selling, or exchange of goods and services between individuals,
communities, or nations.
 Exchange is the act of giving one thing in return for another, either through barter
(goods for goods) or with the use of money.
 In early Nigerian societies, trade was an important activity that promoted survival,
cultural interaction, and unity.
39
LOCAL TRADE IN PRE-COLONIAL NIGERIA
Definition: Local trade refers to the exchange of goods within nearby communities or regions of
Nigeria.
Features of Local Trade:
1. Conducted within towns, villages, and neighboring communities.
2. Often based on barter system (exchange of goods for goods) before the introduction of
cowries and manillas.
3. Goods traded were mostly farm produce, crafts, livestock, salt, and pottery.
4. Weekly or periodic markets were common (e.g., Eke, Orie, Afo, Nkwo in Igboland).
40
Examples of Local Trade Goods:
 Salt from Awka (Igbo) and Ebonyi areas.
 Fish from riverine areas.
 Kolanuts from Yoruba and Hausa regions.
 Palm oil and palm kernels from Igbo land.
 Pottery from Gwari and Nupe people.

WEEK 1: ISLAM IN NIGERIA
INTRODUCTION TO ISLAM IN NIGERIA
Islam is one of the major religions in Nigeria today. It originated in Arabia in the 7th century
A.D. through the teachings of Prophet Muhammad (SAW). The religion spread rapidly across
North Africa and later into West Africa through trade, migration, scholarship, and jihad.
In Nigeria, Islam first entered through the Kanem-Borno Empire around the 11th century and
later spread into the Hausa states. It was not forced at first, but it grew gradually through contact
with North African and Arab traders who brought Islamic teachings along with their goods.
SPREAD OF ISLAM IN NIGERIA
45
The spread of Islam in Nigeria happened in stages and through different agents:
1. Trade
o Trans-Saharan trade routes brought North African merchants into contact with
Kanem-Borno, Hausa states, and other parts of Northern Nigeria.
o These traders not only exchanged goods such as salt, kolanuts, leather, and slaves
but also introduced the religion of Islam.
2. Migration and Intermarriage
o Arab and Berber traders who settled in parts of Northern Nigeria married local
women, thereby spreading Islam within families and communities.
3. Rulers and Political Leaders
o Some kings and rulers converted to Islam, making it the religion of the royal court.
This encouraged their subjects to follow suit. For example, Mai Ume Jilmi of
Kanem-Borno accepted Islam in the 11th century.
4. Islamic Scholars and Clerics
o Muslim scholars and teachers, such as Mallams, played a great role in spreading
Islamic teachings, building schools, and encouraging literacy in Arabic.
46
5. The Jihad of Uthman dan Fodio
o In the early 19th century, Shehu Uthman dan Fodio led a religious reform
movement (the Sokoto Jihad) in Hausaland.
o The movement spread Islam widely across the Hausa states and established the
Sokoto Caliphate, one of the largest Islamic empires in Africa.
6. Islamic Education
o Quranic schools were established across Northern Nigeria, which taught children
how to read and write in Arabic and learn Islamic values.
7. Colonial Period and After
o Even during British colonial rule, Islam continued to spread peacefully, especially
with the growth of urban centers, trade, and Islamic organizations.
IMPACT OF ISLAM ON NIGERIAN SOCIETY
1. Religious Impact
o Islam introduced monotheism (belief in one God – Allah).
o It brought new forms of worship such as daily prayers, fasting, almsgiving (zakat),
and pilgrimage (hajj).
47
2. Educational Impact
o Islam introduced Quranic and Arabic education, which encouraged literacy and
scholarship.
o Many centers of learning were established in Kano, Katsina, Borno, and Sokoto.
3. Political Impact
o The Sokoto Caliphate created a large centralized Islamic state, which influenced
governance based on Islamic law (Sharia).
o Leaders were expected to rule justly according to Islamic principles.
4. Cultural Impact
o Islam influenced dressing styles such as wearing hijabs, turbans, and flowing
gowns.
o It shaped marriage customs, burial rites, and dietary laws (halal food).
5. Social Impact
o It promoted values such as honesty, charity, respect for elders, and care for the
poor.
o It brought about unity among Muslims across ethnic groups through shared
practices and festivals like Eid-el-Fitr and Eid-el-Kabir.
6. Economic Impact
o Islam encouraged trade and commerce by stressing fairness and honesty in
business.
o Pilgrimage routes also boosted international trade between Nigeria and other
Muslim countries.

WEEK 2: CHRISTIANITY IN NIGERIA
INTRODUCTION TO CHRISTIANITY IN NIGERIA
Christianity is one of the major religions in Nigeria today. It is based on the teachings of Jesus
Christ, who lived and preached in Palestine over 2,000 years ago. The religion spread from the
Middle East to Europe, and later to Africa through missionary activities, trade, colonization, and
migration.
Christianity was first introduced to Nigeria in the 15th century through the activities of the
Portuguese Catholic missionaries, but it did not last because of lack of support and the
dominance of the slave trade at that time. The more lasting introduction of Christianity began in
the 19th century with the arrival of European missionaries from Britain, such as the Church
Missionary Society (CMS), Roman Catholic Mission (RCM), Methodist Mission, Baptist
Mission, and others.

 The Wesleyan Methodist Mission.
 The Baptist Mission from the USA.
 The Roman Catholic Mission.
2. Role of Freed Slaves
o Freed slaves who returned to Nigeria from Sierra Leone and Brazil (such as
Samuel Ajayi Crowther) played an important role in spreading Christianity.

catechists, and clergy.
3. Translation of the Bible
o The translation of the Bible into Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa, and other Nigerian
languages made Christianity more accessible to the local people.

6. Urbanization and Migration

burial rites.

5. Social Impact
o Christianity promoted social welfare by building hospitals, orphanages, and health

MEANING OF TRANS-ATLANTIC SLAVE TRADE
 The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade was the buying and selling of African people as slaves
across the Atlantic Ocean to Europe and the Americas between the 15th and 19th
centuries.
 It is also called the Triangular Trade because it connected three continents:
1. Europe (manufactured goods such as guns, cloth, beads, alcohol).
2. Africa (slaves captured and sold).
60
3. Americas/Caribbean (plantations producing sugar, cotton, tobacco, coffee using
slave labour).
 Nigeria, especially ports like Bonny, Brass, Calabar, Lagos, and Badagry, played a
major role in supplying slaves.
CAUSES OF THE SLAVE TRADE
1. High Demand for Labour in the Americas
o Europeans needed cheap labour to work on sugar, tobacco, and cotton plantations.
2. European Quest for Wealth
o Slavery was highly profitable for European merchants.
3. African Internal Conflicts
o Frequent wars and raids among African communities provided captives that were
sold into slavery.
4. Availability of Slave Markets
o African chiefs and middlemen cooperated with Europeans in capturing and selling
slaves.
5. Greed and Corruption
o Some African leaders and traders exchanged human beings for European goods
(e.g., guns, gin, mirrors, cloth).
6. Weak African Political Systems
o The inability of fragmented states to resist European exploitation.
EFFECTS OF THE TRANS-ATLANTIC SLAVE TRADE ON NIGERIA
Negative Effects:
61
1. Depopulation: Millions of Nigerians, especially the young and strong, were taken away.
2. Wars and Insecurity: Communities raided one another to capture slaves, leading to
instability.
3. Breakdown of Traditional Societies: Families and villages were destroyed as members
were sold.
4. Economic Damage: Productive labour force was lost; agriculture and industries declined.
5. Moral Decay: Human life was devalued, and corruption increased as chiefs sold their
own people.
6. Introduction of Firearms: Increased violence and wars in Nigeria.
7. Legacy of Hatred and Distrust: Created division among Nigerian communities.
8. Foundation for Colonization: Slave trade weakened African societies, making it easier
for Europeans to later colonize Nigeria.
Positive (Minor) Effects:
1. Introduction of new crops such as cassava, maize, and pineapple from the Americas.
2. Some parts of Nigeria (like Lagos) later developed as trading centers during the shift to
legitimate trade.
3. Exposure of Nigerians to new cultures and goods (though this also destroyed traditional
systems).

 By the early 19th century, the trans-Atlantic slave trade began to decline due to:

3. Efforts of Christian missionaries who condemned slavery.

MEANING OF ABOLITION OF SLAVE TRADE
 Abolition means putting an end to something that has been in practice.
 The abolition of slave trade was the process of ending the capture, buying, and selling

 It started in the late 18th century and became strong in the 19th century, when

o The Industrial Revolution in Europe created new factories that needed workers
who were free and paid, not enslaved.

o Europeans discovered that they could make more profit trading in palm oil, cotton,
and groundnuts (for industries) than in selling human beings.
 Political reasons
o Enlightenment thinkers in Europe and America promoted ideas of freedom,
justice, and equality.
64
o Countries like Britain wanted to be seen as civilised leaders by ending the cruel
trade.
 Slave resistance
o There were constant slave revolts on plantations and ships.
o Example: The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), led by enslaved Africans,
frightened Europeans and showed that slavery could not last forever.
o In West Africa, communities resisted slave raiders, making the trade more

WEEK 10: AMALGAMATION OF 1914
MEANING OF AMALGAMATION
 Amalgamation means joining two or more things together to form one.
 In Nigerian history, it refers to the merging of the Northern Protectorate and the
Southern Protectorate by the British colonial government.
 This was done by Lord Frederick Lugard, who became the first Governor-General of
Nigeria.
 The event took place on 1st January 1914.
 From that day, Nigeria officially became one single country (though Nigerians were not
asked if they wanted it).
REASONS FOR THE AMALGAMATION
The Northern Protectorate had very little money to run its administration. In fact, Britain was
spending more money than they were making to govern the North. Meanwhile, the South,
especially Lagos, was rich with trade in palm oil, cocoa, and other resources. To solve this, Lord
Lugard and the British government decided: “Why not join North and South together, so the
South’s money can help pay for the North?”
 Economic reasons
o The North was poor and couldn’t pay for itself, but the South had revenue.
o Britain wanted to use one economy for the whole of Nigeria.
o It made it easier to exploit resources (palm oil, cocoa, groundnuts).
 Administrative convenience
o Running two regions separately was expensive.
o With amalgamation, Britain could have one governor, one railway system, one
port system, and one civil service.
70
 Expansion of trade and transport
o Linking North and South by roads and railways allowed goods like groundnuts,
hides, palm oil, and cocoa to move faster to the coast for export.
 Political reasons
o A united Nigeria gave Britain stronger control.
o It also reduced rivalry between Northern and Southern administrations.
IMPACT OF THE AMALGAMATION
Imagine a huge wedding where the bride and groom never met each other, and nobody asked
them if they even wanted to marry. That was what happened in 1914. The North and South were
“married” by Britain without their consent.
Positive Impacts
 Nigeria became one political unit. This later made independence in 1960 possible.
 Transport and communication improved (railways, telegraphs).
 Trade and commerce grew across regions.
 Britain saved money on administration.
Negative Impacts
 Nigerians were not consulted, so it ignored cultural and religious differences.
 Different systems of government caused regional imbalance (Indirect Rule in the North
vs Western-style government in the South).
 It created the root of today’s ethnic and political tensions.
 The union was designed for Britain’s benefit, not Nigeria’s.
IMPORTANCE IN NIGERIAN HISTORY
71
 It was the foundation of modern Nigeria as one country.
 It marked the beginning of Nigerian nationalism (people started to see themselves as
“Nigerians” later on).
 It shows how colonial decisions shaped Nigeria’s unity and divisions.

WEEK 1: COLONIAL ADMINISTRATION
MEANING OF COLONIAL ADMINISTRATION
Colonial administration refers to the system of government established by European colonial
powers, particularly Britain, to control and manage their colonies. In Nigeria, colonial
administration was the structure and method used by the British to rule after they had conquered
and occupied the territory.
The main methods used in Nigeria were:
1. Indirect Rule – ruling through traditional rulers and existing political structures.
2. Direct Rule – ruling directly through European officials without relying on traditional
authorities.
74
INDIRECT RULE
Definition: Indirect rule is a system of colonial administration whereby the colonial masters
govern their colonies through the existing traditional rulers and institutions. It was introduced by
Lord Frederick Lugard in Northern Nigeria in the early 20th century.
Features of Indirect Rule
 Traditional rulers (Emirs, Obas, Obis, Chiefs) acted as intermediaries between the people
and the British colonial authorities.
 Local laws and customs were recognized but subject to approval by colonial officials.
 Native courts and native authorities were established.
 The colonial officers such as Residents and District Officers supervised the rulers.
 The system was cheaper for the British because it required fewer European officials.
Examples in Nigeria
 In Northern Nigeria, the British used the Emirs of the Sokoto Caliphate, who already had
centralized authority. This made indirect rule very successful there.
 In Western Nigeria, the British used the Obas and other chiefs, but modified their powers.
The system had some success.
 In Eastern Nigeria, where the Igbo had a decentralized system of government, indirect
rule failed because there were no strong traditional rulers. The attempt to use warrant
chiefs led to resistance, such as the Aba Women’s Riot of 1929.
Advantages of Indirect Rule
 Preserved local customs and traditions.
 Cheaper and easier for the colonial government to administer.
 Created minimal resistance in areas with centralized systems (e.g., the North).
 Helped maintain law and order at the local level.
Disadvantages of Indirect Rule
75
 Reduced the powers of traditional rulers, making them mere puppets of the British.
 Failed in decentralized societies like the Igbo because the people resisted imposed rulers.
 Encouraged corruption and abuse of power by some chiefs.
 Promoted division among Nigerians because colonial officials treated regions differently.
DIRECT RULE
Definition: Direct rule is a system of colonial administration in which the colonial masters
govern the people directly without making use of traditional rulers or institutions. It was
76
practiced mainly by the French in West Africa, but the British also applied it in some parts of
Nigeria where indirect rule failed.
Features of Direct Rule
 European officials (colonial officers, district commissioners) exercised direct authority.
 Indigenous political structures were disregarded or replaced.
 French colonies practiced assimilation, where Africans were expected to adopt French
culture and laws.
 The system was expensive because it required many Europeans to run the administration.
Examples in Nigeria
 In Igbo land, the British attempted direct rule after indirect rule failed. They introduced
warrant chiefs and appointed individuals who had no traditional legitimacy.
 In Lagos Colony, direct rule was practiced through British officials, as Lagos had been
annexed earlier in 1861 and administered like a British settlement.
Advantages of Direct Rule
 Allowed colonial officials to exercise total control.
 Bypassed resistant traditional institutions.
 Ensured quicker decision-making by colonial authorities.
Disadvantages of Direct Rule
 Very expensive, requiring many Europeans and resources.
 Disregarded local traditions and political systems, leading to resentment.
 Caused uprisings, protests, and resistance (e.g., Aba Women’s Riot of 1929).
 Alienated the people from their traditional rulers and customs.
COMPARISON BETWEEN INDIRECT RULE AND DIRECT RULE
77
 Use of Traditional Rulers: Indirect rule relied on traditional rulers, while direct rule
sidelined them.
 Cost: Indirect rule was cheaper; direct rule was more expensive.
 Acceptance: Indirect rule was more accepted in centralized societies; direct rule faced
more resistance.
 Implementation in Nigeria: Indirect rule worked well in the North, partially in the West,
and failed in the East. Direct rule was used in Lagos and parts of Igbo land.

4. Explain the meaning of direct rule and give two features.

WEEK 4: EDUCATION IN COLONIAL NIGERIA
MEANING OF COLONIAL EDUCATION
 Colonial education refers to the type of education introduced by European colonial
masters, especially the British, during their rule in Nigeria (19th–20th century).
 Before this, Nigerians had traditional education (learning from parents, elders,
apprenticeship, farming, crafts, etc.).
 When Europeans arrived, they brought missionary and Western-style schools.
MISSIONARY EDUCATION
 Missionaries (Christian church groups) were the first to introduce formal education in
Nigeria.
 They believed schools would help them spread Christianity.
89
 The first mission schools were established in the mid-1800s.
Examples:
 The Church Missionary Society (CMS) opened schools in Badagry and Abeokuta.
 Roman Catholic Mission, Methodist Mission, and Baptist Mission also started schools.
 In these schools, pupils learned:
o Reading, writing, arithmetic (the “3Rs”)
o Bible studies, hymns, and Christian morals
 Missionary schools were mostly built in the South (Yoruba and Igbo areas), because:
o Missionaries had more access to the coast.
o The North was mostly Muslim and resisted Christian schools.
WESTERN SCHOOLS AND GOVERNMENT INVOLVEMENT
 Later, the British colonial government joined in education.
90
 They set up schools to train clerks, interpreters, teachers, and messengers to help run
colonial offices.
 Examples:
o CMS Grammar School, Lagos (founded in 1859 by Rev. Thomas Babington
Macaulay) – first secondary school in Nigeria.
o Hope Waddell Training Institute, Calabar – focused on skills like carpentry,
agriculture, printing.
o King’s College, Lagos (1909) – prepared students for higher education and
government jobs.
 The government also introduced Education Ordinances (laws) to regulate and supervise
schools (e.g., 1882, 1887, 1916 ordinances).
EFFECTS OF COLONIAL EDUCATION
Positive Effects
 Nigerians gained literacy (reading and writing in English).
 It produced the first generation of African elites (educated Nigerians) like Herbert
Macaulay, Nnamdi Azikiwe, Obafemi Awolowo, and Ahmadu Bello.
 It created jobs in the colonial civil service.
 It gave rise to newspapers and political awareness.
 It prepared the ground for nationalism and the struggle for independence.
Negative Effects
 Education was concentrated in the South, leading to regional imbalance.
 It focused on producing clerks and interpreters, not scientists or engineers.
 It weakened Nigerian traditional values by promoting Western culture.
91
 The North lagged behind in education due to resistance to Western schools.
SUMMARY
 Missionaries introduced schools mainly to spread Christianity.
 The colonial government later supported education to train workers for administration.
 Colonial education had both positive and negative effects: it created educated Nigerians
but also caused imbalances and cultural disruption.

WEEK 10: ROAD TO INDEPENDENCE
INTRODUCTION
 The “Road to Independence” in Nigeria refers to the series of constitutional changes
introduced by the British colonial government between 1922 and 1960.
 These constitutions gradually allowed Nigerians to participate in government, though real
power remained with the British until independence in 1960.
 Four important constitutions marked this journey: Clifford (1922), Richards (1946),
Macpherson (1951), and Lyttleton (1954).
CLIFFORD CONSTITUTION (1922)
 Introduced after World War I by Governor Sir Hugh Clifford.
 Main Features:
o Created a Legislative Council with 46 members (27 officials, 19 unofficial).
o For the first time, Nigerians could elect representatives into the Legislative
Council (though only in Lagos and Calabar).
o Encouraged the growth of political associations and newspapers (e.g., Herbert
Macaulay’s NNDP).
 Weakness: Limited franchise (only wealthy men in Lagos and Calabar could vote).
RICHARDS CONSTITUTION (1946)
 Introduced by Governor Sir Arthur Richards.
 Main Features:
o Aimed to promote Nigerian unity while still protecting British interests.
106
o Created Regional Councils (North, West, East) and a Central Legislative
Council in Lagos.
o Allowed some Nigerians to discuss regional matters.
 Weakness: Nigerians were not fully consulted, so many nationalists rejected it.
MACPHERSON CONSTITUTION (1951)
 Introduced by Governor Sir John Macpherson after wide consultation with Nigerians.
 Main Features:
o Created House of Representatives at the central level.
o Regional Houses of Assembly had more powers.
o Allowed for the growth of political parties (e.g., NCNC, AG, NPC).
 Weakness: Regionalism increased as parties focused on their regions instead of Nigeria
as a whole.
LYTTLETON CONSTITUTION (1954)
 Introduced by Oliver Lyttleton, the British Colonial Secretary.
 Main Features:
o Made Nigeria a federal system of government with strong regions (North, West,
East, and later Mid-West).
o Regional governments had their own premiers and controlled many affairs.
o Central government handled defense, foreign affairs, and trade.
 Importance:
o Laid the foundation for independence.
107
o Nigerian leaders began to hold top positions in government.
SIGNIFICANCE OF THE CONSTITUTIONS
 Step-by-step transfer of power from Britain to Nigerians.
 Allowed the rise of political leaders like Nnamdi Azikiwe, Obafemi Awolowo, Ahmadu
Bello, and Tafawa Balewa.
 Helped Nigeria prepare for self-rule, leading to independence in 1960.
EVALUATION
1. Who introduced the Clifford Constitution, and in what year?
2. Mention two features of the Richards Constitution.
3. State one advantage and one weakness of the Macpherson Constitution.
4. Which constitution introduced federalism in Nigeria?
