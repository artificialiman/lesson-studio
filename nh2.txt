WEEK 1: THE SOKOTO CALIPHATE
1.1 Introduction: The Pre-Jihad Context in Hausaland
Before 1804, the Hausaland region (in modern-day Northern Nigeria) was not a single,
unified country. It was comprised of several independent and often rival city-states, known
as the Hausa Bakwai (the "seven true" Hausa states: Daura, Kano, Katsina, Zaria, Rano,
Gobir, and Biram) and the Banza Bakwai (the "seven "illegitimate" states).
These states were politically independent, ruled by kings (Sarkis), and had complex systems
of government. While Islam had been in Hausaland for centuries (introduced by traders and
clerics from Mali and Songhai), it was often practiced in a "syncretic" way. This means
traditional Hausa beliefs (Bori spirit possession, animism) were mixed with Islamic practices.
2
The rulers (Sarkis) often claimed to be Muslim but continued to engage in practices seen as
"pagan" by strict reformers. They also imposed heavy, arbitrary taxes on the people,
conscripted men into their armies, and were seen by many as corrupt and unjust.
1.2 Origin and Rise of the Caliphate: Usman dan Fodio
The Sokoto Caliphate was born out of a religious, social, and political reform movement
known as the 1804 Jihad. The central figure of this movement was Shehu Usman dan Fodio.
 Who was Usman dan Fodio?
o He was a Fulani scholar (cleric) and preacher, born in 1754 in the Hausa state
of Gobir.
o He was from the Toronkawa clan of Fulani, a group known for its Islamic
scholarship.
o He was a member of the Qadiriyya Sufi brotherhood.
o He spent his early life as an itinerant preacher, traveling through Gobir, Kebbi,
and Zamfara, teaching a "purified" version of Islam based strictly on the
Qur'an and Sunnah.
 His Preaching and Followers:
o Usman dan Fodio's preaching was revolutionary. He called for a tajdid
(renewal) of Islam.
o He attacked the Hausa rulers for their:
1. Religious Syncretism: Mixing Islam with pagan rituals.
2. Unjust Rule: Imposing oppressive taxes (like the jangali or cattle tax
on the Fulani), seizing property, and conscripting subjects.
3. Corruption: The judicial system was corrupt, and rulers lived in
luxury while the common people (talakawa) suffered.
4. Enslavement of Muslims: A practice forbidden by Islamic law.
o His message attracted a massive following, known as the jama'a (community).
This group included not just his fellow Fulani (especially the pastoralists who
felt marginalized) but also many disgruntled Hausa commoners.
3
1.3 The 1804 Jihad: Causes and Course
The "jihad" (which translates to "struggle," in this context a "holy war") was the armed
conflict that established the Caliphate.
 Immediate Trigger:
o The Sarkin Gobir, Yunfa (a former student of dan Fodio), grew fearful of
Usman's rising popularity and influence. He saw the jama'a as a "state within
a state."
o Yunfa attempted to assassinate Usman dan Fodio and began persecuting his
followers.
o In 1804, Usman dan Fodio and his followers fled from their home in Degel.
This migration is known as the Hijra (mirroring the Prophet Muhammad's
migration from Mecca to Medina).
o They settled at a place called Gudu. From here, Usman dan Fodio formally
declared a jihad against Yunfa and the other Hausa rulers, whom he now
labeled as kafir (unbelievers).
 Fundamental Causes of the Jihad:
1. Religious: The primary stated cause. To purify Islam, eradicate syncretic
practices, and establish a state governed by Shari'a (Islamic Law).
2. Political: The desire to overthrow the corrupt, oppressive, and "un-Islamic"
Hausa ruling dynasties. The jama'a wanted a just government.
3. Social/Economic: The deep-seated grievances of the common people (both
Hausa talakawa and Fulani pastoralists) against the ruling aristocracy. This
included high taxes, land appropriation, and social injustice. The Fulani, in
particular, resented the jangali (cattle tax).
 Course of the War:
o Usman dan Fodio was the spiritual leader (Amir al-Mu'minin - Commander of
the Faithful). He did not fight in battles himself.
4
o He gave "flags" (tuta) to his most trusted followers (flag-bearers), authorizing
them to carry the jihad to other Hausa states.
o His key commanders included his brother, Abdullahi of Gwandu, and his
son, Muhammadu Bello.
o The jihadists' forces, though often poorly armed, were highly motivated. They
successfully defeated the larger, but disunited, armies of the Hausa states.
o By 1808, Gobir had fallen. By 1810, Kano, Katsina, Zaria, and other states
were conquered. The old Hausa dynasties were overthrown and replaced by
Fulani Emirs.
1.4 Effects of the Jihad
(Note: This will be covered in greater detail in Week 3)
1. Political: The creation of a single, vast empire (the Sokoto Caliphate), which
replaced the fragmented Hausa states.
2. Religious: The widespread purification and expansion of Islam.
3. Social: The emergence of a new ruling class (the Fulani aristocracy) and the eventual
creation of a "Hausa-Fulani" identity.
4. Intellectual: A major boom in Islamic scholarship, with Sokoto and Gwandu
becoming centers of learning.

WEEK 2: THE ADMINISTRATION OF THE CALIPHATE
2.1 Introduction
The Sokoto CaliphATE, once established, was not just a loose collection of states. It was a
sophisticated, organized, and centrally-governed empire. At its peak, it was the largest and
most populous state in West Africa. Its administration was a theocracy, meaning the
government was based on religious law (Shari'a).
2.2 Political and Religious Organization
 The Caliph (Sultan of Sokoto):
o The supreme head of the Caliphate was the Amir al-Mu'minin (Commander of
the Faithful), also known as the Caliph or, later, the Sultan of Sokoto.
o Usman dan Fodio was the first Caliph. After his retirement, his son
Muhammadu Bello (who built the city of Sokoto as the capital) succeeded
him.
o The Caliph held both supreme political and religious authority. He was the
final court of appeal and was responsible for appointing Emirs.
7
 The Sokoto-Gwandu Duality:
o In 1812, even before the war was fully over, Usman dan Fodio retired from
active administration.
o He divided the Caliphate into two major administrative zones:
1. The Eastern Zone: Administered by his son, Muhammadu Bello,
from Sokoto. This included major emirates like Kano, Katsina, and
Zaria.
2. The Western Zone: Administered by his brother, Abdullahi, from
Gwandu. This included emirates like Nupe and Ilorin.
o This was not a split. The Emir of Gwandu still owed allegiance to the Caliph
in Sokoto, but he had significant autonomy in managing the western emirates.
 The Waziri (Vizier):
o The Caliph's chief administrator and advisor, similar to a Prime Minister.
o The Waziri of Sokoto was the most powerful official after the Caliph,
responsible for the day-to-day running of the empire, diplomacy, and
managing relations with the emirs.
2.3 The Emirate System
The Caliphate was composed of numerous emirates (the former Hausa states and newly
conquered territories). Each was ruled by an Emir.
 The Emir:
o He was the governor or ruler of his emirate.
o He was appointed by the Caliph in Sokoto (or the Emir of Gwandu for the
western zone). His appointment was symbolized by receiving a flag.
o Duties of the Emir:
1. Allegiance: He had to be loyal to the Caliph.
2. Tribute: He had to pay annual tribute (in the form of goods, slaves, or
currency) to Sokoto.
3. Military: He had to raise and lead an army for the Caliph's campaigns
when called upon.
8
4. Governance: He was responsible for maintaining law and order and,
most importantly, ruling according to Shari'a law.
o Autonomy: As long as the Emir fulfilled these duties, he had considerable
local autonomy to run his emirate's internal affairs.
 Key Officials within an Emirate: Each Emir replicated the central administration in
his own capital. Key officials included:
o Galadima: Head of the capital city administration.
o Madawaki (or Sarkin Yaki): Commander-in-chief of the emirate's army.
o Waziri: The Emir's own chief advisor.
o Sarkin Ruwa: Chief of fishing/rivers.
o Sarkin Pawa: Chief of butchers.
o Yari: Chief of the prisons.
2.4 Taxation
The Caliphate's financial system was based on Islamic law, replacing the arbitrary taxes of
the Hausa rulers.
1. Zakat: An obligatory religious alms tax on wealth and property, used to care for the
poor. It is one of the Five Pillars of Islam.
2. Jizya: A protection tax levied on non-Muslim subjects (dhimmi) living within the
Caliphate. In return, they were granted religious freedom and military protection.
3. Kharaj: A tax on land, particularly conquered land.
4. Jangali: The cattle tax, which had been a major grievance, was retained but was now
regularized and governed by Islamic law, making it less arbitrary.
5. Ghanima: Spoils of war (a share of which was sent to Sokoto).
2.5 Justice and Governance
 The entire legal system was based on Shari'a Law (derived from the Qur'an and
Hadith, following the Maliki school of jurisprudence).
 This replaced the Hausa rulers' mix of Islamic law and traditional customs.
9
 The system was administered by Alkali (judges).
 An Alkali was an educated scholar appointed by the Emir. He presided over a Shari'a
court.
 Cases were heard and judged strictly according to Islamic legal texts.
 The Emir's court was a higher court of appeal, and the Caliph's court in Sokoto was
the supreme court of the Caliphate.
 This system was seen as much fairer and less corrupt than the one it replaced.

2. Who was the first Caliph, and who succeeded him as the second Caliph?
3. Explain the administrative "duality" of Sokoto and Gwandu. Who led each part?
4. What were the three main duties an Emir owed to the Caliph in Sokoto?
5. Define the following terms: Alkali, Zakat, and Jizya.

WEEK 3: THE IMPACT OF THE JIHAD
3.1 Introduction
The Sokoto Jihad was one of the most significant events in West African history. Its
consequences were profound and are still evident today. It permanently reshaped the
political, religious, social, and educational landscape of Northern Nigeria and beyond.
3.2 Political Transformations
1. Creation of a Unified Empire: The jihad's most obvious impact was the destruction
of the 14 independent and warring Hausa states and their replacement with a single,
vast, and centralized empire—the Sokoto Caliphate.
2. New Ruling Aristocracy: The old Hausa dynasties (Habe rulers) were overthrown
and replaced by a new Fulani-led aristocracy. The "flag-bearers" of the jihad and
11
their descendants became the new Emirs, establishing new ruling dynasties that
continue to this day (e.g., in Kano, Zaria, Katsina).
3. End of Inter-State Warfare: The constant, petty wars between the Hausa states,
which had destabilized the region, were brought to an end. Peace and security (Pax
Islamica) were established over a vast area, stimulating trade and agriculture.
4. Inspiration for Other Jihads: The success of Usman dan Fodio's jihad inspired a
wave of similar reformist movements across West Africa in the 19th century. These
included the jihad of Seku Amadu in Masina, and the jihad of Al-Hajj Umar Tal in
the Tukulor Empire.
5. Defined Borders: The jihad and the Caliphate's expansion defined the political
geography of Northern Nigeria, establishing a major bloc that the future British
colonizers would have to confront (and later incorporate as "Northern Nigeria").
3.3 Religious Transformations
1. Purification and Spread of Islam: This was the primary goal of the jihad. Islam was
"purified" of syncretic and animist (Bori) practices. It spread to new areas and was
deepened in existing Muslim communities.
2. Supremacy of Shari'a Law: Islamic law (Shari'a) became the official legal code of
the entire empire, governing everything from criminal justice and commerce to
personal family matters. The Ulama (scholars) and Alkali (judges) became a powerful
and essential part of the state.
3. Mass Conversion: Many animist communities and "lapsed" Muslims converted to
Islam, either through conviction or through political pressure, swelling the ranks of
the faithful.
3.4 Social Transformations
1. Creation of a "Hausa-Fulani" Identity: Although the jihad was led by Fulani, they
were a minority in the region. Over time, the Fulani rulers intermarried with the
local Hausa population, adopted the Hausa language (which, ironically, became the
12
lingua franca of the Caliphate, not Fulfulde), and absorbed Hausa culture. This led to
the creation of a new, blended "Hausa-Fulani" identity.
2. Increase in Slavery: This is a major negative consequence. The jihad was a "war,"
and Islamic law at the time permitted the enslavement of those who resisted (deemed
kafir). This led to the capture of many non-Muslims and even "unbelieving" Muslims.
These slaves were used in households and on large slave-worked plantations (rinji),
which produced grain and other goods for the Caliphate's economy.
3. New Social Stratification: A new social hierarchy emerged:
o The Ruling Class: The Fulani Emirs, their families, and top officials.
o The Ulama: The respected class of Islamic scholars, judges, and teachers.
o The Talakawa: The free-born Hausa and Fulani commoners (farmers,
merchants, artisans).
o Slaves: The bottom of the social ladder.
3.5 Educational and Intellectual Transformations
1. Flourishing of Islamic Scholarship: The jihad was led by scholars, and the
Caliphate they built was a state that valued knowledge. Sokoto, Gwandu, Kano, and
Katsina became major centers of Islamic learning.
2. Increased Literacy: Arabic literacy (the language of the Qur'an and scholarship)
spread rapidly.
3. Massive Literary Output: The leaders of the jihad were prolific authors. Usman dan
Fodio, Muhammadu Bello, and Abdullahi of Gwandu wrote hundreds of books and
poems on theology, law, politics, history, and ethics. This intellectual legacy is one of
the richest in African history.
4. Promotion of Education: The state encouraged the establishment of Qur'anic
schools for both boys and girls. Usman dan Fodio himself was a strong advocate for
the education of women, including his own daughters (like Nana Asma'u).
Evaluation

4. What was a negative social consequence of the jihad?
5. Who were the three main intellectual figures of the Caliphate who wrote hundreds of
books?

WEEK 4: BORNU UNDER THE SHEHUS: THE EMERGENCE OF EL-KANEMI
4.1 Introduction: The Decline of the Sefawa Dynasty
While the jihad was transforming Hausaland, the neighbouring empire of Bornu was facing
its own crisis. Bornu was one of Africa's oldest and most enduring empires, ruled by the
Sefawa Dynasty for nearly 1,000 years (since the 9th century).
By the late 18th and early 19th centuries, the Sefawa dynasty was in a state of severe decline:
 Weak Rulers: The Mais (Kings) of Bornu had become decadent, weak, and isolated
in their palaces. They were figureheads with no real power.
 Corrupt Nobility: Real power was held by a corrupt and factious aristocracy that
often fought among itself.
15
 Economic Decline: The trans-Saharan trade routes, which had been Bornu's
lifeblood, were shifting, and the economy was weakening.
 Military Ineffectiveness: The once-mighty Bornu army was outdated and unable to
defend the empire's borders.
4.2 The Sokoto Jihad and the Attack on Bornu
The Sokoto jihadists, fresh from their victories in Hausaland, turned their attention to Bornu.
Fulani clerics within Bornu's borders rose up, accusing the Sefawa Mais of being
"unbelievers" and "syncretists," just as they had accused the Hausa Sarkis.
 The Fall of the Capital: In 1808, the jihadists (led by local Fulani leaders like
Gwoni Mukhtar) attacked Bornu. The Sefawa army collapsed. The Mai of Bornu,
Dunama IX, was forced to flee as the jihadists captured and sacked the ancient
capital of Ngazargamu.
4.3 The Rise of Muhammad al-Amin El-Kanemi
In this moment of total collapse, Mai Dunama appealed for help from a respected Islamic
scholar and warrior who lived in the province of Kanem. His name was Muhammad al-
Amin El-Kanemi (often shortened to El-Kanemi).
 Who was El-Kanemi?
o He was not from the Sefawa dynasty.
o He was of Kanembu and Shuwa Arab ancestry.
o He was a renowned scholar and military strategist who had a large following
of his own, especially among Kanembu and Shuwa Arab warriors.
 El-Kanemi's Counter-Jihad:
o El-Kanemi agreed to help the Mai... for a price (control of territory and
power).
o He assembled his army and launched a brilliant military campaign against the
Sokoto jihadists.
16
o He successfully recaptured Ngazargamu and drove the Fulani forces out of
Bornu's heartland.
 The Famous Intellectual Debate:
o El-Kanemi did not just fight with weapons; he also fought with ideas.
o He engaged in a famous series of letters with the Sokoto leaders (Usman dan
Fodio and Muhammadu Bello).
o Sokoto's Argument: The Mai of Bornu is an unbeliever, oppresses his people,
and allows paganism. Therefore, the jihad against him is just.
o El-Kanemi's Argument: Bornu is, and has been, an Islamic state for 800
years. Its rulers are Muslims. If they are "bad" Muslims, the solution is reform,
not a bloody invasion. He argued that the Sokoto jihad in Bornu was not for
religion but for political power and plunder.
4.4 His Reforms and Leadership: The Dual Monarchy
El-Kanemi's success made him the de facto (in reality) ruler of Bornu. The Sefawa Mais were
powerless and owed their throne to him.
 The Title "Shehu": El-Kanemi was too clever to depose the 1,000-year-old dynasty.
This might have caused a civil war. Instead, he allowed the Mai to continue existing
as a symbolic, figurehead ruler.
 El-Kanemi took the simple title of Shehu (from the Arabic Sheikh, meaning "leader"
or "scholar").
 A Dual Monarchy: This created a unique "dual monarchy" system:
1. The Mai (Sefawa): Lived in a separate palace, held the traditional court, and
was the de jure (by law) king. He had no real power.
2. The Shehu (El-Kanemi): Held the real military and administrative power. He
was the de facto ruler.
 A New Capital: The old capital, Ngazargamu, was destroyed. El-Kanemi built a
new capital for himself at Kukawa in 1814.
 Reforms:
17
o He began rebuilding the army, basing it on his loyal Kanembu and Shuwa
Arab followers, not the old, disloyal Sefawa nobility.
o He replaced the corrupt Sefawa nobles with his own trusted companions,
known as Kachellawa (military commanders).
o He sought to restore Bornu's economy and re-establish the supremacy of
Shari'a law.

WEEK 6: DEVELOPMENTS UNDER EL-KANEMI AND THE SHEHUS
6.1 Introduction
After emerging as the savior of Bornu, Muhammad El-Kanemi (who ruled c. 1814-1837)
and his successors consolidated their power, reorganized the state, and established a new
dynasty that would rule Bornu until the end of the 19th century.
6.2 The End of the Sefawa Dynasty
The "dual monarchy" (with a powerless Mai and a powerful Shehu) was an awkward
arrangement. It could not last forever.
 Muhammad El-Kanemi was succeeded by his son, Shehu Umar (who had a long
reign, 1837-1881).
 In 1846, the Sefawa puppet, Mai Ibrahim, conspired with the Sultan of Wadai (a
neighbouring kingdom) to overthrow Shehu Umar and regain Sefawa power.
20
 Shehu Umar decisively crushed this rebellion. He defeated both the Mai and the
forces of Wadai.
 After this act of treason, Shehu Umar executed Mai Ibrahim and his sons.
 With this act, the Sefawa dynasty was formally and permanently abolished after
ruling for nearly a millennium.
 Shehu Umar (and his father El-Kanemi) established the new Kanemi (or Shehu)
Dynasty as the sole rulers of Bornu, de jure and de facto.
6.3 Reorganization of Bornu Administration
El-Kanemi and Shehu Umar had to rebuild Bornu from scratch. They based the new
administration on El-Kanemi's loyal followers, not the old Sefawa nobility.
1. Central Government:
o The Shehu was the supreme political, military, and religious leader.
o He was assisted by a Council (Nokena) composed of his most trusted relatives
and companions (Kachellawa).
o The Waziri (Vizier) was the Shehu's chief administrator.
o The capital at Kukawa was built as a large, fortified city and became a major
center for trans-Saharan trade and Islamic learning.
2. Provincial Administration:
o The old Sefawa system of fief-holding (granting land to nobles) was abolished,
as it had made the nobles too powerful.
o Instead, El-Kanemi appointed officials called Koguna (district heads) who
were directly responsible to him.
o He also placed his loyal military commanders (Kachellawa) in charge of key
provinces to ensure security and loyalty.
6.4 Resistance to the Sokoto Caliphate
A key feature of El-Kanemi's and Shehu Umar's reigns was the successful resistance to the
Sokoto Caliphate.
21
 El-Kanemi's military victories and intellectual arguments had permanently halted the
Sokoto jihad at Bornu's western border.
 Bornu never became part of the Sokoto Caliphate. It remained a powerful,
independent, and rival Islamic empire throughout the 19th century.
 The border between Bornu and the Sokoto Caliphate (specifically the Hadejia and
Katagum emirates) remained a site of occasional raids and skirmishes, but neither
side could conquer the other.
6.5 Restoration of Stability
 Under El-Kanemi and Shehu Umar, Bornu regained much of its former strength.
 Internal stability was restored, and law and order (based on Shari'a) were reestablished.
 The trans-Saharan trade routes were revived. Kukawa became a major terminus,
trading with North Africa (Tripoli) in goods like slaves, ivory, ostrich feathers, and
leather goods.
 Bornu re-asserted its dominance over the Lake Chad region.
 This period represented a "silver age" for Bornu, restoring its position as a major
regional power.
Evaluation
1. Who was Shehu Umar, and what was his relationship to El-Kanemi?
2. How and in what year was the Sefawa dynasty finally abolished?
3. What was the new capital of Bornu under the Shehus?
4. Who were the Kachellawa and Koguna in El-Kanemi's administration?
5. What was Bornu's political relationship with the Sokoto Caliphate throughout the
19th century?

statement, comparing his actions to those of Usman dan Fodio.

WEEK 8: YORUBA LAND IN THE 19TH CENTURY: OYO EMPIRE
8.1 Introduction: The Old Oyo Empire at its Peak
Before the 19th century, the Oyo Empire was the dominant political and military power in
Yorubaland.
 It was a vast empire, with its capital at Oyo-Ile (Old Oyo).
 Its power rested on its formidable cavalry (horse-based army), which was effective in
the open savanna.
 It controlled a vast territory, including vassal states like the Egba, Egbado, and, most
importantly, the coastal kingdom of Dahomey, which gave it access to the Atlantic
slave trade.
8.2 The Political Structure of Old Oyo
25
The empire had a sophisticated system of "checks and balances":
1. The Alaafin: The emperor of Oyo. He was seen as Ekeji Orisa (companion of the gods)
and held supreme, semi-divine authority.
2. The Oyo Mesi: The Council of Seven, composed of the heads of the seven non-royal
wards of the capital.
o They were the kingmakers.
o Their leader was the Bashorun (Prime Minister).
o The Oyo Mesi had the constitutional power to "reject" an Alaafin by
presenting him with an empty calabash (igba iwa), a sentence that meant the
Alaafin had to commit ritual suicide. This was the ultimate check on his
power.
3. The Ogboni: A powerful, secret society of elders, priests, and notable figures. The
Ogboni cult served as a check on both the Alaafin and the Oyo Mesi, ensuring neither
became too powerful.
4. The Aare Ona Kakanfo: The Generalissimo, or Field Marshal, of the Oyo army. He
was the supreme military commander. By tradition, he was forbidden from living in
the capital, Oyo-Ile, to prevent him from using the army to stage a coup.
8.3 The Decline and Fall of the Oyo Empire
The 19th century witnessed the total and catastrophic collapse of this great empire. The
causes were both internal and external.
 Internal Conflicts:
1. Constitutional Breakdown: The delicate balance of power between the
Alaafin and the Bashorun broke down. In the late 18th century, Bashorun
Gaa became overmighty, reportedly deposing and executing four successive
Alaafins, creating deep instability.
2. Rebellion of Afonja (c. 1817): The Aare Ona Kakanfo at the time, Afonja, was
based in Ilorin, a major Oyo provincial town. He entered into a bitter
26
personal and political struggle with the reigning Alaafin Aole. Afonja decided
to rebel and break Ilorin away from the Oyo Empire.
 External Pressures (The Fulani Factor):
1. Afonja's Fatal Alliance: To win his rebellion, Afonja needed a strong army.
He invited a charismatic Fulani cleric, Alim al-Salih (known as Alimi), and
his jama'a (a mix of Fulani pastoralists, Hausa slaves, and other jihadist
followers) to settle in Ilorin and become his private army.
2. The Jama'a Takeover: This plan backfired disastrously. Afonja's new army
(the jama'a) grew more powerful than he was. They were inspired by the
Sokoto Jihad to the north. Soon, they turned on Afonja, killing him around
1824.
3. Ilorin Becomes an Emirate: Alimi's son, Abdulsalam, took control of Ilorin.
He declared himself Emir and pledged allegiance to the Sokoto Caliphate.
4. The Ilorin Jihad: Ilorin, once Oyo's key northern province, was now an
aggressive enemy emirate and a base for the Sokoto Caliphate to launch a
jihad south into the rest of Yorubaland.
 The Final Collapse:
1. The Ilorin cavalry, led by the Fulani, attacked the remnants of the Oyo
Empire.
2. In a series of battles (like the Battle of Ogele), the Oyo army was defeated.
3. Vassal states, like the Egba (who founded Abeokuta) and Dahomey, took the
opportunity to declare independence.
4. The capital, Oyo-Ile, was sacked and destroyed (c. 1835-1837).
5. The empire completely disintegrated. The Oyo people fled south, and the
Alaafin's son, Atiba, eventually founded a new, much smaller capital at Oyo-
Atiba (the present-day Oyo town).

WEEK 9: THE ERA OF IBADAN IMPERIALISM
9.1 The Power Vacuum and the Rise of New States
The collapse of the Oyo Empire (c. 1837) created a massive power vacuum and a period of
chaos. Refugees (son-of-a-bitch) fled south, and the old order vanished. This led to the rise of
new, powerful "successor states," which were often founded as war camps. The two most
important were Abeokuta (founded by Egba refugees) and Ibadan.
9.2 The Rise of Ibadan (c. 1829)
 A Military Republic: Ibadan was founded around 1829 by a mix of Ife, Ijebu, and
refugee Oyo warriors. It was not a traditional kingdom. It was a republican military
state.
29
 Government by Merit: Ibadan had no Oba (king) in the traditional sense. Its
leadership was based on military prowess and achievement, not heredity.
 The Two Chieftaincy Lines: Power was divided into two parallel lines of chiefs:
1. The Civil Line (Baale): This line was headed by the Baale (later titled
Olubadan). This was the senior-most chief, but he was not an absolute
monarch.
2. The Military Line (Balogun): This line was headed by the Balogun
(commander of the army).
 A man started at the bottom of a line and was promoted to a higher title based on his
success in war, his wealth, and his influence. This made Ibadan a meritocracy that
attracted ambitious and ruthless warriors from all over Yorubaland.
9.3 Ibadan Imperialism: The "Successor" to Oyo
Ibadan quickly became the most powerful military force in Yorubaland. Its leaders saw
themselves as the true successors to the old Oyo Empire. Their "imperial" goals were
twofold:
1. To Stop the Ilorin/Fulani Advance: To prevent the Sokoto Caliphate from
conquering all of Yorubaland.
2. To Control Trade Routes: To control the lucrative trade routes to the coast (Lagos
and Badagry) to import European firearms and gunpowder.
 The Battle of Osogbo (c. 1840): This was Ibadan's defining moment. The Ilorin-
Fulani army, having destroyed Oyo-Ile, was pushing deep into Yorubaland. They
laid siege to Osogbo. The Ibadan army, led by Balogun Oderinlo, marched north and
inflicted a crushing defeat on the Ilorin cavalry.
 Significance: This battle permanently halted the Fulani jihad's advance into the
Yoruba forest regions. Ibadan was hailed as the "savior of Yorubaland."
9.4 The Ajele System and the Kiriji War
After Osogbo, Ibadan began its own wars of expansion, creating a large empire.
30
 It conquered and dominated large parts of Yorubaland, especially the Ijesha, Ekiti,
Akoko, and Ife territories.
 The Ajele System: To rule this empire, Ibadan placed resident "governors" or agents,
called Ajele, in the subject towns.
 Oppression: The Ajele system quickly became corrupt and oppressive. The Ajeles
were often arrogant, brutal, and extorted taxes and property from the local people.
 The Ekiti-Parapo Coalition: The oppressive rule of the Ibadan Ajeles led to a
massive rebellion. The Ijesha and Ekiti kingdoms formed a powerful military alliance
called the Ekiti-Parapo ("Ekiti-together").
 They were led by a brilliant Ijesha warrior named Ogedengbe of Ilesa.
 The Kiriji War (c. 1877-1893):
o The Ekiti-Parapo rebellion sparked the longest and most destructive war in
Yoruba history, known as the Kiriji War (or 16-Years' War).
o It was Ibadan vs. the Ekiti-Parapo, who were soon joined by Ilorin, Ijebu, and
Ife, forming a "Grand Alliance" to crush Ibadan.
o The war gets its name, "Kiriji," from the sound "ki-ri-jiii" that the new, longrange
breach-loading rifles (bought from Europeans) made, which both sides
used for the first time.
o The war became a long, bloody stalemate in the hills of Ijeshaland,
weakening all of Yorubaland.

WEEK 10: THE PEACE TREATY OF 1886 AND ITS AFTERMATH
10.1 Introduction: The Stalemate
By the mid-1880s, the Kiriji War had been raging for nearly a decade. It had devolved into a
massive, entrenched stalemate.
 Ibadan: Still held the military advantage in open-field battles but could not break the
Ekiti-Parapo's defenses in the hills. Its army was exhausted.
 The Ekiti-Parapo: Were determined to win their independence but lacked the
offensive power to destroy Ibadan.
 Ijebu and Egba: Controlled the trade routes from the coast and blockaded Ibadan,
preventing them from getting fresh supplies of firearms.
 The entire region was suffering from famine, depopulation, and the collapse of all
normal trade and farming.
10.2 British Intervention
The British, who had established the Lagos Colony in 1861, were growing increasingly
concerned.
33
1. Trade: The war was disastrous for "legitimate trade" (in palm oil, etc.), which the
British wanted to promote.
2. Missionary Pressure: Christian missionaries (like Rev. Samuel Johnson, who was
also a Yoruba historian) were active in the interior and pleaded with the British
government in Lagos to intervene and stop the bloodshed.
3. Humanitarian Concerns: The Lagos government, under Governor Alfred Moloney,
saw the war as a humanitarian crisis.
In 1886, the Lagos government sent special commissioners (Higgins and Oliver) into the
interior to mediate a peace agreement.
10.3 The Peace Treaty of 1886
The warring factions, exhausted and under pressure from the British mediators, met at a
village called Igbajo to sign a peace treaty.
 Key Terms of the Treaty:
1. Cessation of Hostilities: An immediate end to the fighting.
2. Independence for the Ekiti-Parapo: This was the most important term.
Ibadan agreed to recognize the full independence of all the towns in the
Ekiti-Parapo confederation.
3. Abolition of the Ajele System: Ibadan was to withdraw all its Ajeles from
Ijesha and Ekiti lands.
4. Disbandment of Armies: All armies were to be disbanded, and the warriors
were to return to their hometowns.
5. Ilorin and Offa: The status of Offa (a town disputed by Ilorin and Ibadan)
was left ambiguous, which would cause problems later.
6. A New Era of Peace: The treaty was meant to end all wars in Yorubaland
and establish permanent peace.
10.4 The Aftermath
34
 A Fragile Peace: The treaty was signed, but the deep-seated hatred and mistrust
remained. The armies did not disband immediately, as no side trusted the other to
lay down its arms first. The war continued in a low-intensity form.
 The Rise of British Power: The most significant consequence of the 1886 Treaty was
not the peace it created, but the power it gave to the British. It showed that the
Yoruba states were now incapable of solving their own problems. The British, as
mediators, had become the new "power brokers" in the region.
 The "Scramble for Africa" and Final Conquest:
o The treaty was signed during the "Scramble for Africa." The British used the
continued instability as a pretext to impose their own rule.
o The French were advancing from the west (Dahomey), and the British were
determined to claim Yorubaland for themselves.
o In 1892, the British attacked the Ijebu (who were blocking trade) in the Ijebu-
Egba War.
o In 1893, the new Governor of Lagos, Sir Gilbert Carter, marched with troops
through all of Yorubaland (the "Carter's Trek"). He went to Abeokuta, Ibadan,
and the new Oyo, forcing their leaders to sign treaties that placed them under
"British Protection."
o This action in 1893, not the 1886 treaty, truly ended the Kiriji War and, with
it, the independence of all the Yoruba states. The colonial era had begun.

WEEK 1: BENIN IN THE 19TH CENTURY
1.1 INTRODUCTION: THE KINGDOM OF BENIN
By the 19th century, the Benin Kingdom was one of the oldest, most powerful, and most
organizationally complex states in West Africa. It had a long history of centralized power,
artistic achievement (especially its brass/bronze casting), and international diplomacy,
having established relations with Portugal as early as the 15th century. Its capital, Benin
City, was a vast, well-planned urban center protected by massive earthen walls.
1.2 STRUCTURE AND POLITICAL ORGANIZATION
The political structure of Benin was a highly centralized monarchy, with the Oba at the
apex of all power.
 THE OBA (KING):
37
o The Oba was the supreme political, religious, and military leader of the
kingdom.
o He was considered semi-divine and was the "owner" of all land in Benin.
o His authority was absolute, though in practice it was balanced by various
chieftaincy groups.
o He ruled from a vast palace complex in the center of the city.
 CHIEFTAINCY GROUPS: The Oba's administration was run by three main
categories of chiefs:
1. THE UZAMA NIHINRON (THE KINGMAKERS): This was the council of the
seven highest-ranking hereditary nobles (led by the Oliha). They were responsible for
crowning a new Oba and represented the most ancient lineages of the kingdom. They held
significant power and could act as a check on the Oba.
2. THE EGHAEVBO N'ORE (THE TOWN CHIEFS): This was a group of "palace
chiefs" who ran the day-to-day administration of the palace and state. They were nonhereditary,
meaning they were appointed by the Oba based on merit and service. This
allowed the Oba to create a loyal bureaucracy to counter the power of the hereditary Uzama.
3. THE EGHAEVBO N'OGBE (THE PALACE GUILDS): These were the chiefs who
managed the Oba's household and the various craft guilds (like brass casters, woodcarvers,
and ivory carvers). They were divided into three main palace associations:
 Iwebo: In charge of the Oba's regalia, wardrobe, and dealings with
Europeans.
 Iweguae: The Oba's personal servants and domestic staff.
 Ibiwe: Looked after the Oba's wives and children.
This complex system of hereditary chiefs, appointed chiefs, and palace guilds created a
sophisticated balance of power, all centered on the divine authority of the Oba.
1.3 TRADE AND DIPLOMACY
 TRADE:
38
o Benin's economy was built on trade, both internal and external (with its
neighbors and with Europeans at the coast).
o Initially (15th-18th centuries), the main exports were slaves, ivory, and pepper.
o By the 19th century, following the British abolition of the slave trade, the
main export had shifted to palm oil and palm kernels.
o The Oba's Monopoly: The Oba maintained a royal monopoly over all key
areas of the economy, especially the trade in palm oil and ivory. This meant
only he, or his designated agents, could legally sell these goods to Europeans.
This policy was a major source of conflict with British traders.
 DIPLOMACY:
o Benin had a long tradition of diplomacy. The Oba sent and received
ambassadors.
o Its earliest diplomatic ties were with Portugal.
o In the 19th century, its main diplomatic challenge was managing the growing
and increasingly aggressive power of Great Britain.
1.4 BRITISH RELATIONS
Relations between Benin and Britain deteriorated steadily throughout the 19th century.
1. Economic Conflict: British traders, led by firms like the Royal Niger Company,
wanted "free trade." They hated the Oba's monopoly, which kept prices high.
2. Missionary Pressure: Christian missionaries saw Benin as a "pagan" kingdom, citing
its practice of human sacrifice (an important part of its state religion) as a reason for
British intervention.
3. Political Pressure: The British, establishing control over the coast, saw Benin as a
powerful independent state standing in the way of their colonial ambitions.
4. The "Gallwey Treaty" (1892): Vice-Consul Harry Gallwey visited Benin and
pressured Oba Ovonramwen Nogbaisi to sign a treaty. The treaty, written in English,
promised "free trade" and placed Benin under British "protection." The Oba and his
chiefs later stated they never agreed to sign away their sovereignty and had no
intention of abandoning their trade monopoly.
39
This clash of interests (Benin's sovereignty vs. Britain's economic and imperial ambitions)
set the stage for the violent conquest that would follow.

WEEK 2: THE FIRST PHASE OF BRITISH CONQUEST (1851–1900)
2.1 INTRODUCTION
The second half of the 19th century was the period when Britain moved from being a
trading partner to an imperial conqueror. This "first phase" of conquest was not a
coordinated plan but a series of aggressive actions, often led by local "men on the spot"
(consuls, traders, and military officers), that focused on securing key coastal territories.
2.2 BRITISH INTERVENTION IN LAGOS (1851)
 THE CONTEXT: Lagos, a key coastal port, was plunged into a succession dispute
between two rivals:
o Oba Akitoye: The recognized Oba, who was more open to the British and the
abolition of the slave trade.
41
o Oba Kosoko: Akitoye's nephew and a powerful usurper, who was a strong
supporter of the slave trade.
 BRITISH MOTIVES:
1. Abolition: To stop the transatlantic slave trade, of which Lagos was a major hub.
2. Trade: To promote "legitimate" trade in palm oil.
3. Strategic: To secure a safe base of operations on the West African coast.
 THE BOMBARDMENT (1851): The British Consul, John Beecroft, sided with
Akitoye. When Kosoko refused to sign an anti-slavery treaty, a British naval
squadron attacked and bombarded Lagos. Kosoko was defeated and fled.
 THE AFTERMATH (1861): The British re-installed Akitoye (and later his son,
Dosunmu). However, in 1861, under the pretext of stopping the slave trade
permanently, the British forced Oba Dosunmu to sign the Treaty of Cession. This
treaty formally handed over Lagos to Britain, and it became a Crown Colony. This
was the first part of Nigeria to be formally annexed.
2.3 THE CONQUEST OF BENIN (1897)
 THE PRELUDE: Relations, already poor (as seen in Week 1), collapsed after the
Gallwey Treaty. Oba Ovonramwen continued to resist British interference.
 THE "PHILLIPS MASSACRE" (JANUARY 1897):
o The acting Consul-General of the Niger Coast Protectorate, James R.
Phillips, decided to force a meeting with the Oba to demand he abide by the
treaty.
o He was warned by the Oba not to come, as the sacred Igue festival was being
celebrated, a time when the Oba could not see foreigners.
o Phillips ignored the warnings and led an unarmed "trade mission" (which
included officials, translators, and 250 porters disguised as carriers) toward
Benin.
o The delegation was ambushed by Benin soldiers. Phillips and most of his
European officers were killed.
 THE "BENIN PUNITIVE EXPEDITION" (FEBRUARY 1897):
42
o The British response was immediate and overwhelming. They assembled a
massive military force of 1,200 Royal Marines and sailors.
o The expedition, led by Admiral Sir Harry Rawson, invaded Benin City.
o The city was captured, systematically looted of its priceless art (the Benin
Bronzes), and burned to the ground.
o Oba Ovonramwen was captured, put on trial, and exiled to Calabar, where he
died in 1914.
o The Benin Kingdom was broken, and its territory was absorbed into the Niger
Coast Protectorate.
2.4 ESTABLISHMENT OF PROTECTORATES
This phase of conquest was not just about annexing (like Lagos) but also about establishing
"protectorates" (territories protected and controlled by Britain, but not formally colonies).
1. OIL RIVERS PROTECTORATE (1885): After the Berlin Conference, Britain
declared a protectorate over the coastal areas from Calabar to the edge of Lagos.
This was to secure the palm oil trade from rival powers (like Germany and France).
2. NIGER COAST PROTECTORATE (1893): The Oil Rivers Protectorate was
expanded and given a more formal administration. This was the entity that
conquered Benin in 1897.
3. THE ROYAL NIGER COMPANY (RNC): Meanwhile, Sir George Taubman
Goldie's RNC had been given a royal charter in 1886 to administer the territories
along the Niger and Benue rivers, effectively creating a third British-controlled entity
in the region.
By 1900, Britain had three separate administrative territories in what would become Nigeria:
Lagos Colony, the Niger Coast Protectorate, and the RNC's territories.

WEEK 3: THE SECOND PHASE OF BRITISH CONQUEST (1900–1914)
3.1 INTRODUCTION
While the "first phase" secured the coast, the "second phase" was a systematic, deliberate
campaign to conquer the vast, powerful, and organized states of the interior, specifically the
Sokoto Caliphate. This phase was masterminded by one man: Lord Frederick Lugard.
3.2 EXPANSION OF COLONIAL RULE TO THE NORTH
 REVOCATION OF THE RNC CHARTER: In 1900, the British government
revoked the charter of the Royal Niger Company. The RNC was a business, not a
45
government, and it was ill-equipped to challenge the military power of the Sokoto
Caliphate.
 CREATION OF THE PROTECTORATE OF NORTHERN NIGERIA: On
January 1, 1900, Britain declared the Protectorate of Northern Nigeria. Lord Lugard
was appointed its first High Commissioner.
 THE WEST AFRICAN FRONTIER FORCE (WAFF): Lugard's instrument of
conquest was the WAFF, a well-armed, professional colonial army made up of
African soldiers (often from Hausa and Yoruba lands) led by British officers.
 THE CAMPAIGN OF CONQUEST: Lugard's "expansion" was a military
conquest, Emirate by Emirate.
1. Bida and Ilorin (1897, re-asserted 1901): The RNC had previously defeated
these emirates, but Lugard had to re-conquer them to establish firm British
authority.
2. Yola and Bauchi (1901-1902): These emirates were subdued.
3. Kano and Sokoto (1903): This was the main campaign.
 Kano: The city was a major commercial center with massive walls.
The WAFF used artillery to breach the walls and captured the city
after a short battle.
 Sokoto: The Caliph, Attahiru I, fled Sokoto as Lugard's forces
approached. A new, compliant Caliph was installed by Lugard.
4. THE BATTLE OF BURMI (1903): Caliph Attahiru I did not give up. He
raised a flag of jihad against the British invaders and rallied a large army. He
made his last stand at Burmi (in present-day Gombe State). He and hundreds
of his followers were killed in a fierce battle. This event marked the final,
decisive end of the Sokoto Caliphate's independence.
3.3 THE USE OF INDIRECT RULE
Lugard had conquered a vast empire with very few British officials and very little money. It
was impossible to rule it directly. This led him to devise the policy of Indirect Rule.
46
 DEFINITION: Indirect Rule was a system of colonial administration where the
British ruled through the existing, traditional political structures and rulers.
 WHY LUGARD USED IT IN THE NORTH:
1. Pragmatism: Lugard was severely understaffed and underfunded.
2. Pre-existing System: The Sokoto Caliphate already had a highly
sophisticated and centralized system of administration (Emirs, District Heads,
Alkali courts, taxation) that was ready-made for the British to co-opt.
3. Minimizing Resistance: It was easier to make the Emirs "partners" (even if
junior partners) than to remove them and risk constant rebellion.
 HOW IT WORKED:
o The Emir remained the head of his emirate. He kept his palace, his titles, and
his court.
o However, a British official, the "Resident," was posted in the emirate. The
Resident was the real power, advising the Emir. The Emir's orders were, in
fact, the Resident's orders.
o The Emir's administration (the "Native Authority") was allowed to collect
taxes, but a portion now went to the British.
o The Alkali (Shari'a) courts were allowed to function, but they were forbidden
from "inhuman" punishments (like mutilation).
3.4 CONSOLIDATION OF BRITISH AUTHORITY
By 1906, all of Northern Nigeria was under British control. In the same year, the Lagos
Colony and the Protectorate of Southern Nigeria (the old Niger Coast Protectorate) were
merged to form the new Colony and Protectorate of Southern Nigeria.
By 1914, Lugard had successfully conquered the North and consolidated the South. The
stage was set for his final act: merging the two.

WEEK 4: EARLY PHASE OF BRITISH RULE (1900–1914)
4.1 INTRODUCTION
This period covers the time after the main conquests but before the 1914 Amalgamation.
During this time, the British had to figure out how to govern the vast and diverse territories
they had seized. This led to the development of different administrative systems in the
North and South.
4.2 ADMINISTRATION: THE THREE (THEN TWO) NIGERIAS
Between 1900 and 1906, British Nigeria was three separate entities:
1. The Lagos Colony
2. The Protectorate of Southern Nigeria
3. The Protectorate of Northern Nigeria
49
In 1906, Lagos and the Southern Protectorate were merged, simplifying this to two large
blocs:
1. The Colony and Protectorate of Southern Nigeria (Governor: Walter Egerton)
2. The ProtectorATE OF Northern Nigeria (High Commissioner: Lord Lugard)
These two territories were run completely separately, with different governors, policies, legal
systems, and budgets.
4.3 POLICIES AND NATIVE AUTHORITIES
 IN THE NORTH (LUGARD):
o Policy: Indirect Rule, as discussed last week.
o Native Authorities (NAs): The administration of the Emirs was formalized
into the "Native Authority." The NA was responsible for local government,
police, courts, and tax collection. The British Resident simply supervised.
This system was very successful on its own terms because it was cheap, efficient,
and maintained order.
o Policy on Missionaries: Lugard forbade Christian missionaries from operating
in the Muslim Emirates, fearing their presence would anger the Emirs and
destabilize the indirect rule system. This had a monumental long-term effect
on Western education in the North.
 IN THE SOUTH (EGERTON):
o The West (Yorubaland): The British tried to apply "indirect rule" using the
Obas (like the Alaafin of Oyo and Ooni of Ife). This was moderately
successful, as the Yoruba had a tradition of centralized monarchies.
o The East (Igboland and environs): Indirect rule was a complete failure. Igbo
society was "acephalous," meaning it had no single king or central chief. It
was governed by councils of elders, age-grades, and title-holders in
autonomous villages.
o The "Warrant Chief" System: Since there were no chiefs to rule through, the
British created them. They issued "warrants" to local men who seemed
50
influential, making them "Warrant Chiefs" and giving them power they never
had traditionally. This policy was deeply resented, and the Warrant Chiefs
were often seen as corrupt and illegitimate puppets.
4.4 TAXATION
Taxation was the primary reason for colonial rule: a colony had to pay for its own
administration.
 IN THE NORTH: This was easy for Lugard. The Sokoto Caliphate already had a
sophisticated tax system (zakat, jangali, jizya). Lugard simply "regularized" this
system, taking a share for the British government.
 IN THE SOUTH: This was extremely difficult and unpopular. Most societies in the
South did not have a tradition of direct taxation. The British imposition of direct
taxes (like the "Hut Tax") was met with intense resistance and evasion. It was this
attempt to tax, especially women, that would later lead to the Aba Women's Riots
of 1929.
This period (1900-1914) entrenched a deep division in Nigeria: a Northern system based on
traditional emirs and Islamic law, and a Southern system based on a mix of co-opted Obas,
artificial Warrant Chiefs, and direct British rule, with a heavy Christian missionary (and
thus, educational) presence.

WEEK 6: THE AMALGAMATION OF NIGERIA (1914) AND ITS SIGNIFICANCE
6.1 INTRODUCTION
On January 1, 1914, the British formally merged the Protectorate of Northern Nigeria and
the Colony and Protectorate of Southern Nigeria into a single entity called the Colony and
Protectorate of Nigeria. This singular event is the origin of the modern Nigerian state. The
architect of this policy was Lord Frederick Lugard, who returned to Nigeria and became its
first Governor-General.
6.2 REASONS FOR AMALGAMATION
The merging of these two vast, different territories was done for British convenience, not for
the benefit of the people living there. The reasons were primarily economic and
administrative.
54
1. THE ECONOMIC IMPERATIVE (THE PRIMARY REASON):
o The North was Broke: The Protectorate of Northern Nigeria was a massive
financial failure. It was landlocked, had high administrative costs (due to its
size and military presence), and its main exports (groundnuts) were not yet
profitable enough to pay its bills. It was surviving on an annual grant-in-aid (a
bailout) from the British Treasury.
o The South was Rich: The Colony and Protectorate of Southern Nigeria was
a huge financial success. Its coastal ports (Lagos, Calabar) generated massive
revenue from customs duties on imported goods (like alcohol and tobacco).
Its palm oil and cocoa exports were highly profitable.
o The "Marriage": The British Treasury in London was tired of bailing out the
North. The simplest solution was to "amalgamate" the two budgets, using the
South's surplus to pay for the North's deficit.
2. ADMINISTRATIVE CONVENIENCE:
o It was illogical and inefficient to run two separate administrations in
neighboring territories.
o They had different policies, different judicial systems, and even different
railway gauges.
o A single, unified administration would be easier and cheaper to manage from
the Colonial Office in London.
3. INFRASTRUCTURE COORDINATION:
o A single, unified railway policy was needed. The British wanted to build a
railway line from the coast (Lagos) to the deep interior (Kano) to evacuate
cash crops (like groundnuts and cotton) and move troops. This required a
single, unified government to oversee.
6.3 LORD LUGARD’S POLICY
Lugard's vision for the amalgamated Nigeria was to extend his "Indirect Rule" system,
which he perfected in the North, over the entire country.
55
 He created a central government in Lagos, but he divided the country into two
"Lieutenant-Governorships" (North and South).
 He made little effort to unify the people. The North and South were still governed as
separate entities.
 He tried to force the "Warrant Chief" system (an imitation of the Northern model)
onto the East, and tried to strengthen the Obas in the West, with mixed results.
6.4 SIGNIFICANCE: ADVANTAGES AND DISADVANTAGES
 ADVANTAGES (FROM THE BRITISH PERSPECTIVE):
1. Solved the Budget Crisis: The South's revenue now paid for the North's
administration.
2. Administrative Efficiency: Created a single, large, and viable colonial
territory.
3. Economic Integration: Allowed for the creation of a national infrastructure
(especially railways) to maximize resource extraction.
 DISADVANTAGES (FROM THE NIGERIAN PERSPECTIVE):
1. A "FORCED MARRIAGE": The amalgamation was done with zero
consultation. Over 250 distinct ethnic and political groups were forced into a
single state.
2. SOWED SEEDS OF DIVISION: Rather than encouraging unity, the British
policy of governing the North and South differently entrenched their
differences. This created the North-South political divide that has defined
Nigerian history.
3. REGIONAL IMBALANCES: The policy preserved the feudal, conservative
structure of the North while the South was rapidly changing due to Western
education and missionary activity. This created a deep social, political, and
educational imbalance between the regions.
4. A MARRIAGE OF "UNEQUALS": The amalgamation is often described
as the "mistake of 1914," a marriage of "incompatible partners" that laid the
groundwork for future political instability, competition, and conflict.
56

WEEK 8: COLONIAL RULE AND ITS IMPACT
8.1 INTRODUCTION
Colonial rule was a total system. It was not just a political takeover; it was a fundamental reengineering
of Nigeria's economy, society, and culture to serve the interests of Great Britain.
This week examines the key pillars of the colonial system and their impact.
8.2 CENTRAL ADMINISTRATION
After 1914, Nigeria was governed by a single Governor-General (later Governor) based in
Lagos. He was a virtual dictator, responsible only to the Colonial Secretary in London.
 THE NIGERIAN COUNCIL (LUGARD'S ERA): Lugard created a council in
1914. It was a "talking shop" with no real power. It consisted of 36 members (mostly
British officials and a few "traditional" rulers) who met once a year to listen to the
Governor's speech.
59
 THE CLIFFORD CONSTITUTION (1922): This was Nigeria's first real
constitution, introduced by Governor Sir Hugh Clifford.
o It created a new Legislative Council (LEGCO).
o Significance: For the first time in British West African history, this council
included elected members.
o It allowed for 4 elected members: 3 from Lagos and 1 from Calabar.
o This provision, though small, kick-started the development of political parties
(like Herbert Macaulay's NNDP) and modern nationalist politics.
 THE EXECUTIVE COUNCIL: This was the Governor's "cabinet." It was
composed entirely of British heads of departments (finance, military, health, etc.)
and had the real power to run the country.
8.3 ECONOMIC EXPLOITATION
The raison d'être (reason for being) of the colony was profit. The colonial economy was a
classic "extractive" economy.
 A "DEPENDENT ECONOMY": Nigeria was forced into a dependent relationship
with Britain.
1. EXPORT: Nigeria's role was to produce and export raw materials (palm oil,
cocoa, cotton, tin, groundnuts) needed by British factories.
2. IMPORT: Nigeria was then forced to import expensive, finished manufactured
goods (like textiles, bicycles, and soap) from Britain.
 SUPPRESSION OF LOCAL INDUSTRIES: This system actively destroyed local
industries. For example, Nigerian textile weavers could not compete with cheap,
mass-produced cloth from Manchester. Local gin production was banned to create a
market for British gin.
 THE ROLE OF OLIGOPOLIES: The entire import-export trade was monopolized
by a few giant British firms, most notably the United African Company (UAC),
which was the successor to the Royal Niger Company. These firms fixed low prices
for Nigerian farmers' crops and high prices for imported goods, draining wealth from
the country.
60
8.4 INTRODUCTION OF WESTERN EDUCATION AND CHRISTIANITY
These two forces were the most powerful agents of social change, and they were almost
inseparable.
 LED BY MISSIONARIES: Colonial policy in the South (especially) left education
almost entirely in the hands of Christian missionary societies:
o CMS (Anglican)
o Roman Catholics (Holy Ghost Fathers)
o Methodists, Baptists, and Church of Scotland
 THEIR GOALS: The primary goal of the missionaries was evangelization
(spreading Christianity). They built schools as their main tool for conversion.
 IMPACT IN THE SOUTH (YORUBALAND AND IGBOLAND):
o There was a massive, enthusiastic adoption of both Christianity and Western
education.
o It created a new, educated elite who became clerks, teachers, lawyers, doctors,
and (most importantly) nationalists.
o It also undermined traditional culture, religion, and the authority of elders.
 IMPACT IN THE NORTH (HAUSA-FULANI EMIRATES):
o As part of the "Indirect Rule" bargain, Lugard banned Christian missionaries
from the Muslim emirates.
o The British government itself provided very little education (a few "secular"
schools, like Katsina College, were set up to train the sons of the aristocracy
to be "Native Authority" administrators).
o Result: A vast educational gap was created between the North and the South,
which became a major source of political imbalance and suspicion.

WEEK 9: THE COLONIAL ECONOMY
9.1 INTRODUCTION
This week is a deeper dive into the specific mechanisms of the colonial economy discussed
in Week 8. The goal was simple: extract the maximum amount of raw materials at the
minimum cost and create a captive market for British goods.
9.2 CASH CROP PRODUCTION
This was the engine of the colonial economy. The British system encouraged (and often
forced) farmers to shift from growing food for themselves (subsistence farming) to growing
crops for export.
 REGIONAL SPECIALIZATION:
63
o THE WEST: Became the Cocoa Belt. Farmers in Yorubaland, responding to
high prices, adopted cocoa cultivation on a massive scale.
o THE EAST: Became the Palm Oil Belt. Palm oil and palm kernels, which
grew wild, were collected and processed for export.
o THE NORTH: Became the Groundnut and Cotton Belt. The railway to
Kano (completed in 1911) made it possible to export groundnuts, leading to
the famous "Groundnut Pyramids." Cotton was also pushed as a raw material
for British textile mills.
 IMPACT:
o Pros: It brought many Nigerians into the cash economy and created some
wealth, especially for cocoa farmers and northern merchants.
o Cons: It made the Nigerian economy a monoculture economy, vulnerable to
price swings on the world market. It also reduced the amount of land and
labor dedicated to food production, sometimes leading to local food shortages.
9.3 TAXATION
Taxation was the primary tool used to force Nigerians into the cash economy.
 THE SYSTEM: The British imposed taxes (like the "Hut Tax" or "Poll Tax") that
had to be paid in British currency (pounds, shillings, and pence).
 THE GOAL: To get this currency, a farmer could no longer just grow his own yams.
He had to:
1. Grow a cash crop (cocoa, groundnuts) and sell it to the UAC.
2. Leave his village to work as a laborer on the railways, in the tin mines (Jos),
or as a clerk for the government.
 Taxation was, therefore, a tool of social engineering, designed to create a mobile,
cash-dependent labor force for the colonial system.
9.4 INFRASTRUCTURE (RAILWAYS, PORTS, ROADS)
64
The British built significant infrastructure, which is often cited as a "benefit" of colonialism.
However, the design of this infrastructure reveals its true purpose.
 PURPOSE: The infrastructure was built only to facilitate the extractive economy.
 THE RAILWAY LINES: The main railway lines did not connect major Nigerian
population centers to each other. They ran from the resource-producing interior to the
coastal ports.
o The Western Line ran from Lagos to Kano (to get groundnuts and cotton).
o The Eastern Line ran from Port Harcourt (a new port built by the British) to
Enugu (to get coal) and later to Jos (to get tin).
 THE PORTS: Lagos (Apapa) and Port Harcourt were developed as major deepwater
ports, not for Nigerian use, but for European ships to load raw materials and
unload manufactured goods.
 THE ROADS: A network of "feeder roads" was built to connect the farms and
mines to the railway stations.
9.5 TRADE
 DOMINATED BY FOREIGN FIRMS: As mentioned in Week 8, the entire
import-export trade was monopolized by a few British firms (UAC, John Holt, G.B.
Ollivant).
 THE MARKETING BOARDS: Later in the colonial period, the British created
"Marketing Boards" (e.g., the Cocoa Marketing Board, the Groundnut Marketing
Board).
o Stated Purpose: To stabilize prices for farmers.
o Real Purpose: To fix a very low price for farmers' crops, sell them on the
world market for a high price, and keep the difference (the "surplus") in British
banks. This was a form of systematic exploitation, draining billions from the
Nigerian economy.

WEEK 10: SOCIAL DEVELOPMENT AND INTER-GROUP RELATIONS
10.1 INTRODUCTION
Colonialism was not just a political and economic project; it was a social one. It completely
re-ordered Nigerian society, creating new cities, new social classes, and, for the first time, a
sense of a common "Nigerian" identity.
10.2 URBANIZATION
Colonial rule led to the explosive growth of new cities, which were the administrative and
commercial hubs of the new economy.
 TRADITIONAL CITIES: Pre-colonial cities like Ibadan, Kano, and Benin
continued to be important.
 NEW "COLONIAL" CITIES: These cities were created by the British for specific
purposes:
o Lagos: Grew from a small port into the sprawling, multi-ethnic capital of the
entire country.
67
o Port Harcourt: A port city built from scratch in 1912 to export coal from
Enugu.
o Enugu: A city created in 1909 after coal was discovered there.
o Kaduna: A "neutral" city built by Lugard in 1912 to be the new capital of the
North, away from the traditional power centers of Sokoto or Kano.
o Jos: Grew as the center of the tin mining industry.
 IMPACT: These new cities became "melting pots" where, for the first time, large
numbers of Igbo, Yoruba, Hausa, Efik, and other groups lived and worked together.
This mixing was essential for the development of a national identity.
10.3 MISSIONARY EDUCATION AND THE NEW ELITE
 (Recap from Week 8): Missionary education was the key to social mobility.
 THE NEW SOCIAL CLASS: The product of this education was a new social class
that did not exist in traditional society: the Western-educated elite.
 WHO THEY WERE: This elite was made up of clerks, teachers, lawyers, doctors,
journalists, and railway workers. They spoke English, wore European clothes, lived
in the new urban centers, and were often Christian.
 A "MIDDLE-MAN" CLASS: They were suspended between two worlds: they
were rejected by the British as "social equals" (kept out of the European clubs and
senior government posts) but were also now different from their own people in the
villages.
 THEIR ROLE: This new elite was the only group with the language (English) and
the intellectual tools (Western political ideas of "liberty" and "self-determination") to
challenge the British. The colonial education system, by accident, created the very
nationalists who would destroy it.
10.4 NATIONAL CONSCIOUSNESS
 THE IRONY OF COLONIALISM: Colonialism, by forcing over 250 groups into a
single country called "Nigeria," accidentally created Nigerian nationalism.
 FACTORS THAT FORGED NATIONAL CONSCIOUSNESS:
68
1. A COMMON NAME: The name "Nigeria" (coined by Flora Shaw) gave all
groups a new, shared label.
2. A COMMON ENEMY: All groups, regardless of their differences, shared a
common experience of foreign, racist, and exploitative rule. This "common
enemy" (the British) was a powerful unifying force.
3. A COMMON LANGUAGE: English became the lingua franca, allowing a
lawyer from Lagos (Yoruba) to communicate with a merchant from Onitsha
(Igbo) and a teacher from Calabar (Efik).
4. SHARED SPACES: The new cities, schools, and railways allowed people to
mix and see themselves as part of something bigger than just their own ethnic
group.
 THE BIRTH OF NATIONALISM: This new "national consciousness" first found
its voice in the new elite. Men like Herbert Macaulay (the "father of Nigerian
nationalism") began to demand not just better treatment, but political rights and,
eventually, independence.

SS2 NIGERIAN HISTORY
THIRD TERM LESSON NOTES
WEEK 1: ORIGINS OF NATIONALISM IN NIGERIA
1.1 INTRODUCTION: WHAT IS NATIONALISM?
NATIONALISM is the feeling of political consciousness or awareness among a group of
people, who share a common territory and identity, that leads them to assert their right to
self-government, free from foreign control.
In the Nigerian context, it was the movement and struggle led by an educated African elite
to overthrow British colonial rule and establish a new, independent Nigerian nation. This
movement began slowly with simple demands for better treatment and inclusion, and grew
into an unstoppable demand for full independence.
71
1.2 CAUSES OF NATIONALISM IN NIGERIA
There was no single cause for nationalism. It was a complex reaction to the colonial
experience itself.
1. COLONIAL POLICIES (THE "COMMON ENEMY"):
 Political Exclusion: Nigerians were completely excluded from any real position of
power. The Governor and his Executive Council (all British) held dictatorial power.
The Legislative Council was merely a "talking shop." This exclusion angered the new
educated elite who felt qualified to rule.
 Economic Exploitation: The colonial economy was designed to exploit Nigeria.
o The Marketing Boards paid Nigerian farmers low, fixed prices for their cash
crops (cocoa, palm oil, groundnuts) and sold them on the world market for a
huge profit, which was kept in Britain.
o Foreign Firms, especially the United African Company (UAC), monopolized
the import-export trade, stifling Nigerian businesses.
o Direct Taxation (like the poll tax) was imposed on people, forcing them into
the cash economy.
 Social Segregation and Racism: Colonial society was deeply racist.
o Europeans lived in "European Quarters" (e.g., Ikoyi in Lagos, the GRA -
Government Reserved Area - in other cities) with separate hospitals, clubs,
and schools.
o Nigerians, no matter how educated or wealthy, were treated as inferior. A
Nigerian lawyer or doctor was still considered "junior" to the lowest British
colonial official.
 This shared experience of being ruled by a common, racist, and exploitative foreign
power (Britain) was the single most powerful factor in uniting diverse groups as
"Nigerians."
2. WESTERN EDUCATION:
72
 The "Accidental" Tool: The colonialists (especially missionaries) introduced
Western education to produce clerks, interpreters, and teachers for the colonial
administration.
 Ideas of Freedom: However, this education exposed Nigerians to Western political
ideas of "Liberty," "Democracy," "Self-Determination," and "Nationalism."
Nigerians read about the American and French Revolutions and began to ask, "Why
can't we rule ourselves?"
 A New Elite: Education created a new, confident class of lawyers, doctors,
journalists, and teachers (the "nationalist elite") who had the intellectual tools to
challenge colonialism.
 A Common Language: English became the lingua franca, allowing the elite from
different ethnic groups (Yoruba, Igbo, Efik, etc.) to communicate and organize a
national movement.
3. THE PRESS:
 The educated elite founded newspapers that became the mouthpiece of nationalism.
 These newspapers exposed colonial injustices, criticized government policies, and
spread nationalist ideas to the literate population in the growing cities.
 Key Newspapers:
o The Lagos Weekly Record (John Payne Jackson)
o West African Pilot (founded by Nnamdi Azikiwe in 1937), which had the
famous motto: "Show the light, and the people will find the way."
4. RELIGION (THE "ETHIOPIAN" MOVEMENT):
 Many Nigerian Christians in European-led churches (Anglican, Methodist, Catholic)
grew frustrated with the racism and discrimination they faced. European
missionaries dominated the leadership and were often as condescending as colonial
officials.
 This led to a "schism," where Nigerians broke away to form their own "African
Independent Churches."
73
 This was a form of "religious nationalism"—an early demand for "self-government"
in the church. It showed that Africans were capable of managing their own affairs
without European supervision.

WEEK 2: NATIONALIST MOVEMENTS AFTER WORLD WAR II
2.1 THE IMPACT OF WORLD WAR II
World War II (1939–1945) was the single greatest catalyst for the acceleration of
nationalism.
1. Demobilized Soldiers ("Ex-Servicemen"): Thousands of Nigerian soldiers fought
for Britain in Burma, India, and East Africa.
o They saw European soldiers (British, French) as equals, not "gods."
o They fought for "freedom" and "democracy," and when they returned, they
demanded those same rights for themselves.
o They became the militant, organized backbone of the nationalist movement.
75
2. The Atlantic Charter (1941): This was a joint declaration by US President Roosevelt
and British Prime Minister Churchill. It stated that all peoples had the "right to selfdetermination"
(the right to choose their own form of government).
o Although Churchill later claimed this only applied to European nations under
Nazi rule, Nigerian nationalists seized on it and used it as a moral and legal
argument against colonialism.
3. Weakening of Britain: The war exhausted Britain, leaving it financially broke and
militarily weak. It no longer had the money or the will to maintain a vast, unwilling
empire.
2.2 POLITICAL ORGANIZATIONS (THE "BIG THREE" PARTIES)
The post-WWII era saw the decline of early nationalist "movements" (like the West African
Students' Union) and the rise of mass political parties. These parties, however, became
increasingly regional, a fact that would define Nigeria's political future.
1. NCNC (NATIONAL COUNCIL OF NIGERIA AND THE CAMEROONS):
 Founded: 1944.
 Founders: Herbert Macaulay (first President) and Nnamdi Azikiwe (first Secretary-
General, who took over as leader after Macaulay's death in 1946).
 Initial Goal: To be a pan-Nigerian, national party to fight for independence. It was
the first "truly" national party.
 Base of Support: Its initial base was broad, but as regionalism grew, it became
increasingly associated with the Igbo people and the Eastern Region.
 Key Policy: A strong, unified Nigeria; immediate independence.
2. AG (ACTION GROUP):
 Founded: 1951.
 Founder: Chief Obafemi Awolowo.
 Origin: The AG was formed from a Yoruba cultural organization called the Egbe
Omo Oduduwa ("Society of the Descendants of Oduduwa").
76
 Base of Support: Overwhelmingly based in the Yoruba-dominated Western Region.
 Key Policy: Independence, but under a federal system ("federalism"). The AG
believed that a federal structure, where each region had autonomy, was the only way
to protect the unique cultures and interests of Nigeria's diverse ethnic groups.
3. NPC (NORTHERN PEOPLE'S CONGRESS):
 Founded: 1951.
 Founder: Sir Ahmadu Bello, the Sardauna of Sokoto (Party Leader), with Sir
Abubakar Tafawa Balewa as his deputy (and the party's leader in Lagos).
 Origin: It began as a Northern cultural organization, Jam'iyyar Mutanen Arewa.
 Base of Support: Exclusively based in the Hausa-Fulani-dominated Northern
Region.
 Key Policy: Its primary goal was not independence. Its motto was "One North, One
People." The NPC's priority was to protect the Northern traditional, political, and
religious systems (the emirates) from being dominated by the more educated South.
They feared that if independence came too quickly ("Independence in 1956"),
Southerners would take over the civil service.
4. OTHERS (NEPU):
 NORTHERN ELEMENTS PROGRESSIVE UNION (NEPU): Founded by
Aminu Kano.
 Goal: A radical, populist party in the North. It was opposed to the NPC, which it
saw as a party of the feudal, conservative aristocracy (the Emirs). NEPU represented
the talakawa (commoners) of the North.

WEEK 3: THE ROAD TO INDEPENDENCE (CONSTITUTIONAL
DEVELOPMENTS)
3.1 INTRODUCTION
The story of Nigeria's journey to independence is a story of constitutional development.
Each new constitution was a "test" by the British and a reaction to nationalist demands.
Each one conceded more power to Nigerians until full independence was achieved.
3.2 THE CLIFFORD CONSTITUTION (1922)
 Governor: Sir Hugh Clifford.
 Key Feature (The Elective Principle): This was the first time in Nigerian history that
any form of election was allowed. It created a Legislative Council (LEGCO) that
included 4 elected members (3 from Lagos, 1 from Calabar).
 Major Flaw:
79
1. Divisive: The LEGCO only made laws for the South. The Governor
continued to rule the North by proclamation (like a dictator). This legally
separated the North and South.
2. Powerless: The council was dominated by British officials. The elected
members were a tiny minority who could be (and were) ignored.
 Significance: Its "elective principle" kick-started modern politics and led to the
formation of the first political party, Herbert Macaulay's Nigerian National
Democratic Party (NNDP).
3.3 THE RICHARDS CONSTITUTION (1946)
 Governor: Sir Arthur Richards.
 Context: A response to post-WWII agitation and the growing influence of the
NCNC.
 Key Features:
1. Unity (on paper): It created a single Legislative Council for the entire country
(North and South) for the first time.
2. Regionalism (in practice): This was its most important feature. It formally
divided Nigeria into Three Regions (North, West, and East) and created a
"House of Assembly" for each.
 Major Flaw (Imposition): The constitution was created with zero consultation from
Nigerians. It was simply imposed by the Governor.
 Significance:
1. It infuriated the nationalists. The NCNC, led by Azikiwe, launched a pan-
Nigeria tour to protest it and even sent a delegation to London.
2. By formally creating the "three-region" structure, it entrenched regionalism and
ethnicity as the basis of Nigerian politics.
3.4 THE MACPHERSON CONSTITUTION (1951)
 Governor: Sir John Macpherson.
80
 Context: A direct response to the failure and protests against the Richards
Constitution.
 Key Features (Consultation): This was Nigeria's first "home-grown" constitution. A
massive, nation-wide consultation was held, from the village level up to a national
conference.
 Political Features:
1. Stronger Regions: It created much more powerful Regional Assemblies with
true legislative powers.
2. Central Legislature: A central "House of Representatives" was created with
members elected from the Regional Houses.
3. Ministers: For the first time, Nigerians were appointed as Ministers in the
central government, creating a "Council of Ministers."
 Major Flaw (Died on Arrival): The constitution was a compromise that satisfied no
one. It was unclear if Nigeria was a unitary or federal state. It led to a major crisis in
1953.
o The 1953 Motion Crisis: An Action Group member, Anthony Enahoro,
moved a motion for "self-government in 1956." The Northern leaders (NPC)
felt this was too soon and were booed by the Lagos crowd. This led to the
Kano Riots of 1953 and a breakdown of trust. The North threatened to
secede.
3.5 THE LYTTLETON CONSTITUTION (1954)
 Governor: Sir Oliver Lyttleton.
 Context: An emergency constitution to fix the 1953 crisis and save Nigeria from
breaking apart.
 Key Features (True Federalism): This is the constitution that defined modern
Nigeria.
1. A True Federation: It officially established Nigeria as a Federation of three
autonomous regions.
2. Division of Powers: It created clear lists of powers:
81
 Exclusive List: Powers for the Federal (central) government only (e.g.,
army, currency, foreign policy).
 Concurrent List: Powers shared by Federal and Regional governments
(e.g., health, education).
 Residual List: Powers for the Regions only.
3. Regional Autonomy: The Regions became fully autonomous. For the first
time, leaders of the majority parties in the regions were named "Premiers"
(Ahmadu Bello in the North, Awolowo in the West, Azikiwe in the East).

WEEK 4: THE ATTAINMENT OF INDEPENDENCE (1960)
4.1 NATIONALIST LEADERS
By the late 1950s, the struggle for independence was dominated by the leaders of the "Big
Three" regional parties. These were the "Founding Fathers" of Nigeria.
1. SIR AHMADU BELLO (NPC):
o The Sardauna of Sokoto. A descendant of Usman dan Fodio.
o Leader of the NPC and Premier of the Northern Region.
o He was the most powerful politician in Nigeria but chose to stay in the North,
sending his deputy to be Prime Minister in Lagos.
2. CHIEF OBAFEMI AWOLOWO (AG):
o Leader of the Action Group and Premier of the Western Region.
o A brilliant intellectual and administrator, known for his "federalist" policies
and the UPE (Universal Primary Education) scheme.
3. DR. NNAMDI AZIKIWE (NCNC):
83
o Leader of the NCNC and Premier of the Eastern Region.
o Known as "Zik," he was a charismatic journalist and the first truly "national"
figure of the independence movement.
4. SIR ABUBAKAR TAFAWA BALEWA (NPC):
o The Deputy Leader of the NPC.
o He was the NPC's parliamentary leader in Lagos and became Nigeria's first
(and only) Prime Minister.
4.2 FINAL EVENTS LEADING TO INDEPENDENCE
1. Constitutional Conferences (1957 & 1958):
o Nigerian leaders (Awo, Zik, Bello) met with the British in London to
negotiate the final steps.
o 1957: The Eastern and Western Regions were granted full internal selfgovernment.
o 1958: The Northern Region was granted internal self-government.
o The Willink Commission (1958): The British set up a commission to address
the deep-seated fears of the ethnic minorities (e.g., the Tiv in the North, the
Efik/Ibibio in the East, the Urhobo/Edo in the West) who were afraid of
being dominated by the "Big Three" (Hausa-Fulani, Igbo, Yoruba). The
Commission found these fears were genuine but decided not to create new
states, believing other "human rights" clauses would protect them. This was a
fatal error.
o Independence Date: An official independence date was set for October 1,
1960.
2. The 1959 Federal Election:
o This was the election to decide which party would rule the newly independent
Nigeria.
o Results:
 NPC (North): Won the most seats (134), but not an outright majority.
 NCNC (East): Won 89 seats.

 AG (West): Won 73 seats.
o The Coalition: No party could rule alone. The NPC formed a coalition
government with the NCNC.
o The New Government (1960):
 Prime Minister: Sir Abubakar Tafawa Balewa (NPC)
 Governor-General: Dr. Nnamdi Azikiwe (NCNC) (representing the
Queen)
 Leader of the Opposition: Chief Obafemi Awolowo (AG)
4.3 INDEPENDENCE DAY (OCTOBER 1, 1960)
On this day, the Union Jack (British flag) was lowered, and the new Nigerian flag was
raised at Tafawa Balewa Square in Lagos. After nearly 100 years of British presence and 60
years of direct colonial rule, Nigeria became a sovereign, independent state (a "Dominion"
within the British Commonwealth).
4.4 IMMEDIATE CHALLENGES OF SELF-GOVERNMENT
Independence was celebrated with great joy, but the new nation faced enormous, unsolved
problems from day one.
1. DEEP ETHNIC AND REGIONAL DIVISION: Loyalty was to the Region (and
ethnic group), not to the "Nation." The parties were regional, not national.
2. THE MINORITY PROBLEM: The fears of the minorities had not been solved.
This would lead to unrest (e.g., the Tiv Riots).
3. EDUCATIONAL IMBALANCE: The South (especially the West) was decades
ahead of the North in Western education. The North feared the "Southern
domination" of the federal civil service.
4. WEAK NATIONAL IDENTITY: The "unity" that existed was largely a product of
the shared fight against the British. With the common enemy gone, the deep cracks
in the foundation of Nigeria became immediately apparent.

WEEK 6: THE FIRST REPUBLIC (1960–1966)
6.1 INTRODUCTION
The First Republic refers to the period from October 1, 1960, to January 15, 1966. It began
with Nigeria as a "Dominion" (with the Queen of England as Head of State) and became a
full Republic on October 1, 1963. This meant the Governor-General (Dr. Azikiwe) became
the President (a ceremonial role). The Prime Minister (Tafawa Balewa) still held the real
political power.
This period was marked by high hopes, but it was dominated by political instability, bitter
regionalism, and a series of crises that ultimately destroyed the republic.
6.2 POLITICAL PARTIES AND REGIONALISM
The political structure was the same as at independence:
 Federal Government: A shaky coalition of the NPC (North) and the NCNC (East).
88
 Opposition: The AG (West).
The primary problem was that the NPC, due to the North's massive population, had a nearpermanent
hold on federal power. The other parties, particularly the AG, felt locked out.
6.3 MAJOR CRISES OF THE FIRST REPUBLIC
The First Republic was destroyed by five (5) major, interlocking crises.
1. THE ACTION GROUP (AG) CRISIS (1962):
 A "personality clash" and power struggle erupted within the AG, the ruling party in
the West.
 The split was between Chief Obafemi Awolowo (Party Leader) and Chief S.L.
Akintola (Premier of the West).
 The conflict turned violent, with supporters of both men fighting on the floor of the
Western House of Assembly.
 Federal Intervention: The NPC-NCNC federal government saw its chance to
destroy its main rival. It declared a "State of Emergency" in the West, dissolved the
regional government, and appointed a Federal Administrator to rule the region.
2. THE TREASONABLE FELONY TRIAL (1963):
 Following the AG crisis, Chief Awolowo and several of his key allies were arrested.
 They were charged with "treasonable felony"—plotting to overthrow the federal
government by force.
 After a highly controversial trial, Awolowo was found guilty and sentenced to 10
years in prison.
 Impact: This was seen by many Yorubas as a political witch-hunt by the North
(NPC) to jail the main leader of the opposition. It was a massive blow to national
unity.
3. THE CENSUS CRISIS (1962–1963):
89
 In Nigeria, population is power (it determines how many seats each region gets in
parliament).
 The 1962 census was held. The results, which were leaked, showed that the South
(East + West) had more people than the North.
 This was politically explosive, as it would mean the North would lose its majority in
parliament.
 The NPC-led government immediately rejected the census results. A "recount" was
held in 1963, which, unsurprisingly, reversed the figures and gave the North a large,
permanent majority.
 Impact: The census was blatantly rigged. It destroyed any remaining trust between
the regions. The NCNC (Igbo leaders) felt betrayed by their NPC coalition partners.
4. THE FEDERAL ELECTION CRISIS (1964):
 The parties realigned into two "grand alliances" for the election:
o NNA (Nigerian National Alliance): The NPC (North) + Akintola's new
party (NNDP, West).
o UPGA (United Progressive Grand Alliance): The NCNC (East) + the
remnants of the AG (West).
 The election was a farce. It was marked by boycotts, rigging, and violence. The NNA
was declared the winner, leading to a constitutional stalemate until Azikiwe
(President) and Balewa (PM) agreed to a fragile "broad-based" government.
5. THE "WILD, WILD WEST" ELECTION (1965):
 This was the final nail in the coffin. A regional election was held in the Western
Region.
 Akintola's NNDP (allied with the North) was deeply unpopular. The AG (UPGA)
was expected to win easily.
 The NNDP massively and openly rigged the election to "win."
 Impact: The people of the West rebelled. There was a complete breakdown of law
and order. Political opponents were murdered, houses were burned ("Operation
90
Wetie"). The federal government, led by Balewa, failed to act. It was this chaos that
directly led the military to intervene.

8.1 INTRODUCTION
Despite the political chaos, the First Republic was a period of impressive social and
economic progress. The three Regions were in fierce competition with each other, leading to
a "golden age" of development. This period was funded primarily by agriculture, not oil.
Here's an image depicting the period of development and regional competition in Nigeria's First
Republic:
8.2 THE ECONOMY
 AGRICULTURAL BASE: The economy was still based on the cash crops
established by the British. The Regional Marketing Boards (now run by Nigerians)
were the main source of government revenue.
o North: Groundnuts, Cotton
o West: Cocoa
93
o East: Palm Oil
 EARLY OIL INDUSTRY: Oil had been discovered in commercial quantities at
Oloibiri (in the Eastern Region) in 1956. By the 1960s, oil was becoming a major
export, but it was not yet the dominant source of income.
 NATIONAL DEVELOPMENT PLAN (1962–1968):
o This was the first major national plan to guide the economy.
o It focused on "Import Substitution Industrialization" (building Nigerian
factories to produce goods that were normally imported, like textiles, cement,

 The Kainji Dam (for nationwide electricity)
 The Niger Bridge (connecting Lagos/West to the East)
 New ports and roads.
8.3 EDUCATION
This was the "golden age" of Nigerian education, driven by regional competition.
 WESTERN REGION: Chief Awolowo's Universal Primary Education (UPE)
program, launched in 1955, was revolutionary. It made primary education free for all
children in the region, leading to a massive boom in literacy.
 EASTERN AND NORTHERN REGIONS: They scrambled to catch up, pouring
money into building new primary and secondary schools.
 UNIVERSITY BOOM:
o Before 1960, Nigeria had only one university (University College, Ibadan).

universities:
 University of Nigeria, Nsukka (UNN): Founded by the Eastern
Region (Azikiwe).
 Ahmadu Bello University (ABU), Zaria: Founded by the Northern
Region (Ahmadu Bello).

 University of Ife (now OAU): Founded by the Western Region
(Awolowo).
 The Federal Government also created the University of Lagos
(UNILAG).
8.4 SOCIAL WELFARE
 INFRASTRUCTURE: The regions competed to build symbols of "modernity."
o Television: The Western Region (WNTV) started the first television station
in all of Africa in 1959.
o Stadiums: Liberty Stadium (Ibadan), Ahmadu Bello Stadium (Kaduna),
Nnamdi Azikiwe Stadium (Enugu).
o Hospitals: New general hospitals were built in many towns.
 NATIONAL PRIDE: This era saw a new sense of national pride, symbolized by the
creation of a new currency (the Nigerian Pound), a new national anthem, a flag, and
a national airline (Nigeria Airways).

WEEK 9: MILITARY INTERVENTION AND THE 1966 COUP
9.1 CAUSES OF THE JANUARY 1966 COUP
By the end of 1965, the First Republic was in a state of total collapse. The military
intervened for the following reasons:
1. "WILD, WILD WEST": The complete breakdown of law and order in the Western
Region, which the Balewa government was unable or unwilling to stop.
2. BLATANT CORRUPTION: Politicians were seen as extremely corrupt, enriching
themselves at the public's expense.
3. RIGGED ELECTIONS: The 1964 and 1965 elections were so badly rigged that
they destroyed all faith in democracy.
4. POLITICIZATION OF THE ARMY: Politicians had begun to "promote" officers
from their own ethnic group, destroying the army's unity and discipline.
5. FEAR OF IMPLOSION: The "five majors" who led the coup believed the country
was about to disintegrate and that they were acting as "patriots" to save it.
9.2 EVENTS OF THE JANUARY 15, 1966 COUP
96
 LEADERS: A group of young, idealistic, and mostly Igbo officers known as "the
five majors." The most prominent was Major Kaduna Nzeogwu.
 GOAL: To overthrow the "corrupt" politicians, end regionalism, and "cleanse" the
nation.
 EXECUTIONS: The coup was bloody. The plotters assassinated:
o Sir Ahmadu Bello (Premier of the North)
o Sir Abubakar Tafawa Balewa (Prime Minister)
o Chief S.L. Akintola (Premier of the West)
o Many high-ranking Northern military officers.
 FAILURE: The coup was successful in the North but failed in Lagos. The plotters
did not capture the General Officer Commanding (GOC) the army.
 HANDOVER OF POWER: The surviving federal ministers, in a state of panic,
handed power not to the coup plotters, but to the GOC of the Army, Major-General
J.T.U. Aguiyi-Ironsi (an Igbo), who then became Nigeria's first military Head of
State.
9.3 CONSEQUENCES (IRONS'S REGIME AND THE COUNTER-COUP)
1. AN "IGBO COUP": The coup was immediately seen as an "Igbo coup."
o Why? The key plotters were Igbo. The politicians and officers killed were
almost all from the North or West. The two Igbo premiers (in the East and
Mid-West) were spared.
2. IRONSI'S MISTAKE (UNIFICATION DECREE):
o Ironsi's regime, trying to "fix" regionalism, issued Decree No. 34 in May 1966.
o This decree abolished the federal system and declared Nigeria a "unitary
state."
o To the North, this was the final proof that the "Igbo coup" was a plot to allow
the more-educated Igbos to dominate the entire country.
3. THE COUNTER-COUP (JULY 29, 1966):
o Northern military officers (led by Lt. Col. Murtala Mohammed) staged a
violent "revenge coup."
97
o Head of State Aguiyi-Ironsi and the Governor of the West were assassinated.
o This coup led to the appointment of a compromise candidate as the new Head
of State: Lt. Col. Yakubu Gowon (a Northerner, but a Christian from a
minority ethnic group).
9.4 THE NIGERIAN CIVIL WAR (1967–1970)
 PRELUDE (POGROMS): The July counter-coup was followed by massacres
(pogroms) of Igbo civilians living in the North. Hundreds of thousands of Igbos fled
back to their "homeland" in the East.
 THE ABURI ACCORD (JANUARY 1967): Gowon and the military governor of
the East, Lt. Col. Chukwuemeka O. Ojukwu, met in Aburi, Ghana. They agreed to
a "confederal" system (a very weak center), but on returning to Nigeria, they disputed
the details.
 GOWON'S 12-STATE DECREE (MAY 1967): In a political masterstroke, Gowon
broke Nigeria's 4 Regions into 12 States.
o Purpose: To break the power of the major regions and, crucially, to "liberate"
the non-Igbo minorities (who lived in the oil-rich areas) from the Eastern
Region.
 SECESSION (MAY 30, 1967): Ojukwu saw this as the final straw. He declared the
Eastern Region an independent nation called the REPUBLIC OF BIAFRA.
 THE WAR: The Federal Government refused to accept the secession, and war broke
out in July 1967. The "police action" turned into a brutal, 30-month (2.5-year) war,
marked by a massive federal blockade that led to widespread starvation inside Biafra.
 THE END: Biafra surrendered in January 1970. Ojukwu fled into exile.

WEEK 10: POST-CIVIL WAR NIGERIA
10.1 INTRODUCTION
The Civil War ended in January 1970 with Biafra's surrender. The conflict had devastated
the East, killed an estimated 1-2 million people, and left the nation deeply scarred. The
immediate post-war period, under the leadership of Yakubu Gowon, was focused on
healing.
10.2 GOWON'S "3 RS" POLICY
Gowon declared a policy of "No Victor, No Vanquished" to show that the goal was
reconciliation, not punishment. This policy was built on "3 Rs":
1. RECONSTRUCTION:
o This involved the physical rebuilding of the war-torn areas (mostly in the
East).
100
o This meant rebuilding bridges (like the Niger Bridge), roads, public buildings,
and markets that had been destroyed.
2. REHABILITATION:
o This focused on the human side of the war.
o It involved caring for refugees, displaced persons, and demobilizing soldiers.
o It also involved efforts to rehabilitate the economy of the East, for example,
by giving a small ex-gratia payment (20 Nigerian pounds) to Igbos to restart
their lives, regardless of what they had in the bank before the war.
3. REINTEGRATION:
o This was the political and social goal of bringing the Igbo people back into the
Nigerian "family."
o Igbo civil servants were re-absorbed into the federal workforce.
o The "Abandoned Property" Issue: This was a major challenge. Property left
behind by Igbos who fled other parts of the country (especially Port Harcourt)
had been seized. A controversial policy was set up to return some of these
properties, though many were never recovered.
o The Indigenization Decree (1972): This policy (which forced foreigners to
sell their businesses to Nigerians) was partly timed to help all Nigerians,
including Igbo businessmen, to get a fresh start and buy into the national
economy.
10.3 THE ROLE OF THE "OIL BOOM"
All of the "3 Rs" would have been impossible without money. Nigeria was extremely lucky.
In the early 1970s, particularly after the 1973 Yom Kippur War, world oil prices
skyrocketed.
 Nigeria was suddenly flooded with billions of dollars ("petrodollars").
 This "oil boom" paid for the entire 3 Rs program. It funded the rebuilding of the East
and massive new national projects.
 Gowon famously said that Nigeria's problem was "not money, but how to spend it."
101
10.4 OTHER UNITY EFFORTS
Gowon's government launched several other programs to deliberately foster a new sense of
national unity and prevent another war.
1. NATIONAL YOUTH SERVICE CORPS (NYSC) (1973):
o This was the most famous unity scheme.
o It made it mandatory for all university graduates to serve for one year in a
state other than their state of origin.
o Goal: To encourage cross-cultural understanding by making a young Yoruba
live and work in Sokoto, an Igbo live in Borno, a Hausa live in Calabar, etc.
2. NEW INFRASTRUCTURE:
o The government embarked on building new, unifying infrastructure, such as
Federal Highways, to physically link the 12 states.
3. CHANGE TO RIGHT-HAND DRIVE (1972):
o Nigeria (a former British colony) used to drive on the left. In 1972, the
country was shut down for a weekend to switch to driving on the right.
o Goal: A symbolic break with the colonial past and a practical move to align
with Nigeria's French-speaking neighbors (Benin, Niger, Cameroon).
4. NATIONAL SPORTS FESTIVAL:
o An "Olympic-style" event created to bring young athletes from all 12 states
together in friendly competition.
