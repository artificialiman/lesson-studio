WEEK 1: INTRODUCTION TO CROP PRODUCTION
Introduction
Crop production is one of the oldest and most important activities of humankind. It refers to the
growing of crops for food, raw materials, and other useful products that support human life.
From ancient times, people cultivated the land to produce grains, vegetables, and fruits to meet
their daily needs. Today, crop production has become a modern and scientific process that
supports not only individuals but also the entire national economy.
In this lesson, we will learn the meaning of crop production, its importance to national
development, and the different ways it contributes to food security, employment, and industrial
growth.
2
Meaning of Crop Production
Crop production is the branch of agriculture that deals with the cultivation of crops for food,
fibre, and industrial use. It includes all the processes involved in growing plants — such as land
preparation, planting, weeding, fertilizer application, pest control, harvesting, and storage.
Crops are plants grown mainly for consumption or commercial purposes. Examples include:
 Food crops: maize, cassava, rice, yam, beans, millet.
 Cash crops: cocoa, cotton, oil palm, groundnut, rubber.
 Vegetable crops: tomato, okra, pepper, cabbage, lettuce.
Crop production ensures the steady supply of food and raw materials needed by people and
industries all over the world.
3
Importance of Crop Production to the National Economy
1. Source of Food
Crop production provides food for both humans and animals. Staple crops such as maize,
yam, rice, and cassava supply carbohydrates, while legumes like beans and groundnut
provide proteins needed for body growth and repair.
2. Source of Raw Materials for Industries
Many industries depend on crops for their raw materials. For example, cocoa is used in
chocolate production, cotton in textile industries, sugarcane for sugar and ethanol, and oil
palm for soap and cosmetics.
3. Employment Opportunities
Crop production provides jobs for farmers, labourers, traders, transporters, and factory
workers in agro-allied industries. It reduces unemployment and promotes economic
stability.
4. Source of Income and Foreign Exchange
Farmers earn money by selling their crops locally or exporting them to other countries.
Exportation of crops like cocoa, groundnut, and palm oil brings foreign exchange that
helps to develop the nation.
5. Contribution to National Development
Agriculture contributes to the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and helps in building the
nation’s economy. The revenue earned is used to provide public services such as roads,
schools, and hospitals.
6. Encouragement of Rural Development
The presence of farms encourages the building of rural infrastructure such as roads,
markets, and electricity. This improves the living standards of people in rural areas.
7. Promotion of Trade and Commerce
Crop production supports both local and international trade. Farmers sell their produce in
4
markets, while exporters trade crops across countries, promoting business and wealth
creation.
8. Environmental Benefits
Crops play a major role in protecting the environment. They prevent soil erosion, enrich
the soil with organic matter, and release oxygen during photosynthesis.
Careers in Crop Production
There are several career opportunities in the field of crop production, such as:
 Agronomist
5
 Horticulturist
 Soil Scientist
 Farm Manager
 Agricultural Extension Officer
 Crop Researcher
These professionals work together to improve crop yields, manage farmland, and ensure
sustainable agricultural practices.
Summary
Crop production is the foundation of agriculture and the main source of food, raw materials, and
employment in most developing countries like Nigeria. It has helped in reducing hunger,
supporting industries, and improving the standard of living for many people. Without crop
production, human survival and national development would be impossible.

WEEK 2: FARM TOOLS AND SAFETY MEASURES
Introduction
Every successful farmer needs the right set of tools to work efficiently on the farm. Farm tools
are simple implements or instruments used by farmers to carry out different operations such as
clearing, planting, weeding, and harvesting. These tools make farm work faster, easier, and more
productive.
However, the use of farm tools also comes with certain risks. Farmers must therefore learn how
to use them correctly, maintain them properly, and observe necessary safety precautions to avoid
injuries and accidents. This lesson will explain the types of farm tools, their uses, how to
maintain them, and important safety measures to follow while working on the farm.
Meaning of Farm Tools
Farm tools are simple hand-held implements used by farmers to perform various agricultural
tasks. They are usually manually operated and made of strong materials like metal, wood, or
8
plastic. Some are traditional tools used by local farmers, while others are modern versions
designed for improved efficiency.
Farm tools can be grouped according to their functions, such as:
 Cutting tools – e.g., cutlass, knife, sickle.
 Digging tools – e.g., hoe, spade, shovel.
 Lifting and carrying tools – e.g., wheelbarrow, head pan.
 Measuring tools – e.g., tape, measuring cylinder, weighing scale.

Maintenance of Farm Tools
Proper maintenance ensures that tools last long, work effectively, and remain safe for use. The
following are common ways of maintaining farm tools:
1. Clean after use: Wash tools to remove dirt, mud, and plant materials.
2. Dry properly: Always dry metal tools after washing to prevent rust.
3. Sharpen edges regularly: Tools like hoes, cutlasses, and sickles should be sharpened to
make them more effective.
4. Oil metal parts: Apply light oil or grease to prevent rusting.
5. Store properly: Keep tools in a clean, dry place or hang them on racks.
6. Repair damaged tools: Replace broken handles and tighten loose bolts immediately.
7. Avoid misuse: Use each tool for its correct purpose to prevent breakage and accidents.

Safety Precautions on the Farm
Farm safety means protecting yourself and others from injuries or harm while working. Safety
rules should always be observed when using farm tools and machinery.
Safety Rules and Precautions:
1. Always wear protective clothing such as gloves, boots, hats, and overalls.
2. Handle sharp tools carefully and always point them downwards when walking.
3. Do not play or run around with tools.
4. Keep tools away from children or untrained persons.
11
5. Use tools only for their intended purpose.
6. Avoid using damaged or blunt tools.
7. Do not leave tools carelessly on the ground — store them properly after use.
8. Be cautious when working under hot sun or during rainfall.
9. Report injuries or accidents immediately for first aid treatment.
First Aid and Emergency Procedures
Accidents may occur on the farm despite all precautions. Knowing basic first aid is important
for quick response and safety.
Meaning of First Aid:
First aid is the immediate care or treatment given to a person who is injured or suddenly taken ill
before professional medical help arrives.
Common Farm Accidents:
 Cuts and wounds from sharp tools
 Burns from fire or chemicals
 Insect bites or stings
 Falls or slips on wet ground
Basic First Aid Steps:
1. Stop any bleeding by applying pressure with a clean cloth or bandage.
2. Clean minor wounds with antiseptic solution.
3. Bandage the wound properly to prevent infection.
4. For serious injuries, burns, or snake bites, call for help and take the victim to a nearby
clinic or hospital.

5. Always have a first aid box on the farm containing items like bandages, cotton wool,
antiseptics, scissors, plasters, and gloves.

Farm tools are essential for efficient agricultural work. They help farmers to clear land, plant
crops, weed, and harvest easily. Proper use and maintenance of tools increase their lifespan and
improve farm productivity. Observing safety precautions and knowing first aid procedures help
prevent and manage farm accidents effectively. A careful and well-prepared farmer is a

WEEK 3: FARMLAND PREPARATION PRACTICES
Introduction
Before planting any crop, the farmer must prepare the land properly. Farmland preparation is the
process of making the soil ready for planting crops. It involves a series of activities such as
clearing, ploughing, harrowing, and ridging. Each stage helps to improve soil structure, remove
unwanted materials, and create a suitable environment for healthy crop growth.
Proper land preparation is the foundation of successful crop production. It ensures that crops
receive enough air, water, and nutrients and that weeds and pests are minimized. In this lesson,
you will learn the importance of farmland preparation, the different stages involved, the tools
used at each stage, and how to layout a farm correctly.
14
Meaning of Farmland Preparation
Farmland preparation refers to all the activities carried out on a piece of land before planting
crops. It is aimed at improving soil conditions and making the land suitable for cultivation. These
activities include clearing vegetation, breaking and loosening the soil, removing stumps and
stones, and forming ridges or beds for planting.
Importance of Farmland Preparation
1. Weed Control: Clearing and tilling the soil help to remove weeds that compete with
crops for nutrients, water, and sunlight.
2. Soil Aeration: Tilling breaks up compact soil, allowing air and water to penetrate easily.
3. Improved Root Growth: Loose soil makes it easier for plant roots to grow deep and
spread widely.
4. Ease of Planting: Well-prepared land makes planting faster and more accurate.
5. Pest and Disease Control: Turning the soil exposes pests and disease-causing organisms
to sunlight and predators.
6. Better Water Infiltration and Drainage: Well-prepared soil absorbs and drains water
properly, preventing waterlogging or erosion.
7. Enhanced Soil Fertility: Mixing organic matter and fertilizers during preparation
enriches the soil with nutrients.
8. Neat and Organized Farm Layout: Land preparation ensures the farm is well-arranged
for efficient crop management and harvesting.
Stages of Farmland Preparation
15
1. Clearing
o This is the first stage in land preparation. It involves removing unwanted
vegetation such as grasses, shrubs, trees, and debris from the land.
o Methods of Clearing:
 Manual clearing: using cutlass, hoe, or axe.
 Mechanical clearing: using bulldozers or tractors for large areas.
 Chemical clearing: using herbicides to kill weeds.
o Tools used: Cutlass, hoe, axe, and rake.
2. Ploughing
o This involves turning and loosening the topsoil using tools or machines. It helps
to bury weeds and mix organic matter with the soil.
o Methods: Manual ploughing with hoe, or mechanical ploughing with tractor and
plough.
o Tools used: Hoe, spade, or plough (animal or tractor-drawn).
3. Harrowing
o After ploughing, the soil may still contain large lumps or clods. Harrowing breaks
these lumps and makes the soil fine and smooth for planting.
o Tools used: Harrow (animal- or tractor-drawn).
16
4. Ridging or Bed Formation
o Ridges, mounds, or beds are formed depending on the type of crop. For example:
 Ridges – for yam, maize, and cassava.
 Mounds – for cocoyam or sweet potatoes.
 Flat beds – for vegetables and rice.
o Importance of ridging:
 Prevents waterlogging.
 Makes weeding and harvesting easier.
 Enhances root development and soil drainage.
o Tools used: Hoe, shovel, or ridger.
5. Farm Layout and Land Demarcation
o After preparing the soil, the land is divided into sections (plots) for easy
management.
o Farm layout means arranging the different parts of the farm such as plots, roads,
buildings, and irrigation lines in an orderly way.
o Land demarcation involves marking out the plots with pegs, ropes, or sticks to
show boundaries and crop spacing.
o Importance:
 Promotes good organization of farm activities.
 Helps in record-keeping and monitoring.
 Prevents overcrowding of crops and reduces wastage of space.

1. Avoid working on waterlogged or overly dry soil.

Farmland preparation is the first and most important stage of crop production. It involves
clearing the land, tilling the soil, and arranging it into suitable structures like ridges or beds for

WEEK 4: SOIL AND ITS PROPERTIES
Introduction
Soil is one of the most important natural resources on earth and the foundation of all agricultural
activities. Without soil, crop production would not be possible because plants depend on soil for
support, water, and nutrients. Understanding the nature and properties of soil helps farmers
choose the right crops to grow and adopt proper management practices to improve soil fertility.
In this lesson, you will learn the meaning and types of soil, the importance of soil in crop
production, the physical properties of soil, and how soil characteristics affect the growth of crops.
Meaning of Soil
Soil is the top layer of the earth’s surface that supports the growth of plants. It is formed from the
weathering (breaking down) of rocks and the decay of organic matter such as leaves, dead plants,
and animals. Soil contains minerals, water, air, organic matter, and living organisms.
20
Soil can be described as a mixture of:
1. Mineral particles (from rocks)
2. Organic matter (from decayed plants and animals)
3. Water
4. Air
5. Living organisms such as earthworms, insects, and microorganisms
Types of Soil
Soil is generally classified into three main types based on the size of particles and texture:
21
1. Sandy Soil
o Made up of large particles and feels rough or gritty when touched.
o Does not hold water well because it has large spaces between particles.
o It warms up quickly and is easy to work with.
o Common crops grown: groundnut, watermelon, and cassava.
2. Clay Soil
o Made up of very small particles that stick together when wet.
o It holds water for a long time and becomes sticky during the rainy season.
o It is heavy and difficult to till.
o Common crops grown: rice and sugarcane.
3. Loamy Soil
o A mixture of sand, clay, and organic matter.
o It is the best soil for farming because it holds water and nutrients well but still
allows air to enter.
o It is dark in colour, fertile, and easy to cultivate.
22
o Common crops grown: maize, yam, and vegetables.
Importance of Soil to Crop Production
1. Anchorage: Soil holds the roots of plants firmly, preventing them from falling over.
2. Nutrient Supply: It provides essential minerals like nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium
needed for plant growth.
3. Water Supply: Soil stores water that plants absorb through their roots.
4. Air Supply: Pores in the soil contain oxygen and carbon dioxide needed for plant
respiration.
5. Habitat for Organisms: Soil provides a home for beneficial organisms like earthworms,
bacteria, and fungi that improve soil fertility.
6. Support for Human Activities: Soil supports buildings, roads, and farm structures.
7. Filtration of Water: It helps purify rainwater as it passes through layers underground.
Physical Properties of Soil
The physical properties of soil determine how suitable it is for crop growth. The major physical
properties include:
1. Soil Colour
o Colour gives information about the type and fertility of the soil.
o Dark brown or black soils usually contain more organic matter and are more
fertile.
o Red or yellow soils may contain iron oxide and are often less fertile.
2. Soil Texture
23
o Refers to the size of soil particles (sand, silt, and clay) and how they feel when
touched.
o Determines how much water and air the soil can hold.
o Loamy soil has a balanced texture suitable for most crops.
3. Soil Structure
o Refers to how soil particles are arranged or grouped together.
o Good soil structure allows roots, water, and air to move freely.
o Poor soil structure (compacted soil) restricts root growth and water movement.
4. Soil Porosity
o The spaces or pores between soil particles where air and water can move.
o Sandy soils have large pores, while clay soils have small pores.
5. Soil Temperature
o Affects seed germination and root development.
o Sandy soils heat up quickly, while clay soils remain cool longer.
6. Water-Holding Capacity
o The ability of soil to retain water for plant use.
o Clay holds more water than sandy soil, but loamy soil maintains the right balance.
Effects of Soil Properties on Crop Growth
Soil Property Effect on Crops
Texture Determines drainage and nutrient-holding capacity.
Structure Affects root penetration and air circulation.
24
Colour Indicates fertility; dark soils are usually rich in humus.
Porosity Influences how easily roots access air and water.
Temperature Affects seed germination and growth speed.
Good understanding of soil properties helps farmers decide which crops to grow and which
fertilizers or organic matter to apply for better yields.
Factors Affecting Soil Formation
Soil does not form instantly; it takes many years through natural processes. The major factors
that influence soil formation are:
1. Parent Material – the type of rock from which the soil is formed.
2. Climate – rainfall and temperature affect the rate of rock weathering.
3. Living Organisms – plants and animals contribute organic matter.
4. Topography – slope and drainage affect soil depth and fertility.
5. Time – soil formation takes hundreds to thousands of years.
Summary
Soil is a natural resource made up of minerals, organic matter, water, and air. It provides plants
with support, nutrients, and moisture needed for growth. There are three major types of soil —
sandy, clay, and loamy — each with unique properties. Understanding soil types and their
physical characteristics helps farmers make better decisions about what crops to grow and how to
manage the land effectively.

WEEK 6: SOIL TEXTURE AND STRUCTURE
Introduction
Soil texture and soil structure are two important physical properties that determine how soil
behaves, how much water it can hold, and how well it supports plant growth. A farmer who
understands these properties can select suitable crops, improve soil fertility, and increase yield.
This lesson explains the meaning of soil texture and structure, their importance, how they are
determined, and how they affect crop production.
Meaning of Soil Texture
Soil texture refers to the relative proportions of sand, silt, and clay particles that make up the
soil. These particles differ in size:
 Sand – the largest particles, coarse and gritty.
 Silt – medium-sized particles, smooth to touch.
 Clay – the smallest particles, sticky when wet.
The combination of these three determines the texture of the soil and affects how water and air
move through it.
Classes of Soil Based on Texture
There are different classes of soil texture depending on the proportion of sand, silt, and clay.
1. Sandy Soil
o Contains a high amount of sand and very little clay or silt.
o Feels rough and loose when handled.
o Drains water quickly and dries out fast.
o Low in nutrients because they are easily washed away.
27
o Suitable for crops like groundnut, watermelon, and cassava.
2. Clay Soil
o Contains a large amount of clay and little sand.
o Feels smooth and sticky when wet, and hard when dry.
o Poor in drainage; holds water for a long time.
o Rich in nutrients but difficult to work with.
o Suitable for crops like rice and sugarcane.
3. Loamy Soil
o Has a balanced mixture of sand, silt, and clay.
o Feels smooth and slightly sticky; easy to cultivate.
o Retains enough water and nutrients for plants.
o Best soil for most crops such as maize, yam, and vegetables.
Importance of Soil Texture
1. Determines Water-Holding Capacity: Clay holds water for a long time; sandy soil
drains water quickly.
2. Affects Air Circulation: Sandy soils allow more air movement, while clay soils have
poor aeration.
3. Influences Nutrient Retention: Clay soils retain nutrients longer than sandy soils.
4. Determines Soil Workability: Sandy soils are easy to cultivate, while clay soils are
heavy and sticky.
5. Affects Root Development: Roots grow better in loose soils with balanced texture.
28
Determining Soil Texture
The texture of soil can be determined using two common methods:
1. By Feel Method (Touch Method)
o Take a moist sample of soil and rub it between the fingers.
o Sandy soil feels rough, clay soil feels sticky, and loamy soil feels smooth and
slightly sticky.
2. Sedimentation Method (Jar Test)
o Mix a soil sample with water in a transparent jar, shake well, and allow it to settle.
o After a few hours, sand settles at the bottom, silt in the middle, and clay on top.
o The proportion of each layer shows the texture of the soil.
Meaning of Soil Structure
Soil structure refers to the way soil particles (sand, silt, and clay) are arranged or grouped
together to form aggregates or crumbs.
These aggregates form pores or spaces through which air, water, and roots can move. The
arrangement affects the soil’s ability to hold water, nutrients, and support plant roots.
Types of Soil Structure
1. Single-Grained Structure
o Found mostly in sandy soils.
o Particles are loose and not bound together.
o Water drains very quickly.
2. Crumb or Granular Structure
29
o Found in loamy soils.
o Particles are grouped into small rounded crumbs.
o Very good for plant growth; allows air and water movement.
3. Blocky Structure
o Found in clay soils.
o Soil particles form blocks that fit together.
o Moderate water movement; common in subsoil.
4. Platy Structure
o Soil particles are arranged in thin plates or layers.
o Restricts root growth and air movement.
5. Prismatic or Columnar Structure
o Soil particles form vertical columns or pillars.
o Found in clay-rich soils; moderate drainage.
Importance of Soil Structure
1. Facilitates Water Movement: Good soil structure allows proper drainage and prevents
waterlogging.
2. Improves Air Circulation: Helps oxygen reach plant roots easily.
3. Enhances Root Penetration: Roots can move and spread freely.
4. Prevents Soil Erosion: Well-aggregated soils resist erosion by wind or water.
5. Supports Soil Organisms: Provides suitable living conditions for earthworms and
microorganisms.
6. Improves Soil Fertility: Proper structure helps retain nutrients for longer periods.
30

1. Add organic matter such as compost or manure.

Soil texture and structure are key physical properties that determine how well soil supports plant

WEEK 8: SITE SELECTION AND CROP TYPES
Introduction
Before planting any crop, it is very important to choose the right place or site for the farm. The
success of any crop production activity depends largely on selecting a suitable site with good soil,
adequate sunlight, proper drainage, and access to water. Site selection also determines what type
of crop can be grown and how productive it will be.
This lesson explains the factors that influence site selection, the different types of crops based on
lifespan and use, and how to match crops to the right types of land for maximum yield.
Meaning of Site Selection
Site selection is the process of choosing the best piece of land for farming activities. It involves
studying and evaluating the physical and environmental conditions of a particular area to
determine whether it is suitable for crop production.
Selecting a good site helps to prevent waste of labour, time, and money. It also improves crop
yield, reduces pest and disease attacks, and ensures long-term soil productivity.
Factors Influencing Site Selection
33
1. Soil Type and Fertility
o The soil must be rich in nutrients and suitable for the type of crop to be planted.
o Loamy soil is generally considered the best for most crops because it retains
moisture and nutrients without becoming waterlogged.
2. Topography (Shape of the Land)
o The slope or flatness of the land affects water flow and soil erosion.
o Flat or gently sloping land is best for mechanized farming and reduces erosion
risk.
3. Drainage
o A good farm site should allow excess water to drain easily.
o Poor drainage can lead to waterlogging, which damages roots and reduces yield.
4. Climate and Rainfall
o Temperature, rainfall, and sunlight determine what crops can grow well.
o For example, rice thrives in swampy areas, while maize and cassava do better in
moderate rainfall areas.
5. Availability of Water Source
o Access to water for irrigation is essential, especially in dry seasons.
6. Accessibility and Location
o The site should be close to roads or markets to make transportation of farm
produce easier.
7. Vegetation and Land Cover
o Lightly vegetated areas are easier to clear and prepare than thick forests.
8. Pest and Disease History
o Avoid sites previously affected by pests or diseases, as they may reoccur.
34
9. Security and Land Ownership
o The land should be legally owned or leased to prevent disputes.
o Secure areas protect crops and equipment from theft or damage.

WEEK 10: FARM RECORDS AND MANAGEMENT
Introduction
Farm management is the process of planning, organizing, and controlling all activities on a farm
to achieve maximum production and profit. To manage a farm effectively, the farmer must keep
accurate and detailed farm records.
Farm records provide valuable information about everything that happens on the farm —
including crop planting, input purchases, labour used, and sales made. Keeping proper records
enables the farmer to know whether the farm is making progress or running at a loss.
Meaning of Farm Records
Farm records are written or documented statements of all activities, transactions, and events
that take place on the farm.
They show what has been done, when it was done, how it was done, and how much was spent or
earned. Farm records help in planning, decision-making, and management of both crops and
livestock.
For example, if a farmer keeps a record of the quantity of fertilizer applied and the harvest
obtained, he can decide whether to use the same quantity next season or adjust it.
Meaning of Farm Management
Farm management is the process of organizing and coordinating all the resources of the farm
(such as land, labour, capital, and management) to achieve specific goals like increased
production and profit.
It involves:
 Planning: Deciding what crops to grow, when to plant, and what inputs to buy.
 Organizing: Arranging all farm activities and workers in an orderly way.
44
 Directing: Supervising workers and ensuring tasks are done properly.
 Controlling: Checking progress and making corrections where necessary.
 Evaluating: Assessing whether farm goals were achieved at the end of the season.
A good farmer is also a good manager — one who uses farm records to guide decisions and
improve performance.
Importance of Farm Records and Management
1. Helps in Planning:
Farm records provide data needed to plan for future farming seasons.
2. Enables Profit Determination:
By comparing income and expenses, the farmer can calculate profit or loss.
45
3. Improves Decision-Making:
Records show which crops or activities are most profitable.
4. Facilitates Loan and Insurance Applications:
Banks and insurance companies often require accurate records before giving financial
support.
5. Aids Accountability:
Helps the farmer track all money and materials used on the farm.
6. Simplifies Tax Assessment:
Proper records make it easier for government officials to assess farm tax correctly.
7. Helps Detect Waste or Loss:
Records can show where resources are being misused or wasted.
8. Guides Farm Expansion:
Successful operations can be identified and expanded based on record analysis.
Types of Farm Records
Farm records vary depending on the nature of the farm (crop or livestock), but the following are
the most common types:
46
1. Farm Diary

harvesting, etc.
o It serves as a reference for future planning.
2. Financial Record (Cash Book)
o Records all money that comes in (income) and goes out (expenses).
o Helps determine whether the farm is making profit or loss.
3. Input Record
o Contains details of all materials used on the farm such as seeds, fertilizers,

47
o Example: Quantity of maize seeds planted and the cost per kilogram.
4. Production Record
o Shows the quantity of crops or produce harvested from the farm.
o Used to assess yield performance and compare results from season to season.
5. Sales Record
o Documents all items sold, their quantities, prices, buyers’ names, and total income
received.
o Helps monitor market trends and profit.
6. Inventory Record (Stock Record)
o Contains a list of all items owned by the farm, both fixed and current assets.

7. Labour Record

WEEK 1: INTRODUCTION TO HORTICULTURE
Introduction
Horticulture is an important branch of agriculture that focuses on the cultivation of fruits,
vegetables, flowers, and ornamental plants. Unlike large-scale crop farming that often involves
staple foods like rice, maize, or yam, horticulture is mainly concerned with the production of
high-value crops that improve nutrition, beautify the environment, and generate income.
This lesson introduces students to the meaning, branches, importance, and career opportunities in
horticulture.
Meaning of Horticulture
Horticulture is derived from two Latin words — hortus meaning “garden” and cultura meaning
“cultivation.”
It therefore means the art and science of growing garden crops, which include fruits,
vegetables, flowers, and ornamental plants.
Horticulture is a science-based field that applies principles of biology, chemistry, and
environmental science to improve plant growth, productivity, and quality.
Horticulture deals with:
 The growing of plants in gardens, greenhouses, and nurseries.
 The improvement of plants through selection, breeding, and management.
 The protection and conservation of ornamental landscapes.
In essence, horticulture is both a science (because it involves research and experimentation) and
an art (because it involves creativity in design and cultivation).
Branches of Horticulture
Horticulture is divided into several branches based on the types of plants cultivated and their
uses. The main branches are:
57
1. Floriculture:
o This branch deals with the growing and management of flowering and ornamental
plants for decoration and landscaping.
o Examples of plants grown: roses, hibiscus, marigolds, lilies, and sunflowers.
o Floriculture products are used in parks, gardens, weddings, and beautification of
homes and offices.
2. Olericulture:
o This is the cultivation of vegetables used for human consumption.
o It involves crops like tomatoes, pepper, onions, cabbage, okra, and spinach.
o Olericulture emphasizes planting, irrigation, pest control, and harvesting of
vegetables for local and commercial purposes.
3. Pomology:
o Pomology focuses on the production of fruits such as mango, citrus, pineapple,
banana, and pawpaw.
o It covers planting, pruning, fertilizing, and harvesting of fruit crops.
o Pomology also includes fruit storage, packaging, and marketing.
Other related branches sometimes included are:
 Post-harvest Technology: Handling, preservation, and storage of horticultural produce.
 Landscape Horticulture: Design and maintenance of parks, lawns, and recreational
gardens.
58
Importance and Scope of Horticulture
Horticulture plays a vital role in food security, health, environmental conservation, and the
economy. Its importance includes:
1. Source of Nutrition:
Fruits and vegetables are rich in vitamins, minerals, and fiber, which are essential for
good health.
2. Income Generation:
Farmers and traders earn income through the sale of horticultural products such as
vegetables, flowers, and fruits.
3. Employment Opportunities:
Horticulture provides jobs for gardeners, nursery operators, florists, landscapers,
marketers, and exporters.
4. Beautification of the Environment:
Ornamental plants and flowers improve the appearance of surroundings and create
relaxation areas in parks and gardens.
59
5. Promotion of Export Trade:
Many countries earn foreign exchange from exporting horticultural produce like fresh
fruits and cut flowers.
6. Raw Materials for Industries:
Fruit processing industries use horticultural produce to make juice, jam, wine, and
cosmetics.
7. Improvement of Air Quality:
Plants absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen, helping to purify the air and support a
healthy environment.
8. Education and Research:
Horticulture serves as a subject for agricultural education and scientific study in schools,
colleges, and research institutions.
The scope of horticulture therefore extends beyond food production to include environmental
management, landscaping, and industrial development.
Career Opportunities in Horticulture
60
Horticulture offers numerous career paths to individuals with interest in plants and
environmental management. Examples include:
1. Horticulturist – Specializes in the science and technology of plant cultivation.
2. Floriculturist – Grows and manages flowering plants and ornamental gardens.
3. Pomologist – Works with fruit crops to improve yield and quality.
4. Olericulturist – Specializes in vegetable production and management.
5. Landscape Designer/Architect – Designs and maintains beautiful outdoor environments.
6. Nursery Manager – Oversees the propagation and care of young plants.
7. Agricultural Extension Officer – Provides technical advice and training to farmers.
8. Research Scientist – Studies plant genetics, soil fertility, and pest control methods.
These careers can be pursued in government ministries, research institutes, universities, and
private agricultural enterprises.
Summary
Horticulture is the science and art of cultivating fruits, vegetables, flowers, and ornamental plants.
It is divided into branches such as floriculture (flowers), olericulture (vegetables), and pomology
(fruits).
Horticulture is important because it provides nutrition, income, jobs, beauty, and raw materials
for industries.
It also contributes to environmental conservation and national economic growth.

WEEK 2: NURSERY ESTABLISHMENT (PART 1)
Introduction
A nursery is an essential part of horticulture and crop production where young plants are raised
under controlled conditions before being transplanted to the main field.
It serves as a temporary home for seeds or seedlings, providing them with proper care and
protection until they are strong enough to survive outdoor conditions.
In this lesson, we will learn the meaning, importance, types, site selection, and steps involved in
establishing a nursery.
Meaning of Nursery
A nursery is a carefully prepared area or structure where young plants are grown, nurtured, and
managed until they are ready for transplanting.
It provides a favourable environment for the germination of seeds and early growth of seedlings.
Nurseries can be found on farms, gardens, or commercial horticultural centres, and they vary in
size depending on the scale of production.
Examples of plants usually raised in nurseries include tomato, pepper, oil palm, mango, guava,
orange, and ornamental plants like hibiscus and roses.
63
Importance of Nursery
1. Better Germination and Growth:
The controlled conditions of a nursery (such as shade, moisture, and temperature)
promote healthy seed germination and uniform seedling growth.
2. Protection from Harsh Conditions:
Nurseries protect young plants from wind, heavy rain, pests, and diseases that could
destroy them in open fields.
64
3. Efficient Use of Land and Inputs:
Only healthy seedlings are selected for transplanting, reducing waste of land, seeds, and
fertilizers.
4. Early and Uniform Crop Establishment:
Seedlings raised in nurseries are transplanted when conditions are favourable, ensuring
uniform maturity and higher yield.
5. Ease of Management and Monitoring:
Small nursery areas make it easy to water, weed, and fertilize the plants efficiently.
6. Economic Benefits:
Nurseries allow farmers to sell seedlings to other growers, providing an additional source
of income.
7. Facilitates Research and Experimentation:
Scientists and students use nurseries to study plant varieties, seed viability, and growth
performance.
Types of Nursery
Nurseries are classified based on duration and purpose. The main types include:
1. Temporary Nursery:
o Established for a short period (one planting season).
o Usually close to the main field where seedlings will be transplanted.
o Example: vegetable nurseries for tomatoes or peppers.
2. Permanent Nursery:
o Established for long-term use and managed continuously.
o Used for perennial crops such as cocoa, oil palm, citrus, and ornamental trees.
o Often equipped with irrigation, shading, and pot systems.
65
3. Commercial Nursery:
o Managed by private or public enterprises for selling seedlings to farmers,
landscapers, or institutions.
o Requires skilled personnel and quality control.
4. Educational or Research Nursery:
o Found in schools, agricultural colleges, or research institutes.
o Used for training students and conducting experiments on plant growth.
Site Selection for Nursery
Choosing the right site is critical to ensure healthy and successful nursery production.
The following factors should be considered:
1. Accessibility:
The site should be easy to reach for transportation of inputs (seeds, soil, manure) and
seedlings.
2. Soil Quality:
The soil should be fertile, loamy, well-drained, and rich in organic matter to support
healthy root growth.
3. Water Supply:
There should be a reliable source of clean water for regular watering and irrigation.
4. Light and Shade:
The area should receive moderate sunlight and partial shade to prevent scorching of
tender seedlings.
5. Topography (Land Shape):
The site should be flat or gently sloping to avoid waterlogging or erosion.
6. Protection from Wind:
The site should be sheltered from strong winds that can damage young plants.
66
7. Proximity to the Main Field:
The nursery should be located close to where seedlings will be transplanted to reduce
transport stress.
Tools and Materials Used in Nursery Establishment
 Cutlass or hoe – for clearing and shaping the land.
 Rake – for smoothing the surface.
 Watering can – for gentle watering of seedlings.
 Hand trowel – for transplanting and mixing soil.
 Shovel – for digging and soil mixing.
 Wheelbarrow – for carrying soil and manure.
 Sieves – for soil preparation.
 Polythene bags or seed trays – for raising seedlings.
 Shade nets or palm fronds – for covering the nursery.
Steps in Establishing a Nursery
67
1. Clearing the Site:
Remove weeds, debris, and stumps to prepare a clean working area.
2. Soil Preparation:
Loosen the soil with a hoe or shovel and remove stones or hard lumps to ensure proper
aeration.
3. Marking and Layout:
Divide the nursery into plots or seedbeds, leaving paths for easy movement and watering.
68
4. Addition of Manure:
Mix organic manure or compost with topsoil to improve fertility and structure.
5. Seed Sowing:
Sow seeds either by broadcasting or in rows, depending on the crop type.
6. Mulching and Watering:
Apply mulch such as dry grass to conserve moisture, and water regularly with a fine
watering can.
7. Providing Shade:
Erect shade using palm fronds, shade nets, or bamboo frames to protect seedlings from
excessive sun or rain.
8. Weeding and Thinning:
Remove unwanted weeds and excess seedlings to ensure proper spacing and healthy
growth.
9. Transplanting:
When seedlings are mature enough (usually 2–4 weeks), they are carefully uprooted and
transferred to the main farm.
Summary
A nursery is a controlled environment where young plants are raised before being transplanted to
the main field.
Nurseries can be temporary, permanent, commercial, or educational.
They are essential for good germination, early planting, and efficient management.
Establishing a nursery requires proper site selection, good soil, water supply, shade, and regular
care.

WEEK 3: NURSERY BED PREPARATION
Introduction
A nursery bed is a small portion of land or a raised surface where seeds are sown and young
plants are nurtured before being transplanted to the main field.
Preparing a good nursery bed is one of the most important steps in successful crop and
horticultural production because it ensures uniform germination, healthy seedlings, and proper
management of young plants.
In this lesson, we will study the meaning and purpose of a nursery bed, materials and tools used
for preparation, soil sterilization, watering, and the practical procedures involved.
Meaning of Nursery Bed
A nursery bed is a specially prepared piece of land where seeds are sown for the purpose of
raising seedlings.
It is usually small in size, rectangular in shape, and may be raised, flat, or sunken, depending on
the type of crop and soil condition.
A well-prepared nursery bed ensures adequate drainage, aeration, and easy management of the
seedlings.
Purpose of Nursery Bed
1. To Raise Seedlings in a Controlled Environment:
It provides favourable conditions such as moisture, temperature, and soil fertility for seed
germination and early growth.
2. To Facilitate Easy Management:
Since the area is small, watering, weeding, and pest control become easier and more
effective.
71
3. To Save Space and Inputs:
Only healthy seedlings are selected for transplanting, reducing waste of seeds, fertilizers,
and land.
4. To Ensure Uniform Growth:
The even soil and planting conditions in nursery beds promote uniform seedling
development.
5. To Protect Seeds and Seedlings:
It helps protect delicate young plants from wind, heavy rain, drought, and pests.
Types of Nursery Beds
72
1. Raised Nursery Bed:
o Built slightly above ground level (about 15–20 cm high).
o Commonly used in areas with heavy rainfall or poor drainage.
o It prevents waterlogging and ensures good aeration.
2. Flat Nursery Bed:
o Built at the same level as the ground surface.
o Suitable for well-drained soils and crops that do not tolerate excessive dryness.
o Easier to prepare but prone to flooding in wet seasons.
73
3. Sunken Nursery Bed:
o Built below the ground level.
o Used in dry or arid areas to help conserve water.
o It retains moisture better than other types.

9. Watering:

11. Labelling:

Meaning:

Importance of Soil Sterilization:

WEEK 4: FERTILIZER APPLICATION
Introduction
Fertilizer application is one of the most important practices in crop and horticultural production.
Fertilizers supply essential nutrients that plants need for healthy growth, flowering, and fruiting.
Without adequate nutrients, plants grow weak, produce low yields, and become easily attacked
by pests and diseases.
In this lesson, we will learn the meaning and types of fertilizers, various methods of application,
importance of balanced fertilization, and the effects of misuse of fertilizers on crops and soil.
Meaning of Fertilizer
A fertilizer is any substance—natural or artificial—added to the soil or plant to increase its
fertility and supply nutrients necessary for plant growth.
Fertilizers improve the soil’s nutrient content and promote healthy development of roots, leaves,
flowers, and fruits.
There are two main categories of fertilizers: organic and inorganic.
79
Types of Fertilizers
1. Organic Fertilizers
Organic fertilizers are obtained from natural sources such as plants and animals.
They contain nutrients in complex organic forms that are released slowly into the soil.
Examples:
 Compost manure
 Farmyard manure
 Green manure
 Bone meal
 Blood meal
 Poultry droppings
80
Advantages:
1. Improve soil structure and texture.
2. Increase water-holding capacity of soil.
3. Supply micronutrients in addition to major nutrients.
4. Encourage the growth of beneficial soil organisms.
5. Environmentally friendly and non-toxic.
Disadvantages:
1. Nutrients are released slowly.
2. Bulky and difficult to transport.
3. May contain weed seeds if not properly decomposed.
2. Inorganic (Chemical) Fertilizers
Inorganic fertilizers are man-made or synthetic substances produced from chemical compounds.
They provide nutrients in a readily available form that plants can absorb immediately.
Examples:
 NPK (Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium) fertilizer
 Urea
 Ammonium sulphate
 Superphosphate of lime (SSP)
 Potassium chloride (Muriate of potash)
Advantages:
1. Supply nutrients quickly to plants.
2. Easy to store, transport, and apply.
81
3. Require small quantities for large areas.
4. Promote rapid crop growth and high yield.
Disadvantages:
1. Continuous use may reduce soil fertility and destroy microorganisms.
2. Can cause soil acidity or toxicity if misused.
3. Expensive to purchase.
4. May cause environmental pollution if washed into rivers.
Importance of Balanced Fertilization
Balanced fertilization means applying the right type and amount of nutrients according to the
needs of the soil and the crop.
When the correct balance of nutrients is maintained, plants grow well and produce abundant
yields.
Importance:
1. Enhances plant growth and yield.
2. Improves quality of fruits, vegetables, and flowers.
3. Strengthens plant resistance to diseases.
4. Prevents wastage of fertilizer and reduces cost.
5. Protects the environment from chemical pollution.
6. Maintains soil fertility for future planting seasons.
Methods of Fertilizer Application
82
Different crops and soil conditions require different methods of fertilizer application. The major
methods include:
1. Broadcasting
Fertilizer is spread evenly over the entire field either before or after planting.
 Suitable for small-seeded crops like rice and pasture.
 Can be done manually or with a spreader.
 May result in nutrient wastage if not well controlled.
2. Ring (Circular) Application
Fertilizer is applied around the base of a plant, about 5–10 cm from the stem, in a circular pattern.
83
 Common in fruit trees, vegetables, and ornamental plants.
 Ensures nutrients are close to the roots where they are needed.
3. Side Dressing
Fertilizer is applied beside the growing crop, usually in a line parallel to the row of plants.
 Useful for maize, cassava, and vegetables.
 Helps plants access nutrients during their active growth stages.
4. Top Dressing
Fertilizer is applied to the surface of the soil after crops have started growing.
 Often used for nitrogen fertilizers like urea and ammonium sulphate.
 Promotes rapid leaf growth and greenness.
5. Foliar Application
Liquid fertilizer is sprayed directly onto the leaves using a sprayer.
 Plants absorb nutrients through their stomata.
 Suitable for micronutrient deficiency correction.
6. Placement (Banding)
Fertilizer is placed in a hole or furrow close to the seed or plant root during planting.
 Prevents nutrient loss and ensures effective absorption by roots.
Effects of Misuse of Fertilizers
Improper or excessive use of fertilizers can cause serious problems for plants, soil, and the
environment.
Effects include:
84
1. Soil Acidification: Excess nitrogen fertilizer reduces soil pH, making it unsuitable for
crops.
2. Nutrient Imbalance: Too much of one nutrient can prevent others from being absorbed.
3. Plant Burn: High concentration of fertilizer salts damages plant roots and leaves.
4. Water Pollution: Fertilizer runoff contaminates streams and rivers, harming aquatic life.
5. Reduced Soil Microorganisms: Continuous use of chemical fertilizers can kill
beneficial soil organisms.
Safety Precautions in Fertilizer Handling and Application
1. Wear protective gloves, boots, and masks.
2. Avoid inhaling fertilizer dust or fumes.
3. Wash hands after handling fertilizer.
4. Do not apply fertilizer during heavy rain to avoid leaching.
5. Store fertilizers in a dry and well-ventilated area away from children and food items.
6. Follow manufacturer’s instructions for dosage and application method.
Summary
Fertilizers are substances added to the soil to enhance fertility and promote plant growth.
They can be organic (natural) or inorganic (chemical).
Balanced fertilization ensures that plants receive the right nutrients for healthy growth.
Common methods of application include broadcasting, ring method, side dressing, top dressing,
foliar, and placement.
However, misuse of fertilizers can harm plants, reduce soil fertility, and cause pollution.

WEEK 6: SEED COLLECTION AND DORMANCY BREAKING
Introduction
Seed collection and dormancy breaking are essential practices in horticulture and crop
production.
Good seed quality determines the success of any planting operation. Before seeds can be planted,
they must be properly collected, stored, and prepared for germination.
Sometimes, even healthy seeds fail to germinate immediately after planting because they are in a
resting state called dormancy.
This lesson will explain how to collect viable seeds, understand seed dormancy, and apply
various methods to break dormancy before planting.
Meaning of Seed Collection
87
Seed collection refers to the process of gathering mature and viable seeds from healthy plants for
propagation or future planting.
Seeds are collected from crops, fruits, or flowers after they have reached maturity and are ready
to reproduce.
Purposes of Seed Collection:
1. To obtain planting materials for the next season.
2. To preserve good plant varieties and desirable traits.
3. To ensure high-quality, disease-free seeds.
4. To reduce the cost of buying seeds.
5. To maintain seed diversity in horticulture.
Qualities of Good Seeds for Collection:
1. Mature and fully developed.
2. Free from pests and diseases.
3. Obtained from healthy and high-yielding plants.
4. Dry and firm to touch.
5. True to type (from the desired variety).
Sources of Seeds
Seeds can be obtained from various places depending on the type of crop.
Common sources include:
1. Research institutions and seed companies.
2. Agricultural extension agencies.
3. Farmers’ cooperative societies.
88
4. Community seed banks.
5. Direct collection from mature plants or fruits in the garden.
Methods of Seed Collection
The method used depends on the type of crop or fruit being harvested.
1. Manual Collection:
o Seeds are handpicked from ripe fruits or pods.
o Used for crops like beans, tomatoes, and peppers.
2. Mechanical Collection:
o Machines are used to harvest seeds from large farms.
o Suitable for grains like maize, rice, and wheat.
3. Shaking or Beating Method:
o Tree branches are gently shaken or beaten with sticks to release dry seeds into
collection nets.
4. Cutting and Threshing:
o Plants are cut, dried, and threshed to separate seeds from pods or husks.
o Used for legumes and cereals.
5. Winnowing and Cleaning:
o After threshing, seeds are cleaned and separated from chaff or debris using wind

89
After collection, seeds must be stored properly to remain viable (alive and capable of

1. Dry Storage:
o Seeds are kept in airtight containers or bags in a cool, dry place.
2. Refrigerated Storage:
o Used for sensitive seeds that lose viability quickly (e.g., vegetable seeds).
3. Seed Banks:
o Institutions that preserve seeds under controlled conditions for long-term use.

 Seeds must be dry before storage.
 Store in moisture-proof containers.
 Keep away from direct sunlight and pests.
 Label containers with crop name and date of collection.
Meaning of Seed Dormancy
Seed dormancy is the natural condition in which a viable seed fails to germinate even when the
environmental conditions (water, oxygen, temperature) are favorable.
Dormancy is a protective mechanism that prevents seeds from germinating before the right
season or under unfavorable conditions.
Causes of Seed Dormancy:
1. Hard or impermeable seed coat (e.g., in beans or tamarind).
2. Presence of chemical inhibitors that prevent germination.
3. Immature embryo inside the seed.
90
4. Lack of light or oxygen.
5. Extremely dry or cold conditions.
Reasons for Breaking Seed Dormancy
1. To encourage uniform germination.
2. To speed up seed sprouting before planting.
3. To ensure higher germination rate.
4. To prepare seeds for planting in the right season.
5. To promote good crop establishment and yield.
Methods of Breaking Seed Dormancy
1. Mechanical Scarification:
o Scratching or gently rubbing the hard seed coat with sandpaper, knife, or file to
allow water entry.
o Used for seeds with hard coverings like beans and tamarind.
2. Hot Water Treatment:
o Seeds are soaked in hot (not boiling) water for a few minutes, then allowed to
cool before planting.
o Helps soften seed coats and speed up germination.
3. Acid Treatment:
o Seeds are soaked briefly in weak acid solution (e.g., sulfuric acid) to weaken the
seed coat.
o Used in industrial seed preparation under controlled conditions.
91
4. Cold Treatment (Stratification):
o Seeds are exposed to low temperatures for a period to break dormancy.
o Common for temperate crops like apples and strawberries.
5. Chemical Treatment:
o Seeds are treated with chemicals such as potassium nitrate (KNO₃) or gibberellic
acid (GA₃) to promote germination.
6. Dry Heat or Sun Drying:
o Seeds are spread under mild sunlight or warm air to remove excess moisture that
delays germination.
7. Soaking in Water:
o Seeds are soaked in clean water for several hours to soften the seed coat.
o Commonly used for cowpea, maize, and groundnut.
92
Importance of Proper Seed Preparation
1. Ensures high germination rate and better crop stand.
2. Promotes early maturity and uniform growth.
3. Reduces seed wastage and production costs.
4. Improves yield and quality of produce.
5. Encourages good farm management and planning.
Safety Precautions During Dormancy Breaking
1. Wear gloves and goggles when handling acids or chemicals.
2. Avoid soaking seeds in excessively hot water.
93
3. Label treated seeds clearly to avoid confusion.
4. Wash hands thoroughly after treatment.
5. Keep chemicals away from food and water sources.
Summary
Seed collection involves gathering mature and healthy seeds for future planting.
Seeds must be properly cleaned, dried, and stored in suitable conditions to maintain viability.
Some seeds do not germinate immediately because they are dormant.
Seed dormancy can be broken using several methods such as soaking, scarification, hot water, or
chemical treatment.
Proper seed handling and dormancy breaking ensure uniform germination, high yield, and
successful crop production.

WEEK 8: ROOTSTOCK PREPARATION
Introduction
In horticulture, one of the most important plant propagation techniques is grafting, which
involves joining two plant parts so that they grow as one.
The lower part of the plant used for support and nutrient supply is called the rootstock, while the
upper part that bears flowers or fruits is the scion.
Proper preparation of the rootstock is very important because it determines the strength, disease
resistance, and survival of the grafted plant.
This lesson explains the meaning, qualities, preparation steps, and care of rootstock used in
horticultural practices.
96
Meaning of Rootstock
A rootstock is the lower part of a plant that provides the root system for another plant in grafting
or budding operations.
It serves as the base onto which the scion is joined.
The rootstock is responsible for anchoring the plant and supplying water and nutrients from the
soil to the scion.
In simple terms, the rootstock provides the foundation, while the scion determines the fruit or
flower characteristics.
Examples of common rootstocks:
 Citrus species (used for oranges, lemons, and tangerines)
 Mango seedlings
 Guava seedlings
 Apple or pear seedlings
 Rubber and cocoa seedlings
Importance of Rootstock in Horticulture
1. Provides a strong and healthy root system for grafted plants.
2. Improves the resistance of plants to soil-borne diseases and pests.
3. Increases the adaptability of plants to different soil and climatic conditions.
4. Controls the growth and size of the scion (e.g., dwarfing effect).
5. Promotes early fruiting and higher yield.
6. Ensures uniformity in growth and production.
7. Helps propagate plants that do not grow well from seeds.
97
Qualities of a Good Rootstock
A good rootstock must have certain desirable qualities to ensure the success of grafting or
budding.
1. Healthy and Vigorous:
o Should be free from diseases, pests, and deformities.
2. Compatible with the Scion:
o The rootstock and scion must belong to the same or closely related species for
proper union.
3. Well-developed Root System:
o Should have strong, fibrous roots that can anchor the plant firmly and absorb
water effectively.
4. Good Adaptability:
o Must tolerate the local soil, temperature, and moisture conditions.
5. Resistance to Diseases and Pests:
o Especially root rot and nematode attacks.
6. Straight Stem and Suitable Diameter:
o The stem should be smooth, straight, and nearly equal in thickness to the scion.
7. Age and Maturity:
o Usually young, actively growing seedlings are best for use as rootstock.
Preparation of Rootstock
98
The process of preparing a rootstock for grafting or budding involves several careful steps to
ensure a healthy and successful plant union.
Step 1: Selection of Parent Plant
 Choose a healthy and disease-free plant from which seeds or cuttings will be obtained.
 The plant should have desirable characteristics such as strong roots and good growth rate.
Step 2: Raising the Seedlings
 Sow seeds of the selected plant species in a well-prepared nursery bed or pots.
 Water regularly and protect from pests until seedlings are strong enough to be
transplanted.
 The seedlings that grow into sturdy young plants will serve as rootstocks.
Step 3: Transplanting
 Transplant seedlings into polybags or pots when they are about 15–30 cm tall.
99
 This allows each rootstock to develop independently and become stronger.
Step 4: Pruning and Maintenance
 Remove weak or damaged shoots to encourage healthy growth.
 Apply manure or fertilizer to promote root development.
 Weed regularly and water moderately.
Step 5: Selection for Grafting
 When the rootstock reaches the desired height and thickness (about the size of a pencil),
it is ready for grafting or budding.
 The stem should be straight, firm, and free from cracks or wounds.
Care of Rootstock
1. Regular Watering:
o Maintain adequate moisture, especially in dry periods.
2. Weeding:
o Keep the nursery or pots free from weeds that compete for nutrients.
3. Pest and Disease Control:
o Inspect regularly and apply organic or chemical control methods where necessary.
4. Fertilization:
o Apply compost or NPK fertilizer sparingly to promote healthy root growth.
5. Proper Spacing:
o Avoid overcrowding to allow enough air and sunlight for each plant.
6. Shading and Protection:
o Provide shade for young rootstocks to protect from direct sunlight and heavy rain.
100
Common Mistakes in Rootstock Preparation
1. Using diseased or weak seedlings.
2. Selecting rootstock and scion that are not compatible.
3. Overwatering, which can cause root rot.
4. Using over-aged or too-young seedlings for grafting.
5. Poor handling that injures the rootstock during transplanting.
Demonstration (Practical Activity)
Students can observe or participate in a simple demonstration:
1. Select healthy citrus seedlings as rootstocks.
2. Transplant them into pots or polybags.
3. Label and care for them for a few weeks until they are ready for budding or grafting.
This practical helps students understand how to select, grow, and maintain rootstocks before
actual grafting.
Summary
A rootstock is the lower part of a plant that supports the scion in grafting or budding.
It must be healthy, vigorous, and compatible with the scion for a successful union.
Proper preparation and care—such as watering, weeding, and pest control—ensure that the
rootstock grows strong and ready for propagation.
Using the right rootstock leads to stronger plants, early fruiting, and better yields in horticulture.

WEEK 9: SCION COLLECTION
Introduction
In horticultural propagation, grafting involves joining two living plant parts so that they unite
and grow as a single plant.
The rootstock forms the lower part, while the scion forms the upper part that determines the
variety and quality of fruits or flowers.
For grafting to succeed, both the rootstock and scion must be carefully selected and prepared.
This lesson focuses on the meaning of a scion, its qualities, techniques of collection, and proper
handling to ensure successful grafting.
Meaning of Scion
A scion is a detached shoot or bud of a desired plant variety that is grafted onto a rootstock.
It is the upper part of the new plant that will produce the branches, leaves, flowers, and fruits.
The scion carries the genetic characteristics of the parent plant, such as fruit size, taste, colour,
and yield quality.
In simple terms:
The scion determines what kind of plant you get, while the rootstock determines how strong the
plant will grow.
Examples of Common Scions Used in Horticulture
 Citrus (orange, tangerine, lemon)
 Mango varieties
 Cocoa
 Guava
 Apple and pear
103
 Rubber
Importance of Scion in Grafting
1. Determines the variety and quality of fruit or flower produced.
2. Preserves desirable traits of a parent plant, such as sweetness, flavour, and size.
3. Enables mass propagation of improved plant species.
4. Reduces the time taken for a plant to start bearing fruits (early maturity).
5. Ensures uniformity in fruit type and production.
6. Helps in combining disease resistance from rootstock with productivity from scion.
Qualities of a Good Scion
To achieve successful grafting, the scion must possess specific desirable qualities:
1. Healthy and Disease-free:
o Should not show signs of infection, pest attack, or physical damage.
2. Mature but Not Old:
o Scion should be taken from a mature branch that has stopped growing rapidly but
is still fresh and green.
3. Proper Size and Thickness:
o The diameter of the scion should be almost equal to that of the rootstock (about
the size of a pencil).
4. True-to-type:
o Must come from a mother plant known for desirable qualities (good fruit size,
taste, or yield).
104
5. Vigorous Growth:
o Should be taken from a well-nourished and vigorously growing parent plant.
6. Presence of Viable Buds:
o Scion should have at least two or three healthy buds that will sprout after grafting.
7. Moist and Fresh:
o Should not be dry or withered; moisture helps maintain life before grafting.
Techniques of Scion Collection
Proper collection of scions requires care and timing. The following are the main steps:
Step 1: Selection of Parent Plant
 Choose a healthy, high-yielding plant with desirable traits.
 Avoid plants that show symptoms of disease or stress.
Step 2: Choosing the Right Time
 Scions are best collected during the dormant or early growing season, when the plant is
not under stress from heat or drought.
 Early morning or late evening is preferable to avoid water loss.
Step 3: Selecting Suitable Branches
 Select straight, healthy shoots with well-developed buds.
 Avoid very soft or overly woody branches.
Step 4: Cutting the Scion
 Use a sharp, sterilized knife or secateurs to make clean cuts.
 Each scion should be about 10–15 cm long and have 2–4 buds.
 Make the cut just below a node to promote new growth.
105
Step 5: Handling and Labeling
 Immediately wrap the cut scions in moist tissue paper or banana leaves to prevent
drying.
 Label each scion with the variety name and date of collection.
Step 6: Storage
 If not used immediately, store scions in a cool, humid place or refrigerate in sealed
plastic bags.
 Scions can remain viable for several days if kept moist and cool.
Handling and Care of Scions Before Grafting
1. Keep the scion moist and shaded to prevent drying.
2. Avoid exposing scions to direct sunlight.
3. Do not crush or bend the scion while handling.
4. Use clean and sharp tools to avoid infection.
5. Graft as soon as possible after collection (preferably within 24–48 hours).
Common Mistakes in Scion Collection
1. Collecting scions from unhealthy or diseased plants.
2. Using dry or immature shoots.
3. Cutting too long or too short scions.
4. Allowing scions to dry out before grafting.
5. Using blunt or unsterilized tools that damage plant tissue.
106
Demonstration (Practical Activity)

WEEK 1: VEGETATIVE PROPAGATION IN HORTICULTURAL CROPS
Introduction
Vegetative propagation is one of the most important techniques in horticulture and crop
production. It enables farmers to produce new plants from existing ones without using seeds.
Many horticultural crops such as oranges, roses, cassava, and hibiscus are propagated through
this method because it ensures that the new plants retain all the desirable qualities of the parent
plant.
In this lesson, students will learn the meaning, methods, advantages, and disadvantages of
vegetative propagation.
Meaning of Vegetative Propagation
Vegetative propagation refers to the process of growing new plants from the vegetative parts of
an existing plant such as stems, roots, or leaves instead of from seeds.
It is a form of asexual reproduction, meaning no fusion of male and female gametes occurs.
The resulting plants are clones, i.e., they are genetically identical to the parent plant.
Examples of plants propagated vegetatively: cassava, yam, sugarcane, banana, pineapple,
citrus, rose, and guava.
Importance of Vegetative Propagation
1. It helps to maintain desirable traits such as fruit quality, taste, or colour in plants.
2. It allows faster multiplication of valuable crops.
3. It helps in the propagation of seedless plants such as sugarcane and banana.
4. It ensures uniform growth and maturity in plants.
122
5. It allows early flowering and fruiting compared to plants grown from seeds.
6. It helps in disease resistance improvement through grafting of resistant rootstocks.
Methods of Vegetative Propagation
1. Cutting
 This involves cutting a portion of the plant stem, root, or leaf and placing it in a suitable
medium such as soil or water to develop roots and grow into a new plant.
 Example: stem cutting in cassava, rose, and hibiscus.
 Steps: select a healthy plant, cut 10–15 cm portion, remove lower leaves, plant in moist
soil, and water regularly.
123
2. Layering
 In layering, a branch of the parent plant is bent down and partially buried in the soil while
still attached.
 Roots develop at the buried part, and the new plant is later detached and transplanted.
 Example: bougainvillea and yam.
 Types of layering: simple layering, air layering (marcotting).
3. Grafting
 This is the process of joining two plant parts so that they grow together as one plant.
 The scion (upper part with buds) is attached to the rootstock (lower part with roots).
 Example: citrus, mango, and apple.
 Grafting helps combine desirable qualities such as good fruit from the scion and disease
resistance from the rootstock.
4. Budding
 Budding is similar to grafting but uses only a single bud instead of a stem piece.
 The bud from one plant (scion) is inserted into another plant (rootstock).
 Example: roses, citrus, and peaches.
 It is used for mass multiplication of ornamental and fruit crops.
5. Division and Suckering
 Division involves separating clumps or bulbs of a plant into smaller units that can grow
individually.
 Example: pineapple, banana, and ginger.
 Suckers growing from the base of the parent plant can be removed and replanted.
124
Advantages of Vegetative Propagation
1. New plants are identical to the parent, preserving desirable qualities.
2. It allows faster growth and early maturity.
3. Useful for plants that produce few or no seeds.
4. It enables the combination of traits (e.g., disease resistance and fruit quality).
5. It ensures uniform yield and quality.
6. It is possible to propagate seedless varieties such as banana and pineapple.
Disadvantages of Vegetative Propagation
1. Lack of variation — plants are genetically identical, so they can all be affected by the
same disease or pest.
2. Propagation requires skilled labour and proper handling.
3. Transporting cuttings or grafts is often difficult and costly.
4. It produces fewer plants compared to seed propagation.
5. Continuous vegetative propagation may cause loss of vigour in plants over time.
Conditions Necessary for Successful Vegetative Propagation
1. Use of healthy and disease-free parent plants.
2. Availability of adequate water and suitable soil.
3. Proper temperature and humidity.
4. Clean and sharp tools to prevent damage.
5. Protection of young propagules from pests and harsh weather.
125
Summary
Vegetative propagation is the process of producing new plants from parts of an existing plant
instead of from seeds.
It is a common method used in horticulture because it ensures faster, uniform, and reliable crop
multiplication.
The main methods include cutting, layering, grafting, and budding.
Although it produces identical plants with desirable traits, it requires skill, labour, and proper
management to avoid disease spread and low plant vigour.

Demonstration of Grafting

3. Carefully remove a bud shield from the parent plant (scion source).

5. Tie the area firmly with budding tape, leaving the bud exposed.
6. Water the plant regularly and keep it under shade.

4. Keep the soil moist until roots develop at the buried section.
5. After rooting, cut the new plant from the parent and transplant it.

WEEK 6: CONCEPT OF MONEY
Introduction
Money is one of the most important tools in the business world, including agriculture and
horticulture.
Every farmer or horticulturist who wants to sell products, buy tools, or keep business records
must understand the use and management of money.
Before the invention of money, people exchanged goods and services directly, a practice known
as barter trade. However, barter trade had several problems, which led to the introduction of
money as a medium of exchange.
This lesson explains the meaning, functions, and types of money, as well as how to manage and
save money wisely in horticultural enterprises.
147
Meaning of Money
Money is anything that is generally accepted as a medium of exchange for goods and services,
and for the settlement of debts.
In simple terms, money allows people to buy what they need and sell what they produce without
having to exchange goods directly.
Examples of money include: coins, paper notes, cheques, bank transfers, and digital currencies.
Historical Background of Money
Before money was invented, people practiced barter trade, which means the direct exchange of
goods for goods or services for services.
For example, a farmer could exchange yam for fish or fruits for clothes.
However, barter trade was not convenient because:
1. It required a double coincidence of wants (both parties must need what the other has).
2. It was difficult to determine the exact value of items exchanged.
3. Some goods were not easily divisible (e.g., exchanging a goat for a handful of maize).
4. It was difficult to store wealth in perishable goods.
Because of these problems, societies later developed money to make trade easier, safer, and more
efficient.
Functions of Money
Money performs several key functions that make it essential in economic and agricultural
activities.
1. Medium of Exchange: Money allows people to buy and sell goods and services easily.
148
2. Measure of Value: It provides a common standard for measuring the worth of goods and
services.
3. Store of Value: Money can be saved and used in the future without losing its value
quickly.
4. Standard of Deferred Payment: Money allows people to borrow and repay debts
conveniently.
5. Unit of Account: It helps to keep business records and accounts accurately.
For example, a horticulturist who sells tomatoes for ₦5,000 uses money as a medium of
exchange and a measure of value.
Characteristics of Money
For anything to serve as money, it must have certain qualities:
1. Portability: Easy to carry around.
2. Durability: Must last long without getting spoilt easily.
3. Divisibility: Can be divided into smaller units for small or large transactions.
4. Acceptability: Widely accepted by people as a means of payment.
5. Uniformity: Each unit must be the same in quality and value (e.g., all ₦100 notes are
equal).
6. Stability of Value: Should maintain its value over time.
7. Recognizability: Easy to identify and difficult to counterfeit.
Types of Money
Different forms of money exist in modern society. Each type serves the same purpose but may be
used differently depending on the situation.
149
1. Commodity Money: Items like salt, gold, cowries, or livestock that were once used as
money.
2. Coin Money: Metal coins made of copper, silver, or nickel used for smaller transactions.
3. Paper Money: Banknotes issued by the government or central bank.
4. Bank Deposits/Cheques: Money kept in the bank and withdrawn or transferred by
writing cheques.
5. Electronic Money (E-Money): Digital forms of money such as ATM cards, bank
transfers, and mobile banking.
6. Cryptocurrency: A modern digital currency (e.g., Bitcoin) used mainly for online
transactions.
Importance of Money in Horticulture and Crop Production
Money plays a vital role in the success of any horticultural enterprise. It is needed at every stage
— from production to marketing.
Importance includes:

Meaning of Bookkeeping
Bookkeeping is the process of recording all financial and business transactions in an orderly
manner.
It helps the farmer to keep track of income (money received) and expenditure (money spent) in
the course of running the farm.
In simple terms, bookkeeping is the act of keeping written records of what goes in and out of a
farm business.
Example:
If a horticulturist buys fertilizer for ₦5,000 and sells vegetables for ₦10,000, these two

Meaning of Profit and Loss
Profit is the financial gain made when the total income is greater than total expenses.
Loss occurs when total expenses are more than total income.
Formulas:
 Profit = Total Income – Total Expenses
 Loss = Total Expenses – Total Income
Example:
If a farmer earns ₦50,000 from selling vegetables and spends ₦30,000 on production,
Profit = ₦50,000 – ₦30,000 = ₦20,000.
If another farmer earns ₦25,000 but spends ₦30,000,
Loss = ₦30,000 – ₦25,000 = ₦5,000.

6. Store the record book safely for future reference.

WEEK 11: REVISION
Introduction
Revision is an important part of learning that helps students review all topics taught during the
term.
In horticulture and crop production, revision enables learners to recall key concepts, definitions,
and practices that contribute to successful crop growth, post-harvest handling, and agribusiness
management.
This week’s lesson is a comprehensive review of all the major topics covered in the third term,
from vegetative propagation to enterprise development. It aims to reinforce understanding,
clarify misconceptions, and prepare students for examinations.
Summary of Key Topics
1. Vegetative Propagation in Horticultural Crops
 Meaning: The process of growing new plants from parts of existing plants such as stems,
roots, or leaves rather than from seeds.
 Methods:
1. Cutting
2. Layering
3. Grafting
4. Budding
 Advantages:
o Produces identical plants (true-to-type).
o Faster maturity and early fruiting.
o Maintains desirable traits.
 Disadvantages:
171
o Limited number of new plants.
o May spread diseases from parent plants.
2. Practical Session on Vegetative Propagation
 Activities: Demonstrations of cutting, layering, grafting, and budding.
 Tools Used: Knife, budding tape, grafting knife, pruning shears, watering can, and
containers.
 Maintenance: Regular watering, shading, and pest control ensure survival of propagated
plants.
3. Post-Harvest Management of Vegetables
 Meaning: The handling of vegetables immediately after harvest to maintain quality and
prevent spoilage.
 Processes:
1. Harvesting at the right maturity stage.
2. Sorting and grading based on size and quality.
3. Packaging in baskets, crates, or boxes.
4. Storage under cool and dry conditions.
5. Transportation to markets or consumers.
 Importance: Reduces losses, maintains freshness, and improves market value.
4. Concept of Entrepreneurship in Horticulture
 Meaning: Entrepreneurship is the ability to identify opportunities in horticulture and turn
them into profitable ventures.
172
 Importance:
o Encourages self-employment and creativity.
o Improves income and living standards.
o Contributes to agricultural development.
 Examples of Agripreneurs:
o Fruit juice producers
o Flower nursery owners
o Vegetable distributors
5. Concept of Money
 Meaning: Money is a medium of exchange used for buying and selling goods and
services.
 Functions of Money:
1. Medium of exchange
2. Store of value
3. Unit of account
4. Standard of deferred payment
 Types of Money: Coins, notes, cheques, electronic money, and barter substitutes.
 Money Management: Involves budgeting, saving, and spending wisely to ensure
business stability.
6. Bookkeeping in Horticulture and Crop Production
 Meaning: Bookkeeping is the systematic recording of financial transactions in a business.
173
 Types of Farm Records:
1. Input record
2. Output record
3. Sales and purchase record
4. Profit and loss record
5. Labour record
 Importance:
o Helps track income and expenditure.
o Guides decision-making.
o Promotes transparency and accountability.
7. Business Start-Up in Horticulture
 Meaning: The process of establishing a new horticultural business from idea to operation.
 Stages:
1. Idea generation
2. Feasibility study
3. Business plan development
4. Sourcing for capital
5. Implementation and monitoring
 Marketing: The process of promoting and selling horticultural products to customers.
 Importance: Encourages entrepreneurship and economic self-reliance.
8. Enterprise Development

 Meaning: Building and expanding a business to achieve growth and sustainability.
 Importance: Provides employment, income, innovation, and food security.
 Steps:
1. Idea generation
2. Planning and feasibility study

4. Evaluation and improvement
 Qualities of a Good Entrepreneur: Hardworking, honest, creative, disciplined, and a
