§01 · The Threat Model

The Threat Model

Every section in this book is downstream of one question, asked before any defense is chosen: secure it against whom, protecting what, at what cost?

The reduction
threat model = asset + adversary + attack surface + acceptable loss security = a limited budget, spent against the risks that matter to YOU acceptable loss = what you can afford to lose — you cannot protect everything equally

Security is not the absence of risk; there is no such thing. The most common real-world failure in the field is not a broken lock. It is a strong lock on the wrong door, bought by someone who never wrote a threat model.

The gate — who is the adversary?
GATE → Who wants it, and what do they bring? Anyone, untargeted, at scale → Opportunistic You specifically, for a reason → Targeted A government's resources behind them → Nation-state (APT) Someone already inside → Insider
AdversaryResourcesHow you resist
OpportunisticLow — reused toolsBasic hygiene; be harder than the next target
TargetedMedium — will adaptDefense in depth; assume they get through once
Nation-stateHigh — time and moneyAssume breach; detect and contain
InsiderAlready trustedLeast privilege; monitoring; separation of duties
the adversary decides the defense, not the other way around. Hygiene that stops a spammer is useless against a patient nation-state; the heavy monitoring you build for a nation-state is wasted on a spammer. Buying controls before naming the adversary is buying a winter coat without knowing the season.

Assume breach — the humbler idea

Prevent → stop them getting in (necessary, never sufficient) Detect → notice fast when they do (because they will) Respond → contain and evict them Recover → restore what was lost
prevention has a 100% failure rate given enough time and a determined enough adversary. Detection and response are where real losses are decided — the Morris worm (Book I) taught it: the machines that recovered were the ones whose owners noticed fast, not the ones with the best locks.

§02 · Identity & Access

Identity & Access

Where the modern attacker spends most of their effort. They rarely break the cryptography. They log in.

The reduction — AAA
AUTHENTICATION → who are you? (prove identity) AUTHORIZATION → what may you do? (grant permission) ACCOUNTING → what did you do? (record it, so it can be reviewed)

Book I gave the first two. The mature discipline adds the third — accounting — the one defenders neglect until the moment they need it. You cannot investigate, contain, or even notice an intrusion if nothing wrote down what happened.

a system with perfect locks and no logs is a crime scene with no witnesses. You may know you were robbed, but never how — so you cannot stop it happening again. Every incident response in Book III begins with someone reading logs that either exist or do not.

The factors, and why one is never enough

password attacks, by shape (know the shape to block it): guesses against one account → brute force one common password, many accounts → password spraying passwords leaked elsewhere, reused → credential stuffing defense = a second factor the attacker does not hold (MFA)
every password attack is the same attack — reuse of a knowledge-factor secret the attacker should not hold — stopped by the same countermeasure. The multi-decade war over passwords ends in one rule: never let one factor be enough.

Privilege escalation — the climb

GATE → which way is the attacker climbing? HIGHER privileges than their account should have → Vertical OTHER accounts at the same level → Horizontal ceiling on the breach = the loosest permission in reach
privilege escalation is not a separate attack; it is the collection on a debt least privilege already owed. The attacker climbs exactly as far as the loosest permission allows and no further — the ceiling on every breach was set, before the attacker arrived, by whoever decided how much power each account would hold.

§03 · The Network & Where Attacks Live

The Network & Where Attacks Live

The backbone everything attaches to. Like the carbon chain in organic chemistry, the layers are a fixed skeleton — and every attack lives at a specific layer, the way every functional group sits at a specific carbon.

The reduction — the layered model
a message = data wrapped in layer after layer of headers each layer adds its own envelope, and strips it at the far end an attack = targeting the one layer where a control is missing
Layer (TCP/IP)JobWhere the attack lives
ApplicationWhat the user sees — web, email, DNSInjection, phishing, malicious payloads
TransportReliable delivery — TCP/UDP portsPort scanning, session hijacking, floods
InternetAddressing & routing — IPSpoofing, man-in-the-middle, rerouting
LinkThe physical hop — Ethernet, Wi-FiEavesdropping, rogue access points
the layers are the reason "where is the control?" is a sharper question than "is there a control?" Encryption at the application layer does nothing for a spoof at the internet layer; a firewall at the transport layer does nothing for an injection at the application layer. A defender who cannot name the layer cannot name the gap.

The perimeter, and its end

OLD model: a hard shell, a soft inside (trust = being inside the wall) NEW model: ZERO TRUST — verify every request, inside or out trust = never granted by location; always earned per request
the trusted-host relationship that let the Morris worm spread (Book I) is the same assumption zero trust exists to kill. "Inside the network" stopped meaning "safe" the moment one breached machine inside the wall could reach every other. Zero trust is least privilege, applied to the network itself.

Reference Plate · Pillar I

The Cyber Kill Chain

The Reaction-Mechanism Equivalent — a reaction proceeds in ordered steps, and so does an intrusion
Grounded in: Lockheed Martin, "Intelligence-Driven Computer Network Defense" (the Cyber Kill Chain), 2011 — a named, published model, not this book's invention.

A chemical reaction mechanism shows the ordered steps between reactants and products — and a defender who knows the mechanism can interrupt it at any step. The kill chain is the same idea for an intrusion: seven ordered stages, and breaking any one of them breaks the whole attack.

#StageWhere the defender breaks it
1ReconnaissanceReduce public exposure; detect scanning
2Weaponization(attacker-side — little defender reach)
3DeliveryEmail filtering, web filtering
4ExploitationPatching, hardening
5InstallationEndpoint detection, least privilege
6Command & ControlNetwork monitoring, egress filtering
7Actions on ObjectivesData-loss prevention, segmentation

Why ordered stages matter. Just as interrupting one step of a reaction mechanism stops the product from ever forming, breaking one link in the chain stops the breach — the attacker cannot act on objectives (7) if command and control (6) never connects. This is the defender's great structural advantage in the classical era: the attacker must complete every step in order; the defender needs to win only once, at any step. Book III's hardest news is what happens to that advantage when the seven steps run in seconds instead of weeks.

§04 · Vulnerability Classes

Vulnerability Classes

Most "new" vulnerabilities are the same few families, worn differently — exactly as compound chemistry's thousands of reactions reduce to one acid/base/salt fork asked again with a different partner.

The gate — what kind of weakness is this?
GATE → What did the system fail to do? Trusted input it should have checked → Injection family Failed to confirm who is asking → Broken authentication Failed to confirm what they may touch → Broken access control Left a door open by default → Misconfiguration Trusted data that crossed a boundary → Deserialization / SSRF family
FamilyThe root failureThe defensive cure
InjectionInput treated as command, not as dataTreat all input as untrusted; separate code from data
Broken authenticationIdentity not properly confirmedMFA; strong session handling
Broken access controlAuthorization not enforced per requestServer-side checks on every access (complete mediation)
MisconfigurationInsecure defaults left in placeFail-safe defaults; harden; remove the unused
Exposed secretsCredentials left in code or filesScan repos; rotate; never embed secrets
the buffer overflow of the Morris worm (Book I) and a modern injection flaw are the same failure — a program trusting its input to be what it expected. Fifty years of "new" vulnerabilities are variations on one unlearned lesson: never trust the size, shape, or content of what crosses the boundary into your program.

Why this matters for Book III

Hold this list. When an autonomous AI agent attacks in Book III, it does not discover new families — it runs down this exact short list, faster and more patiently than a human would. The vulnerability classes do not change. Only the speed and tirelessness of the thing checking them does.

Reference Plate · Pillar II

MITRE ATT&CK

The Periodic Table Equivalent — every known adversary behaviour, catalogued by an external authority
Grounded in: MITRE ATT&CK — a public, maintained knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques observed in the real world, curated by MITRE, not by this book.

The Periodic Table earned trust because an external body — IUPAC — decides what goes in it, by checkable evidence. ATT&CK holds itself to the same standard: it is not a list of what an attacker could do in theory, but a catalogue of what has actually been observed, each technique tied to real reported intrusions.

TACTIC = the attacker's goal at a stage (the "why") TECHNIQUE = the specific way they achieve it (the "how") the matrix = tactics across the top, techniques beneath each
Tactic (a goal)Example technique family (defensive view)
Initial AccessPhishing; exploiting a public-facing app
PersistenceCreating accounts; scheduled tasks
Privilege EscalationAbusing over-permissive access
Credential AccessHarvesting stored or exposed secrets
Lateral MovementUsing trust between hosts
ExfiltrationMoving data out over a channel

Why a catalogue beats a story. A defender who thinks in war stories defends against the last attack. A defender who thinks in ATT&CK defends against the catalogue of observed techniques — the same shift chemistry made when it stopped teaching "reactions I have seen" and started teaching the table that organizes all of them. Book III's threat reports (Check Point, Microsoft, Google) all map ARTEX's behaviour onto this exact matrix — proof that an AI attacker, for all its speed, is still playing techniques already in the table.

The OWASP Top Ten

The Named-Family Equivalent — the handful of classes that cover most of what actually goes wrong
Grounded in: OWASP (Open Worldwide Application Security Project) Top Ten — a community standard, periodically revised from real-world data on the most common web application risks.

Compound chemistry did not ask a student to memorize every salt; it taught the acid/base/salt families and let the thousands of specifics fall out. The OWASP Top Ten does the same for web security: not every bug, but the handful of families that account for most real breaches.

FamilyPlain meaning
Broken Access ControlUsers reach what they shouldn't
Cryptographic FailuresSensitive data not properly protected
InjectionInput treated as command
Insecure DesignThe flaw is in the plan, not the code
Security MisconfigurationInsecure defaults, left in place
Vulnerable ComponentsOld libraries with known holes
Identification/Auth FailuresWeak login and session handling

The list that barely changes. The striking thing about the Top Ten across its revisions is how little it moves — broken access control and injection have led it for years. That stability is the whole lesson: the families are stable, the specific exploits are endless, and a defender who holds the families holds the ground the exploits keep re-attacking. This is the acid/base/salt insight, transplanted whole.

§05 · Defense in Depth

Defense in Depth

No single control holds. Defense in depth is the layered toolkit — the four-instruments idea of the chemistry volume, where no one instrument answers everything and the chain of them does.

The reduction — controls by job
PREVENTIVE → stop it happening (firewall, MFA, patching) DETECTIVE → notice it happening (logging, monitoring, alerts) CORRECTIVE → fix it after (backups, incident response) defense in depth = layers, so one failure is not total failure
LayerExample controls
PerimeterFirewall, segmentation, zero-trust access
IdentityMFA, least privilege, strong session handling
EndpointEDR, patching, hardening
DataEncryption at rest and in transit, DLP, backups
MonitoringCentralized logs, alerting, anomaly detection
defense in depth is the CIA triad's trade-off, solved in layers instead of all at once. Each layer alone makes a weak promise; stacked, they make a strong one — because an attacker now has to defeat every layer in sequence, while the defender needs any one layer to hold long enough to be noticed.

The complete hierarchy, restated

THREAT MODEL (asset + adversary + surface + acceptable loss) ↓ IDENTITY (the currency the attacker steals rather than breaks) ↓ NETWORK LAYERS (where each attack lives) ↓ KILL CHAIN → the ordered steps; break any one → Pillar I ↓ VULNERABILITY CLASSES → the short, stable family list → Pillars II, III ↓ DEFENSE IN DEPTH → preventive / detective / corrective, in layers ↓ ASSUME BREACH → detect and respond, because prevention eventually fails

This is the classical discipline, whole. A complete craft, built on one assumption that held for fifty years: the adversary is a human, working at human speed, one keyboard at a time. Hold that assumption in view. Book III is the story of the day it broke.

Reference Plate · Pillar IV

The NIST CSF Functions

The Activity Series Equivalent — a fixed, ordered set that tells you what to do and in what order
Grounded in: NIST Cybersecurity Framework — the core functions by which a US federal standard organizes an entire security program.

The Activity Series is valuable because it is ordered and fixed — read it once and it governs every displacement that follows. The CSF functions are the same kind of object for a whole security program: a fixed, ordered set that turns "do security" into named, sequenced work.

GOVERN → set the strategy and accountability IDENTIFY → know what you have and what threatens it PROTECT → put the safeguards in place DETECT → notice when something is wrong RESPOND → act to contain and evict RECOVER → restore and learn

Why order is the whole point. You cannot protect what you have not identified, cannot respond to what you did not detect, cannot recover without having protected your backups. The functions are not a menu to pick from — they are a sequence, each depending on the one before, exactly as the Activity Series' power comes from its fixed order and not from any single entry. A program missing one function is a chain missing a link, and the attacker finds the gap by walking the same ordered path the defender skipped.

Glossary — every definition, gathered

AAA — authentication, authorization, accounting; the three jobs of an identity system.

Accounting (auditing) — recording what users did, so intrusions can be noticed and investigated.

Assume breach — the stance that prevention will eventually fail, so detection and response must be funded.

Attack surface — the complete set of points where an attacker could try to get in, including the forgotten ones.

Credential stuffing — reusing passwords leaked from one site against another.

Defense in depth — layering preventive, detective, and corrective controls so one failure is not total.

Injection — a flaw where input is treated as a command rather than as data.

Kill chain — Lockheed Martin's seven ordered stages of an intrusion; breaking any one breaks the attack.

Lateral movement — spreading from one compromised host to others by abusing trust between them.

Least privilege — every account and process gets only the access its job requires; sets the blast radius.

MITRE ATT&CK — a public knowledge base of real-world adversary tactics and techniques.

OWASP Top Ten — a community standard listing the most common web application security risks.

Password spraying — trying one common password against many accounts.

Privilege escalation — gaining more access than an account should have (vertical) or reaching peer accounts (horizontal).

Threat model — the statement of asset, adversary, attack surface, and acceptable loss that decides which defenses are worth buying.

Zero trust — granting trust per request and never by network location; least privilege applied to the network.
