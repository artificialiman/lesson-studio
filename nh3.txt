WEEK 1: ISLAMIC MOVEMENTS IN WEST AFRICA
1.1 INTRODUCTION: ISLAM IN WEST AFRICA
Islam had a long history in West Africa, dating back to the 8th century. It was introduced to the
Western Sudan (regions of Ghana, Mali, Songhai) by Berber and Arab traders from North
Africa via the Trans-Saharan trade routes. For centuries, Islam was primarily the religion of
the urban commercial and ruling classes in major trade cities like Timbuktu, Gao, and Kano.
The rural populations largely remained animist, and even the rulers who converted often blended
Islamic practices with traditional animist rituals (a practice called syncretism).
By the 18th and 19th centuries, a powerful wave of Islamic revivalism swept across West Africa,
leading to a series of Jihads (holy wars) aimed at purifying and spreading the faith.
1.2 CAUSES OF ISLAMIC MOVEMENTS (JIHADS) IN WEST AFRICA
These Jihads were complex events, driven by a combination of religious, political, social, and
economic factors.
A. RELIGIOUS CAUSES
1. Desire for Religious Purity (Puritanism): The main driving force. The Jihad leaders
(who were often Fulani scholars, or Ulama) were distressed by the state of Islam in West
Africa. They preached that the religion had become corrupted.
2. Syncretism: They condemned the mixing of Islamic practices with traditional animist
rituals (e.g., sacrifices to spirits, ancestral worship) by existing rulers.
3. Laxity of Rulers: They accused the Hausa (in Nigeria) and other rulers of being
"nominal" Muslims (Muslims in name only). They pointed to practices like public
consumption of alcohol, immodest dressing of women, and the consultation of diviners
and sorcerers at the royal courts.
2
4. Influence of Revivalism Elsewhere: The Jihads in West Africa were part of a wider
global Islamic revival movement, inspired by similar reformist ideas in the Arabian
Peninsula (e.g., Wahhabism).
B. POLITICAL CAUSES
1. Corrupt and Oppressive Governance: The Hausa rulers were seen as corrupt,
tyrannical, and unjust.
2. Un-Islamic Taxation: Rulers imposed heavy, arbitrary, and numerous taxes (like market
taxes, cattle taxes) that were not sanctioned by Islamic Law (Shari'a). This burdened the
common people, especially the pastoral Fulani.
3. Injustice in the Courts: The existing legal systems were seen as corrupt. Justice was
often for sale, and judgments did not follow the Shari'a.
4. Desire for Political Power: The Fulani scholars (Ulama) formed a highly educated and
influential intellectual class. However, they were often excluded from real political power
by the hereditary ruling aristocracy. The Jihads became a vehicle for this educated elite to
seize control and establish a new political order based on their ideals.
C. SOCIO-ECONOMIC CAUSES
1. Fulani Grievances: The Fulani (also called Fula, Fulbe) were a key group. They were
divided into two:
o Town Fulani (Fulani Gida): Educated, urban-dwelling scholars and traders.
They felt politically marginalized.
o Pastoral Fulani (Bororoje): Nomadic cattle herders. They suffered the most
from the Hausa rulers' extortionate cattle taxes and land disputes with settled
farmers.
2. Unjust Enslavement: Rulers were accused of illegally enslaving fellow Muslims for
personal gain, which is strictly forbidden in Islam.
3. Support from the Masses: The Jihads were not just a Fulani movement. They attracted
support from many non-Fulani commoners (Hausa, Nupe, etc.) who were tired of
3
oppression and were drawn to the Jihadists' promise of justice, fair taxation, and
righteous leadership.
1.3 ESTABLISHMENT OF MUSLIM STATES IN WEST AFRICA
The Jihads were incredibly successful, toppling old empires and establishing new, large
theocratic states (states ruled by religious law).
1. Futa Jallon (1725): One of the earliest Jihads, led by Karamokho Alfa and later
Ibrahim Sori in present-day Guinea. It established a Fulani-led state.
2. Futa Toro (1776): Led by Sulayman Bal and Abdul Qadir Kan in present-day Senegal.
They overthrew a traditional dynasty and established an Almamate (state led by an
Almami, or Imam).
3. Sokoto Caliphate (1804): The largest and most influential. Led by Usman dan Fodio
against the Hausa rulers of Gobir, Kano, Katsina, etc. (This was covered in SS2). It
established a vast caliphate that controlled most of Northern Nigeria.
4. Masina (Macina) Empire (1818): Led by Seku Ahmadu (Ahmadu Lobbo), a Fulani
scholar, in the Inner Niger Delta region (present-day Mali). He was inspired by Usman
dan Fodio.
5. Tukolor Empire (1852): Led by Alhaji Umar (Umar Tal), who waged Jihads against
the Bambara and other groups in the Mandinka region (present-day Mali, Guinea,
Senegal).
6. Mandinka Empire (1870s): While not strictly a "Jihad" in the same vein, Samori Toure
used the banner of Islam to unite Mandinka peoples and build a vast empire, which he
defended against the French.

4. Name four (4) new Muslim states established by the Jihads.

WEEK 2: THE JIHADS OF AHMADU SEKU AND ALHAJI UMAR
2.1 THE JIHAD OF SEKUN AHMADU (AHMADU LOBBO) - THE MASINA EMPIRE
A. BACKGROUND AND CAUSES
 Ahmadu Seku (Seku Ahmadu Lobbo) was a Fulani Islamic scholar from the Masina
(Macina) region of the Inner Niger Delta (modern Mali).
 He was a student and a devout follower of Usman dan Fodio. He visited Sokoto and was
deeply inspired by the Jihad in Hausaland.
 Local Causes:
1. Religious: Like in Hausaland, he was angered by the religious syncretism and lax
Islamic practices of the Fulani rulers (Ardo) and the Bambara rulers of Segu who
dominated the region.
2. Political: The Masina region was politically fragmented and subject to the
oppressive rule of the animist Bambara Kingdom of Segu, which demanded
heavy tribute.
3. Social: The Fulani pastoralists in Masina felt oppressed and wanted independence.
B. COURSE OF THE JIHAD
 Around 1818, Seku Ahmadu declared a Jihad.
 He received a flag and blessing from Usman dan Fodio's successor, Muhammadu Bello,
in Sokoto, which gave him legitimacy.
 He gathered a large following of Fulani and other Muslims.
 He first attacked and defeated the local Fulani Ardo, then turned his forces against the
powerful Bambara Kingdom of Segu, defeating them.
 He also captured the key trading city of Timbuktu (in 1826).
 He established a new, centralized theocratic state called the Masina Empire (or the
Caliphate of Hamdullahi), with its capital at Hamdullahi ("Praise be to God").
C. EFFECTS AND NATURE OF THE MASINA EMPIRE
6
1. Theocratic State: The empire was ruled strictly according to Shari'a law.
2. Centralization: Seku Ahmadu replaced the old, fragmented leadership with a strong
central government, led by himself as the Almami (Imam).
3. Social Reforms: He forcibly settled many nomadic Fulani into new towns to promote
agriculture and education.
4. Economic Policy: He regulated trade, markets, and pastoral movements, creating a very
rigid and controlled state.
5. Rigidity: His rule (and that of his successors) became known for its extreme Islamic
puritanism, which was often unpopular.
 The Masina Empire was later conquered by Alhaji Umar Tal in the 1860s.
2.2 THE JIHAD OF ALHAJI UMAR TAL (THE TUKOLOR EMPIRE)
A. BACKGROUND
 Alhaji Umar (Umar Tal) was a Tukolor (a Fulani-related people) scholar from the Futa
Toro region (Senegal).
 He was a brilliant and charismatic leader and a member of the Tijaniyya Islamic
brotherhood (unlike the Sokoto and Masina leaders, who were mostly Qadiriyya).
 He was a well-travelled scholar; he performed the Hajj (pilgrimage) to Mecca, lived in
Sokoto (where he married one of Muhammadu Bello's daughters), and visited the Masina
Empire.
 He became convinced of his own divine mission to spread the Tijaniyya brand of purified
Islam.
B. CAUSES OF THE JIHAD
1. Religious: To purify and spread Islam (specifically the Tijaniyya order) and convert
animist (pagan) populations.
2. Political: He had a personal ambition to create a vast Islamic empire.
7
3. Anti-French: His movement gained an anti-colonial dimension as he came into
increasing conflict with French military expansion from their bases in Senegal. He
wanted to unite West Africa to resist the European threat.
C. COURSE OF THE JIHAD
 Phase 1 (1852): From his base in Dinguiraye (in modern Guinea), Alhaji Umar declared
his Jihad.
 Phase 2 (Conquest of Animist Empires): He first attacked his animist neighbours. He
successfully conquered the Bambara Kingdom of Kaarta (1854) and the much larger
Bambara Kingdom of Segu (1861).
 Phase 3 (Conflict with Masina): This is a controversial part of his career. He came into
conflict with the Masina Empire (which was already Muslim). He accused them of
being too rigid and of aiding his animist enemies. In 1862, he attacked and conquered the
Masina Empire, killing its ruler (Ahmadu III, the grandson of Seku Ahmadu). This act of
a Muslim state attacking another Muslim state was heavily criticized.
 Phase 4 (Conflict with the French): He fought several battles against the French, who
were expanding from Senegal. In 1857, he besieged the French fort at Medina but was
eventually repulsed. The French pressure on his western flank was a constant problem.
 Alhaji Umar was killed in 1864 while fighting a rebellion in Masina.
D. EFFECTS AND LEGACY
1. Creation of the Tukolor Empire: He forged a massive, though short-lived, empire that
stretched across parts of modern-day Guinea, Senegal, and Mali.
2. Spread of Islam: His wars and campaigns led to the widespread conversion (often forced)
of animist populations in the Mandinka region.
3. Spread of the Tijaniyya: He was instrumental in establishing the Tijaniyya brotherhood
as the dominant Islamic order in much of West Africa.
4. Resistance to the French: He was one of the first and most formidable West African
leaders to militarily resist French colonialism.
8
5. Internal Division: His empire was held together by his personal charisma and military
skill. After his death, it was ruled by his son, Ahmadu Seku (not to be confused with
Seku Ahmadu of Masina), but it was weakened by internal rebellions and increasing
French pressure, finally collapsing in the 1890s.

4. Why was Alhaji Umar's conquest of Masina in 1862 controversial?
5. What European power did Alhaji Umar fight against?

WEEK 3: THE ACTIVITIES OF SAMORI TOURE
3.1 HIS RISE TO POWER
 Samori Toure was born around 1830 in the Dyula region of modern-day Guinea. He was
not a Fulani scholar like the other Jihadists. He was a Dyula, a Mandinka-speaking
people famous for their long-distance trading.
 He was born into a pagan (animist) family, though his mother was a Muslim.
 His early life was as a trader. His career changed when his mother was captured in a slave
raid by a local chief. Samori joined the chief's army to win her freedom, where he proved
to be an exceptional soldier and military strategist.
 He left, built his own small band of loyal followers (sofas), and began a career of
conquest.
 He converted to Islam and used the title of "Almami" to unite the fragmented
Mandinka-speaking peoples. He declared a Jihad, which was a powerful tool for statebuilding.
3.2 BUILDING THE MANDINKA EMPIRE
 From the 1870s, Samori Toure used his military genius to build a vast Mandinka
Empire.
 Military Genius:
o Modern Army: He did not rely on traditional levies. He created a professional,
well-drilled, full-time army, divided into units (infantry and cavalry).
o Modern Weapons: He understood the importance of firearms. He traded gold,
ivory, and slaves (a controversial aspect) to the coast (especially Freetown, Sierra
Leone) to buy modern European rifles (breech-loaders and repeaters).
o Gunsmiths: He established workshops with blacksmiths who could repair these
rifles and even manufacture passable copies.
 Administration:
o He divided his empire into provinces, each ruled by a loyal governor.
o He established his capital at Bissandugu.
10
o He promoted Islam, building mosques and Islamic schools, and basing his law on
the Shari'a.
3.3 RESISTANCE AGAINST THE FRENCH
Samori Toure is most famous as a symbol of primary resistance to European colonialism. His
empire-building activities brought him into direct conflict with the French, who were
aggressively expanding from Senegal.
 First War (1882-1886): Samori's forces fought the French effectively. The French were
impressed by his army's discipline. This war ended in a stalemate and a treaty.
 "Scorched Earth" Tactics: As French pressure (and firepower) increased, Samori's
armies could not defeat them in open battle. He adopted a brutal but effective "scorched
earth" policy.
o When the French advanced, Samori's army would evacuate the entire population
of a town or village, burn the town, destroy all food and crops, and poison the
wells.
o The French army would then arrive at a destroyed, empty town with no food or
water, while Samori's cavalry harassed their supply lines.
 Moving the Empire (1891-1898):
o When the French captured his capital, Bissandugu (1891), Samori did something
remarkable: he moved his entire empire.
o He led a mass migration of his people (tens of thousands) eastward, burning the
land behind him, to create a new empire (the "Second Mandinka Empire") in the
upper part of modern-day Ivory Coast.
 Capture and Exile:
o The French, in alliance with the British (who blocked his access to weapons from
Ghana), finally cornered him.
o He was captured in 1898 by a small French force.
o The French, fearing his influence, exiled him to Gabon, where he died in 1900.
3.4 LEGACY
11
1. State-Builder: He was a military and political genius who built one of the largest
indigenous empires in 19th-century West Africa.
2. Primary Resistance Hero: He is celebrated as one of the greatest heroes of West
African resistance to colonial conquest, holding off a superior European army for almost
20 years.
3. National Hero: He is a national hero in Guinea, and his great-grandson, Ahmed Sékou
Touré, became the first president of independent Guinea.
4. Military Innovator: He demonstrated a sophisticated understanding of modern warfare,
firearms, and guerrilla tactics.
5. Use of Islam: He skillfully used Islam as a unifying ideology to build his Mandinka state,
even though he was not a traditional Islamic scholar.

WEEK 4: MUSLIM STATES AND EUROPEAN COLONIZATION
4.1 INTRODUCTION: THE CLASH OF IMPERIALISMS
The 19th century in West Africa was defined by two major imperialist movements that
ultimately clashed:
1. Islamic Imperialism: The expansion of the new, powerful Muslim states (Sokoto,
Tukolor, Mandinka) conquering their neighbours.
2. European Imperialism: The expansion of European powers (Britain, France, Germany)
from the coast into the interior.
By the 1880s, the "Scramble for Africa" was in full swing, and these two forces met. The newlyformed
Muslim states, which were the largest and most organized polities in West Africa,
became the main targets and the main centres of resistance to European colonization.
4.2 EFFECTS OF EUROPEAN EXPANSION ON MUSLIM STATES
1. Military Conquest and Subjugation
The Europeans had superior military technology (especially the Maxim gun), which gave them a
decisive advantage in open battle.
 The Tukolor Empire (France): After Alhaji Umar's death, his son Ahmadu Seku could
not hold the empire together. The French exploited internal rivalries and launched a
"divide and rule" campaign, conquering the empire piece by piece, culminating in the
capture of its capital, Segu, in 1890.
 The Mandinka Empire (France): As seen in Week 3, Samori Toure's empire was
systematically hunted and destroyed by the French, ending with his capture in 1898.
 The Sokoto Caliphate (Britain): The British, using the Royal Niger Company, moved
north from their base in Lokoja. They adopted a "divide and rule" strategy, first making
"treaties" with individual emirs. When the central Caliphate in Sokoto resisted, the
British launched a full-scale military campaign, conquering Bida and Ilorin (1897), Kano
and Katsina (1903), and finally Sokoto itself in 1903 at the Battle of Burmi.
13
2. Artificial Boundaries and Division
The Berlin Conference of 1884-85 formalized the "Scramble for Africa." The European powers
drew lines on a map of Africa with no regard for existing political, ethnic, or religious realities.
 Effect: This had a devastating effect on the Muslim states.
o The Sokoto Caliphate was split between Britain (Northern Nigeria), France
(Niger Republic), and Germany (Northern Cameroon).
o The Tukolor and Mandinka lands were carved up between France (Guinea, Mali,
Ivory Coast) and Britain (Sierra Leone, Ghana).
o This separated peoples, disrupted trade routes, and undermined the cultural and
religious unity of these states.
3. The Policy of "Indirect Rule"
The Europeans (especially the British in Northern Nigeria) did not have the manpower or money
to rule these vast territories directly.
 Policy: They co-opted the existing administrative structure.
 How it worked: The British defeated the Emir (e.g., of Kano), but did not abolish the
title. They appointed a new, more compliant Emir (often from the same ruling family).
 The System: The Emir was allowed to keep his title, his palace, and his authority over
the local population (including the Shari'a courts for civil matters). However, his ultimate
loyalty was now to the British Resident (Adviser), who held the real power.
 Impact:
o Positive (for the British): It was a cheap and effective way to govern.
o Negative: It preserved the form of the Caliphate but destroyed its substance. The
Emir was no longer an independent Muslim ruler but a colonial civil servant. The
Caliph (Sultan of Sokoto) lost all real political power over the other emirs,
retaining only a religious/spiritual authority.
4. Social and Economic Disruption
14
 End of Trans-Saharan Trade: European colonization re-oriented the entire economy of
West Africa. The ancient Trans-Saharan routes (which had sustained the Muslim states)
died, replaced by new railways and roads that all led south to the seaports, to export raw
materials (e.g., groundnuts, cotton) to Europe.
 Abolition of Slavery: The British and French officially abolished slavery and the slave
trade, which was a major (though controversial) part of the economy of the Muslim states.
 Introduction of Western Education: Christian missionaries and the colonial
government introduced Western (Christian) education, which created a new, Westerneducated
elite that would eventually challenge both the traditional Emirs and the colonial
rulers.

WEEK 6: CHRISTIAN MISSIONARY ACTIVITIES IN WEST AFRICA
6.1 ARRIVAL AND SPREAD
While Christianity (Catholicism) had a brief contact with West Africa in the 15th and 16th
centuries (with the Portuguese in Benin and Warri), the major wave of sustained missionary
activity began in the late 18th and 19th centuries.
This new wave was driven by the Evangelical Revival in Europe and America, which sparked a
passionate belief in spreading the gospel, and was closely linked to the Abolitionist Movement
(the campaign to end the slave trade).
 The Sierra Leone Base: The movement began in Sierra Leone. The British founded
Freetown in 1787 as a settlement for freed slaves (known as Creoles or Saros).
 The "Recaptives": After 1807 (when Britain abolished the slave trade), British naval
squadrons patrolled the Atlantic, captured slave ships, and liberated the slaves (recaptives)
in Freetown.
 Missionary Work in Freetown: Missionaries came to Freetown to convert and educate
these recaptives. The most prominent groups were:
o Church Missionary Society (CMS): Anglican.
o Wesleyan Methodist Missionary Society: Methodist.
o The Baptists.
 The "Saros": These educated, converted, and often Westernized Africans (many of
whom were of Yoruba origin) became the vanguard of missionary expansion. They were
eager to return to their "homelands" to spread Christianity and trade.
6.2 SPREAD TO NIGERIA
 Badagry and Abeokuta (1842): The spread to Nigeria began when Yoruba "Saros" (like
Samuel Ajayi Crowther) and missionaries (like Henry Townsend of the CMS and
Thomas Birch Freeman of the Methodists) left Freetown and Badagry to establish a
mission in Abeokuta in 1842.
17
o They were welcomed by the Egba (Yoruba) leaders in Abeokuta, who wanted
them as allies (and for their access to guns) in their wars against Dahomey.
 The Niger Mission:
o The "Bible and the Plough" was the strategy: the idea that legitimate trade and
Christianity should replace the slave trade.
o This led to the Niger Expeditions (1841, 1854, 1857), which were attempts to
travel up the River Niger.
o The 1857 expedition was successful (thanks to the use of quinine to combat
malaria) and led to the establishment of mission stations in the interior, such as
Onitsha (1857), Lokoja, and Gbebe.
o This mission was famously led by Samuel Ajayi Crowther, who became the first
African Anglican Bishop in 1864.
6.3 MAJOR MISSIONARY SOCIETIES
1. Protestant Missions:
o Church Missionary Society (CMS): (Anglican). The most prominent in Nigeria,
focusing on Yorubaland (Abeokuta, Ibadan, Lagos) and the Niger Mission
(Onitsha, Lokoja).
o Wesleyan Methodist Missionary Society: (Methodist). Also active in
Yorubaland (Badagry, Abeokuta).
o The (American) Southern Baptist Convention: Focused on Ogbomosho and
Shaki.
o Church of Scotland Mission (Presbyterian): Led by Mary Slessor, this mission
was famous for its work in Calabar, where it fought against the killing of twins.
2. Roman Catholic Missions:
o The Catholics arrived slightly later than the Protestants in this "second wave."
o The Society of African Missions (SMA) arrived in Lagos in the 1860s and also
established a strong presence in Onitsha, competing with the CMS.

WEEK 8: IMPACT OF CHRISTIAN MISSIONARY ACTIVITIES
The arrival and work of the Christian missionaries had a profound, revolutionary, and permanent
impact on West African society.
8.1 RELIGIOUS IMPACT
1. Introduction of Christianity: The most obvious impact. It led to the conversion of a
significant portion of the population, particularly in the coastal and southern (non-Muslim)
regions.
2. Competition and Division: It introduced a new religion that competed with both
traditional animism and Islam. It also created divisions within Christianity (e.g., Catholic
vs. Protestant rivalry) and within families (converted children vs. traditionalist parents).
3. Abolition of Inhuman Practices: Missionaries were instrumental in campaigning
against and ending practices they deemed "pagan" and "inhuman," such as:
o The killing of twins (led by Mary Slessor in Calabar).
o Human sacrifice.
o Ritual murder.
4. Rise of Independent African Churches: By the late 19th century, many educated
Africans (like the "Saros") became frustrated with the racism and paternalism of
European missionaries who refused to hand over leadership. This led to a "schism" and
the creation of the first Independent African Churches (e.g., the African Baptist Church,
the United Native African Church).
8.2 EDUCATIONAL IMPACT
This is arguably their most significant and lasting legacy.
1. Introduction of Western Education: The missionaries' primary tool for evangelism was
the school. They believed converts must be able to read the Bible.
2. Establishment of Schools: They established the first Western-style schools in Nigeria:
o CMS Grammar School, Lagos (1859): The first secondary school.
o St. Gregory's College, Lagos (Catholic).
21
o Methodist Boys' High School, Lagos.
o Hope Waddell Training Institute, Calabar (1895).
o They also built hundreds of primary schools and teacher-training colleges (e.g., St.
Andrew's College, Oyo).
3. Creation of a Western-Educated Elite: These schools produced a new class of Africans
who were literate in English. This elite became the first clerks, teachers, lawyers, doctors,
and civil servants for the new colonial government.
4. The First Nationalists: Ironically for the colonialists, this same mission-educated elite
(e.g., Herbert Macaulay, Nnamdi Azikiwe, Obafemi Awolowo) would use their Western
education to understand European ideas of "liberty" and "nationalism" and would
eventually lead the fight for independence.
5. Orthography: Missionaries were the first to write down local languages. They translated
the Bible into Yoruba, Igbo, Efik, etc., and in doing so, they created the first written
orthography (alphabets and grammar rules) for these languages.
8.3 SOCIO-POLITICAL IMPACT
1. Legitimate Trade: Missionaries were at the forefront of promoting "legitimate trade" (in
palm oil, cotton, etc.) to replace the slave trade, which had a massive impact on the
coastal economies.
2. Facilitating Colonialism (The "Agents of Imperialism"):
o While many missionaries were well-intentioned, they played an undeniable role in
paving the way for colonial rule.
o They often called on the British government for "protection" from local rulers
(e.g., in Abeokuta).
o Their reports on the "barbarism" and "paganism" of local rulers were used as
justification for British intervention and conquest (e.g., the 1897 Benin
Expedition).
o They acted as translators and advisors for colonial officials and treaty-makers.
3. Introduction of Western Medicine: Missionaries brought Western medicine,
establishing the first hospitals and clinics (e.g., Sacred Heart Hospital, Abeokuta) and
introducing practices like vaccination.
22
4. Cultural Change: They promoted Western culture (e.g., monogamous marriage,
Western dress—the "suit and tie," European names) and actively suppressed many
traditional African cultural practices (e.g., traditional festivals, polygamy), which they
saw as inferior or evil.

WEEK 9: EMPIRES IN WESTERN SUDAN
This is a review/consolidation topic, as these empires (Ghana, Mali, Songhai) are foundational
to West African history and are often studied in SS1/SS2. They provide the deep historical
context for the later Jihads and colonial period.
9.1 THE WESTERN SUDAN
The "Western Sudan" is not the modern country of Sudan. It is a historical and geographical term
for the vast savanna grasslands (called the Sahel) that lie south of the Sahara Desert and north
of the tropical forests. This region was a natural corridor for trade and the birthplace of West
Africa's greatest empires.
9.2 THE EMPIRE OF GHANA (c. 300 - 1076)
 Origin: The first great empire, formed by the Soninke people. Its capital was Kumbi
Saleh.
 Administration (Basis of Power):
1. Trans-Saharan Trade (Economic): Ghana's location was its power. It was the
"middleman." It controlled the gold (which came from the forests to its south) and
the salt (which came from the desert to its north).
2. The King of Ghana taxed this trade. He was called the "Lord of the Gold."
 Decline:
1. The Almoravid Jihad (1076): Ghana was attacked and its capital captured by the
Almoravids, a fanatical Muslim Berber group from North Africa. This invasion
fatally weakened the empire.
2. Loss of control over the gold trade.
3. Internal rebellions by subject states.
9.3 THE EMPIRE OF MALI (c. 1235 - 1500)
 Origin: After Ghana's fall, the Mandinka (or Malinke) people rose to power.
24
 Sundiata Keita (The "Lion King"): The founder of the empire. He was a prince (and a
traditionalist) who defeated the cruel King Sumanguru Kante of the Soso kingdom at the
Battle of Kirina (c. 1235).
 Administration:
1. Trade: Mali took control of the Trans-Saharan trade routes (gold, salt).
2. Islam: Mali's rulers (unlike Ghana's) converted to Islam, which improved
relations with North African traders.
3. Agriculture: Mali also had a strong agricultural base in the fertile lands of the
Niger River.
 Mansa Musa (c. 1312 - 1337):
o Mali's most famous and richest king.
o Hajj (1324-1325): He made a legendary pilgrimage to Mecca, travelling with
thousands of followers and so much gold that he gave it away freely. He spent so
much in Cairo that he caused inflation.
o Legacy: His Hajj put Mali "on the map." European cartographers began drawing
"Mansa Musa" on maps of Africa. He returned with Arab scholars and architects,
who helped make Timbuktu a world-renowned centre of Islamic learning and
trade.
 Decline:
0. Weak and incompetent rulers after Mansa Musa.
1. Internal conflicts (civil wars).
2. Attacks from outside (e.g., by the Tuaregs, who captured Timbuktu).
3. The rise of a new power: Songhai.
9.4 THE EMPIRE OF SONGHAI (c. 1464 - 1591)
 Origin: The Songhai people were based in the city of Gao (on the bend of the River
Niger) and had been a vassal state of Mali.
 Sunni Ali (c. 1464 - 1492):
25
o The military founder of the empire. He was a powerful, ruthless ruler (and a
traditionalist, not a devout Muslim).
o He built a large army and a river navy of canoes to control the Niger River.
o He conquered the key cities of Timbuktu (1468) and Djenne (1473), taking
control of the trade routes.
 Askia Muhammad I (Askia the Great) (c. 1493 - 1528):
o A devout Muslim general who overthrew Sunni Ali's son in a coup.
o He was the great administrator. He consolidated the empire, established a central
bureaucracy, promoted Islam, and (like Mansa Musa) made a famous Hajj to
Mecca.
o He expanded the empire to its largest extent, making it the biggest state in West
African history. Timbuktu flourished as an intellectual centre.
 Decline (The Fall of 1591):
1. The Moroccan Invasion (1591): The "catastrophe." The Sultan of Morocco, Al-
Mansur, sent a professional army (the Arma) across the Sahara Desert.
2. Battle of Tondibi: The large Songhai army, armed with spears and arrows, was
decisively defeated by the smaller Moroccan force, which was armed with
muskets and cannons (gunpowder).
3. Result: The Songhai Empire collapsed. The Moroccans could not rule the vast
territory, and the central authority that had guaranteed safety for trade for
centuries was shattered. The Western Sudan broke apart into many small, warring
states, paving the way for the chaos that the 19th-century Jihadists later sought to
"fix."

WEEK 10: INDUSTRIAL REVOLUTION AND NEW IMPERIALISM
10.1 THE INDUSTRIAL REVOLUTION
 What was it? A period of massive technological and economic change that began in
Great Britain in the late 18th century (c. 1760) and spread to Europe and the USA.
 Key Change: A shift from making goods by hand (in small cottages) to making goods
by machine (in large factories).
 Key Inventions:
o Steam Engine (James Watt): Provided a new source of power for factories,
trains, and ships.
o Textile Machinery (e.g., Spinning Jenny): Mass-produced cloth.
o Railways and Steamships: Revolutionized transportation, making it faster and
cheaper to move heavy goods.
10.2 "NEW IMPERIALISM" (THE SCRAMBLE FOR AFRICA)
"Imperialism" (building an empire) was not new (e.g., the Roman Empire). But the "New
Imperialism" of the late 19th century (c. 1870-1914) was different. It was a frantic, aggressive,
and competitive rush by all the European powers to seize territory, especially in Africa.
10.3 CAUSES OF NEW IMPERIALISM (WHY EUROPE EXPANDED)
The Industrial Revolution is the single most important cause of the New Imperialism.
A. ECONOMIC CAUSES (The Primary Driver)
1. Need for Raw Materials: The new factories in Europe were "hungry." They needed
massive, cheap, and reliable supplies of raw materials, many of which came from Africa.
o Palm Oil: For soap and as an industrial lubricant for machines. (Niger Delta).
o Cotton: For textile factories.
o Rubber: For tires, hoses, and wiring. (Congo).
o Tin, Copper, Gold, Diamonds.
28
2. Need for New Markets: The factories produced too many goods (e.g., textiles, pots,
guns). The European population could not buy them all. European powers needed to find
new, "captive" markets where they could sell their manufactured goods.
o Colonies were perfect. The British could sell their "Made in Manchester" cloth in
Nigeria and India, and ensure that local people could not (or would not) buy from
their rivals (like France or Germany).
3. Need for Investment: Wealthy European bankers and industrialists had excess capital
(money) that they wanted to invest for high profits. Building a railway in Africa (which
would transport raw materials) was seen as a profitable investment.
B. POLITICAL CAUSES
1. Nationalism and Prestige: European nations were in a fierce competition. Owning
colonies became a status symbol.
o Germany (newly unified in 1871) wanted colonies to prove it was a "Great
Power" just like Britain and France.
o France, having lost a war to Germany, wanted colonies to restore its national
pride.
o This led to a "race" – if Britain claimed Nigeria, France would claim Dahomey,
just to stop Britain from getting it. This is why it's called the "Scramble."
C. STRATEGIC CAUSES
1. Protecting Trade Routes: Some colonies were seized not because they were valuable,
but because they were strategically located.
o Britain seized Egypt (1882) primarily to control the Suez Canal, which was the
vital "shortcut" to their most valuable colony, India.
o They then seized other territories (like Sudan) to protect Egypt.
D. SOCIAL AND CULTURAL CAUSES (The "Justification")
These were the racist and pseudo-religious justifications that Europeans told themselves to make
their actions seem noble.
29
1. "The White Man's Burden" (Rudyard Kipling): This was the racist idea that the
"white race" was more advanced and had a moral duty to "civilize" the "backward" and
"savage" peoples of Africa and Asia.
2. Social Darwinism: A twisted application of Darwin's theory of evolution. Europeans
believed they were "fitter" (more evolved) than other races and were therefore "destined"
to rule over them.
3. Missionary Zeal (The 3 'G's):
o Gold: The economic motive.
o Glory: The political motive.
o God: The religious motive. Missionaries (as seen in Week 8) sometimes
supported imperialism, believing that colonial rule would bring "peace" and
"order," allowing them to spread Christianity.
10.4 GLOBAL IMPACT
 Total Colonization of Africa: In 1870, less than 10% of Africa was under European rule.
By 1914, over 90% was colonized. Only Liberia and Ethiopia remained independent.
 End of African Sovereignty: All pre-existing African political structures (empires,
kingdoms) were destroyed or subjugated.
 Creation of Artificial States: The map of modern Africa was drawn by Europeans in
Berlin, lumping rival ethnic groups together and splitting others apart, which has led to
many of Africa's modern-day conflicts.
 Economic Re-orientation: African economies were transformed into "colonial
economies," designed only to export raw materials and import manufactured goods,
creating a dependency that still affects many nations today.

SS3 NIGERIAN HISTORY LESSON NOTES
SECOND TERM
WEEK 1: THE BERLIN CONFERENCE AND PARTITION OF AFRICA
1.1 INTRODUCTION: THE "SCRAMBLE FOR AFRICA"
By the 1880s, European interest in Africa, driven by the economic needs of the Industrial
Revolution (for raw materials and new markets), had reached a fever pitch. European powers
(Britain, France, Portugal, Belgium, Germany) were aggressively claiming territories, often
based on the explorations of figures like H.M. Stanley (working for King Leopold II of Belgium
in the Congo) or the treaties of traders like George Goldie (Royal Niger Company). This frantic,
competitive rush for colonies is known as the "Scramble for Africa."
1.2 CAUSES OF THE BERLIN CONFERENCE (1884-1885)
The Scramble was chaotic and dangerous. Claims overlapped, and by 1884, there was a real
danger that two European powers might go to war against each other over territory in Africa.
1. Congo Crisis: The immediate trigger. King Leopold II of Belgium was claiming the vast
Congo basin as his personal property, which conflicted with French claims (led by
explorer De Brazza) and Portuguese claims.
2. German Ambition: Germany, newly unified (1871), had suddenly "joined the
Scramble," claiming territories in Togo, Cameroon, and South-West Africa (Namibia),
which alarmed Britain and France.
3. Desire for Order: The conference was called by the German Chancellor, Otto von
Bismarck, to create a set of "ground rules" for the partition. The goal was not to initiate
the partition (which had already begun) but to regulate it, ensuring it happened in an
orderly way that would prevent a European war.
1.3 THE BERLIN CONFERENCE (NOVEMBER 1884 - FEBRUARY 1885)
Delegates from 14 nations (all European, plus the USA and the Ottoman Empire) met in Berlin.
33
 Crucial Point: No African rulers were invited. The conference was based on the racist
assumption that Europeans had the right to decide the fate of the African continent.
1.4 MAJOR DECISIONS AND ARTICLES OF THE BERLIN ACT
1. The "Principle of Effective Occupation": This was the most important rule. It stated
that a European power could only claim a part of Africa if it could prove it had
"effectively occupied" the area.
o This meant a power had to have a real presence on the ground:
 Establishing a "Sphere of Influence."
 Raising their flag.
 Building a trading post, military fort, or mission station.
 Having a treaty (however fraudulent) with a local chief.
o Impact: This rule made the Scramble worse and faster. It triggered a "race to the
interior" as European agents rushed to sign treaties and raise flags to prove
"effective occupation" before their rivals.
2. Free Trade in the Congo Basin: The Congo was declared "free" for all nations to trade
in. It was placed under the personal "guardianship" of King Leopold II, creating the
"Congo Free State" (which, in reality, became a brutal private slave colony).
3. Free Navigation: The Niger River (controlled by the British) and the Congo River
(controlled by Leopold) were declared open to "free navigation" by ships of all nations.
4. Abolition of the Slave Trade: All powers publicly agreed to suppress the internal slave
trade (which was a moral justification for their presence).
5. Notification: Any power that claimed a new territory had to formally notify the other
signatory powers.
1.5 CONSEQUENCES OF THE BERLIN CONFERENCE FOR AFRICA
The conference had profound, lasting, and largely devastating consequences.
1. Formalized the Partition: It gave "legal" international sanction to the Scramble for
Africa. It was the "starting gun" for the full-scale conquest of the continent.
34
2. Creation of Artificial Boundaries: The powers drew lines on maps in Berlin that had no
regard for African realities.
o They divided single ethnic groups: The Ewe were split between British Ghana
and German Togo; the Yoruba were split between British Nigeria and French
Dahomey (Benin).
o They lumped together historic rivals: In Nigeria, the Sokoto Caliphate, the
Yoruba kingdoms, and the Igbo societies were all forced into a single new
"country."
o Impact: This is the root cause of many of Africa's modern-day civil wars, ethnic
conflicts, and political instability.
3. Intensified Conquest and Resistance: The "Principle of Effective Occupation" forced
Europeans to conquer and administer territories. This led to a period of violent conquest
and, in response, widespread African resistance (see Week 2).
4. Loss of African Sovereignty: Within 20 years of the conference (by 1914), the entire
continent, except for Ethiopia and Liberia, had lost its independence.
5. Economic Exploitation: The new boundaries and colonial systems were designed for
one purpose: the efficient extraction of raw materials for European industries (see Week
4).

WEEK 2: COLONIAL SUBJUGATION AND AFRICAN REACTION
2.1 INTRODUCTION: PRIMARY RESISTANCE
The European partition of Africa was not a peaceful, administrative process. It was a violent
military conquest. "Effective Occupation" meant war. Africans did not passively accept colonial
rule; they resisted fiercely. This initial, armed resistance to the original conquest is called
"Primary Resistance."
While Africans fought bravely, most primary resistance movements (except one) ultimately
failed.
 Why? Technological Disparity. African armies, fighting with spears, arrows, and old
muzzle-loading guns, were defeated in open battle by European armies (often small)
armed with the Maxim gun (the first true machine gun) and heavy artillery.
2.2 CASE STUDIES OF AFRICAN RESISTANCE
1. SAMORI TOURE (MANDINKA EMPIRE) - West Africa
 Who: As studied in the First Term, Samori Toure was a military genius who had built the
vast Mandinka Empire.
 Enemy: The French.
 Reaction: He waged a brilliant, 18-year war (1880s-1898).
o Modern Army: He had a professional, full-time army equipped with modern
European rifles.
o "Scorched Earth" Tactics: He evacuated and burned his own towns and crops,
starving the French supply lines.
o Moved his Empire: He conducted a mass "fighting retreat," moving his entire
empire eastward to create a new state.
 Outcome: Failure. He was captured in 1898 and exiled. His resistance was the longest
and most sophisticated in West Africa.
2. MENELIK II (ETHIOPIA) - East Africa
36
 Who: The Emperor of Ethiopia, an ancient Christian kingdom.
 Enemy: The Italians.
 Reaction: Menelik II was a brilliant diplomat and modernizer.
o Played Rivals: He exploited European rivalries (French vs. British vs. Italians) to
get them to sell him weapons.
o Modern Army: He built a massive, modern army armed with tens of thousands

3. THE ZULU (SOUTH AFRICA)
 Who: A powerful, centralized, and militaristic kingdom in Southern Africa.
 Enemy: The British.
 Background: The British, wanting to create a "Confederation of South Africa," saw the

WEEK 3: COLONIAL RULE IN AFRICA: SYSTEMS OF ADMINISTRATION
3.1 INTRODUCTION
Once conquest was complete (by c. 1900), the European powers had to govern their new, vast
territories. They did this with a tiny number of European officials, which required a system to
manage the population. The two main systems were Indirect Rule (perfected by the British) and
Direct Rule (used by the French, Belgians, and Portuguese).
3.2 INDIRECT RULE
 Main Proponent: Lord Frederick Lugard (who first applied it in Northern Nigeria,
based on his experience in India).
 Definition: A system of colonial administration where the colonial power (Britain)
governed through existing, pre-colonial traditional rulers and institutions.
 How it Worked (The "Nigerian Model"):
1. The British recognized the Emir (in the North) or the Oba (in the West) as the
"Native Authority."
2. This ruler was allowed to keep his title, his palace, his robes, and his prestige.
3. He was given his own Native Treasury (funded by local taxes), Native Court
(applying "customary law"), and even local police.
4. The Emir was responsible for tax collection, law and order, and recruiting labour
for the colonial government.
5. However, the real power was held by the British "Resident" (or District Officer),
who stood behind the Emir and gave him "advice"—which was, in reality, a
command. The ruler became a paid civil servant of the colonial government.
 Why the British Used It:
1. Cheap: It required very few British officials (just a Resident and his staff), saving
money.
2. Pragmatic: The British (like Lugard) genuinely believed it was more
"acceptable" to the local people and less disruptive.
39
3. Lack of Manpower: Britain did not have enough officials to rule every town and
village directly.
 Success and Failure:
o Success (Northern Nigeria): It worked "perfectly" in the Sokoto Caliphate,
which already had a centralized, hierarchical system (Emirs, District Heads) and
a legal/tax system (Shari'a). The British simply co-opted this existing structure.
o Failure (Eastern Nigeria): It was a disaster when applied to the Igbo and other
societies. These societies were acephalous (lacked central kings). They were
governed by councils of elders and village assemblies.
o The British, not understanding this, looked for "chiefs" where none existed and
appointed "stooges" or "Warrant Chiefs," giving them a "warrant" (a certificate)
to rule. These men were corrupt, oppressive, and hated by the people, leading to
massive unrest (e.g., the Aba Women's Riot of 1929).
3.3 DIRECT RULE
 Main Proponents: France, Portugal, Belgium.
 Definition: A system of colonial administration where the colonial power (France)
governed directly through its own European officials. Pre-colonial rulers and institutions
were largely destroyed or ignored.
 Ideology: "Assimilation" (French):
o The French ideology was that their culture was "superior." The goal of
colonialism was to "civilize" Africans by turning them into "black Frenchmen."
o If an African learned to speak perfect French, converted to Christianity, and
adopted French culture, he could (in theory) become an "Assimilé" and be
granted full French citizenship.
o In reality, this was a myth. By 1936, in all of French West Africa (population 15
million), only about 80,000 were "citizens." The bar was set impossibly high.
 How it Worked:
1. The colony was ruled directly from Paris by the Minister of Colonies.
40
2. The colony was divided into provinces and districts, all run by French officials
(Commandant de Cercle, etc.).
3. Traditional chiefs were not partners. They were demoted to minor civil servants,
often used as tax collectors or labour recruiters. The French would often replace a
traditional chief with a random, Western-educated African.
4. French law was imposed, not customary law.
5. The system was centralized, rigid, and uniform.
 Later Policy: "Association": When Assimilation clearly failed, the French adopted
"Association," which was a more pragmatic policy (similar to Indirect Rule) of
"associating" with local cultures, but it still maintained French superiority.

WEEK 4: COLONIAL ECONOMY AND UNDERDEVELOPMENT
4.1 INTRODUCTION: THE "COLONIAL COMPACT"
The "colonial economy" was not designed to benefit Africa. It was designed to do two things for
Europe:
1. Extract cheap raw materials.
2. Create a "captive market" to sell European manufactured goods.
This entire system, which made Africa dependent on Europe, is the root of modern African
"underdevelopment."
4.2 KEY FEATURES OF THE COLONIAL ECONOMY
1. CASH CROP PRODUCTION
 Colonial governments aggressively promoted (or forced) the production of cash crops
(crops for export, not for food).
 Method:
o Peasant Production (West Africa): In Nigeria and Ghana, the British
encouraged (and sometimes forced) local farmers to grow cash crops on their own
land.
 Nigeria: Palm oil (East), Cocoa (West), Groundnuts (North).
 Ghana: Cocoa.
o Plantation Production (East/Central Africa): In places like Kenya and Congo,
the best land was seized from Africans and given to white settlers, who created
massive "plantations" and forced Africans to work on them.
 Impact:
o "Monoculture" Economy: The entire economy of a country became dependent
on one crop (e.g., Ghana and cocoa). This made them vulnerable to world price
changes.
43
o Decline in Food Production: The best land and all the labour were used for cash
crops, leading to a neglect of food crops. This led to food shortages and the need
to import food (e.g., rice) for the first time.
2. TAXATION
 Purpose: The colonial state had to be self-funding. The British (or French) taxpayer did
not want to pay for ruling Africa.
 How: The colonial government imposed taxes (e.g., Hut Tax, Poll Tax) on all Africans,
payable only in the new colonial currency (e.g., British Shillings, not cowries or
manillas).
 Impact (The "Catch-22"):
o To get the money to pay the tax, an African had no choice but to participate in the
new economy.
o He had two options:
1. Stop growing yams (food) and start growing a cash crop (e.g., cocoa) to
sell.
2. Leave his village to work for a European as a labourer on a railway, in a
mine, or on a plantation.
o Taxation was the primary tool used to create a "labour force."
3. FORCED LABOUR
 When taxes failed to produce enough workers, the colonial states used forced labour
(corvée).
 Villages were ordered to provide men (for no or little pay) to work on "public projects,"
which were almost always for economic extraction.
 Examples: Building railways, roads, or working in mines. The brutal system in King
Leopold's Congo (for rubber) was the most extreme and violent form.
4. INFRASTRUCTURE (ROADS AND RAILWAYS)
 The colonialists built impressive infrastructure (railways, roads, ports).
44
 Purpose: These were not built to "help" Africans or connect African cities. They were
built for one purpose: extraction.
 Pattern: The railway lines in Nigeria and Ghana all run from the interior (where the cash
crops were) directly to a single port city (Lagos, Port Harcourt, Accra) for export. They
were designed to "drain" the wealth of the interior to the coast.
5. TRADE EXPLOITATION (MERCANTILISM)
 Policy: Africans were free to produce cash crops, but they were not free to sell them to
whomever they wanted.
 Monopoly: Large European trading companies (e.g., Royal Niger Company in Nigeria,
UAC) were given monopolies.
 The System:
1. The European companies (who were all in agreement) set a very low, fixed price
for the cash crops they bought from African farmers.
2. The same companies imported European manufactured goods (e.g., cloth,
bicycles, machetes) and sold them to the same farmers at a very high, fixed price.
 Result: The African farmer was trapped. He was forced to sell his goods cheaply and buy
his needs expensively, locking him into a cycle of poverty and dependency.

2. What is a "cash crop"? Name two (2) examples from Nigeria.
3. Explain how taxation was used to force Africans to work for the colonial economy.
4. What was the real purpose of the railways and roads built by the colonial governments?
5. Explain the "sell low, buy high" trade exploitation system used by European companies.

WEEK 6: COLONIAL AFRICA AND THE WORLD WARS
6.1 INTRODUCTION
The two great "World Wars" (WWI: 1914-1918; WWII: 1939-1945) were fundamentally
European conflicts, but they were fought in Africa as well. The wars had a massive and
transformative impact on the continent, shaking the foundations of colonialism and directly
leading to the rise of nationalism.
6.2 AFRICA'S ROLE IN THE WORLD WARS
1. A THEATRE OF WAR
 WWI: As soon as Britain and France declared war on Germany, their colonies were also
at war.
o Conquest of German Colonies: British and French forces in West Africa
immediately invaded and conquered German Togoland and German Cameroon.
o East Africa Campaign: A long, brutal guerrilla war was fought in German East
Africa (Tanzania), where the brilliant German commander, Von Lettow-Vorbeck,
led his African troops (Askaris) against a much larger Allied force for the entire
war.
 WWII:
o North Africa Campaign: The deserts of Egypt, Libya, and Tunisia were a major
battleground between the Allies (British, Americans) and the Axis (Germans,
Italians).
o Ethiopian Campaign: The British, aided by Ethiopian freedom fighters, liberated
Ethiopia from its Italian occupiers (who had invaded in 1935).
2. SUPPLIER OF SOLDIERS
 Africans were recruited (often by force) to fight for their colonial masters.
 WWI: Hundreds of thousands of African soldiers (e.g., the Tirailleurs Sénégalais for
France) were sent to fight in the trenches of Europe.
47
 WWII: This was on an even larger scale. The Royal West African Frontier Force
(RWAFF), with many Nigerian and Ghanaian soldiers, fought brilliantly against the
Japanese in the jungles of Burma (Asia).
3. SUPPLIER OF RESOURCES AND LABOUR
 Colonial economies went into overdrive.
 Forced Production: Africans were forced to produce more cash crops and minerals (e.g.,
tin from Jos) for the war effort.
 Forced Labour: Millions of Africans were conscripted as porters, labourers, and carriers,
often suffering terrible conditions.
6.3 IMPACT OF THE WARS ON AFRICA
The wars were a "double-edged sword" for the colonial powers. They helped them win the war,
but they lost the empire as a result.
1. "The Myth of White Invincibility" was Shattered:
o African soldiers saw white soldiers (Europeans) bleed, die, and surrender.
o In Burma, they saw white British soldiers defeated by a non-white power (Japan).
o In North Africa, they saw white Frenchmen fighting white Germans.
o Impact: The "spell" was broken. The old, racist idea that the white man was a
"god," naturally superior and invincible, was destroyed forever. African soldiers
returned home with a new confidence and a new contempt for their colonial
masters.
2. Economic Hardship:
o The wars caused massive inflation, disruption of trade, and shortages of imported
goods.
o The colonial powers "squeezed" the colonies harder, increasing taxes and forced
production, which made colonialism even more unpopular.
3. The "Atlantic Charter" (1941):
o This was a joint declaration by the leaders of the USA (Roosevelt) and Britain
(Churchill) during WWII.
48
o It stated that one of their war aims was to "respect the right of all peoples to
choose the form of government under which they will live."
o This was a promise of self-determination. While Churchill claimed it only applied
to European nations, African nationalists (like Nnamdi Azikiwe) seized on this
promise and used it to demand independence.
4. Creation of a New "Radicalized" Elite:
o The returning ex-servicemen (veterans) were a new, angry, and radical force.
They were trained in modern warfare, had seen the world, and were promised jobs
and pensions that they never received. They became the "shock troops" of the new
nationalist movements.
o The "urban elite" (workers, teachers) who had suffered economically also became
more radical.
5. Weakening of the Colonial Powers (Britain and France):
o The wars (especially WWII) left Britain and France victorious, but financially and
morally bankrupt. They were too broke and too tired to fight a new round of
colonial wars to hold onto their empires against the rising tide of nationalism.
6. Rise of New Superpowers (USA and USSR):
o The new world leaders were the USA and the Soviet Union. Both were (for
different reasons) anti-colonial and put pressure on Britain and France to grant
independence to their colonies.

WEEK 8: ORIGINS OF NATIONALISM IN AFRICA
8.1 INTRODUCTION
Nationalism is the desire of a people to be free from foreign domination and to rule themselves;
it is the feeling of a shared identity (as "Nigerians" or "Ghanaians") that leads to a demand for an
independent nation-state.
This was a new idea. Before colonialism, people's identities were ethnic (Yoruba, Igbo, Asante),
not "national." Colonialism, ironically, created nationalism by:
1. Lumping all these groups into one "country."
2. Giving them a common enemy (the colonial government).
8.2 FORMS OF NATIONALISM
1. EARLY RESISTANCE (PRIMARY RESISTANCE)
 Who: Traditional rulers and peoples (e.g., Samori Toure, the Zulu).
 Goal: "Restoration." They wanted to drive the Europeans out and restore the old, precolonial
order. They were fighting for the independence of the Mandinka Empire or the
Zulu Kingdom, not "Guinea" or "South Africa."
 Outcome: Failed by 1900.
2. EARLY NATIONALISM (1900s - 1930s)
 Who: The new, Western-educated elite (doctors, lawyers, journalists, "Saros") created by
the missionaries (see SS3 First Term, Week 8).
 Key Organizations:
o National Congress of British West Africa (NCBWA) (1920): Led by
professionals like J.E. Casely Hayford (Ghana) and Akinwande Savage
(Nigeria).
 Goal: "Accommodation" and "Reform."
o They were not demanding independence. They were "cultural nationalists."
52
o They accepted colonial rule but wanted "a seat at the table."
o Their Demands: More representation for Africans in the colonial Legislative
Councils; an end to racism and discrimination in the civil service; more schools
and universities; not "Africa for the Africans," but "a British West Africa."
 Tactics: Gentle, "aristocratic" methods: writing newspaper articles, sending petitions to
London, holding polite meetings.
 Outcome: They were largely ignored by the colonial government.
3. RADICAL / MASS NATIONALISM (1940s - 1960s)
 Who: A new, more radical generation (the "post-war" generation).
o The "Young Men": (e.g., Nnamdi Azikiwe, Kwame Nkrumah). Often USeducated,
more radical, and impatient.
o The Masses: They were joined by the angry ex-servicemen (WWII veterans),
urban workers (e.g., trade unions), farmers (angry at low cash crop prices), and
market women.
 Goal: "INDEPENDENCE NOW!"
o They were not asking for reform; they were demanding a total end to colonialism.
 Tactics: "Positive Action."
o They rejected the "gentle" tactics of the NCBWA.
o They used "mass" tactics: Mass political parties (NCNC, CPP), fiery newspaper
articles (Zik's West African Pilot), strikes, boycotts, and mass rallies.
 Outcome: Succeeded in winning independence for most of Africa between 1957 and
1963.
8.3 FACTORS THAT GAVE RISE TO NATIONALISM
1. Colonial Oppression (The "Common Enemy"): Economic exploitation (tax, forced
labour), social discrimination (racism), and political oppression (lack of a voice) gave
everyone a reason to unite against the colonial government.
53
2. Western Education: Mission schools created an elite that could read, write, and
understand European ideas of "Democracy," "Liberty," and "Nationalism." They
used the "master's tools" against him.
3. The Impact of the World Wars: (See Week 6). Shattered the "white myth," created
angry veterans, and provided the "Atlantic Charter."
4. The "African" Press: Newspapers (like Zik's West African Pilot and Nkrumah's Accra
Evening News) were crucial for spreading nationalist ideas to the masses.
5. Rise of Trade Unions: Colonialism created a new "working class" (e.g., railway workers,
miners). They organized into unions to demand better pay. The 1945 General Strike in
Nigeria (led by Michael Imoudu) was a key nationalist event, paralyzing the colonial
economy.
6. External Influences: (See Week 10). Pan-Africanism, the UN, etc.

WEEK 9: PATTERNS OF NATIONALIST MOVEMENTS AND INDEPENDENCE
9.1 INTRODUCTION
Not all African countries gained independence in the same way. The "pattern" of decolonization
depended on one, single factor: Was there a large, powerful population of white settlers?
 Pattern 1: No Settlers (West Africa):
o Examples: Nigeria, Ghana.
o Process: Relatively peaceful, constitutional, and political.
o Reason: There was no large European population demanding to stay. The British
could "hand over" power to the nationalist elite.
 Pattern 2: Large Settler Population (East/Southern Africa):
o Examples: Kenya, Algeria, South Africa, Zimbabwe.
o Process: Violent, protracted, and led to "Wars of Liberation."
o Reason: The white settlers (who owned all the best land and ran the government)
refused to give up power, leading to armed struggle.
9.2 CASE STUDY: GHANA (GOLD COAST)
 Pattern: Peaceful, "No Settler" model.
 Key Nationalist: Kwame Nkrumah.
 The Story:
1. Early Nationalism: The NCBWA (led by Casely Hayford) was active, but seen
as too slow.
2. UGCC (1947): The "United Gold Coast Convention" was formed by moderate,
elite nationalists (like J.B. Danquah) as a reformist party.
3. Nkrumah's Arrival: They invited Kwame Nkrumah (who had been in the USA
and UK, and was highly radical) to be their General Secretary.
4. Accra Riots (1948): Ex-servicemen rioted over inflation. The British panicked
and arrested Nkrumah and Danquah ("The Big Six"). This made Nkrumah a
national hero.
55
5. The Split (1949): Nkrumah found the UGCC too "elitist" and slow. He broke
away and formed the Convention People's Party (CPP).
 Slogan: "Self-Government NOW!"
 Base: He appealed to the "masses" (the youth, workers, market women).
6. "Positive Action" (1950): Nkrumah launched a nationwide campaign of strikes
and boycotts. The British arrested him and put him in jail.
7. Election (1951): While still in prison, Nkrumah's CPP won a landslide victory in
the election. The British Governor (Arden-Clarke) had no choice: he released
Nkrumah from jail and made him "Leader of Government Business" (and later
Prime Minister).
8. Independence (1957): After 6 years of "self-government," Ghana became the
first sub-Saharan African country to gain independence on March 6, 1957.
9.3 CASE STUDY: NIGERIA
 Pattern: Peaceful, "No Settler" model, but complicated by ethnic divisions and
regionalism.
 Key Nationalists: Nnamdi Azikiwe (Zik), Obafemi Awolowo (Awo), Ahmadu Bello
(Sardauna).
 The Story:
1. Early Nationalism: Herbert Macaulay, NCBWA.
2. NCNC (1944): The National Council of Nigeria and the Cameroons (later, ...of
Nigerian Citizens). Founded by Herbert Macaulay and Nnamdi Azikiwe. This
was the first "national" party, and Zik (with his West African Pilot newspaper)
was a pan-Nigeria hero.
3. Constitutional Era: The British (under Governors Richards and Macpherson)
tried to write constitutions that would unite the country.
4. The "Tragedy" of Nigerian Nationalism: Instead of a single, united movement
(like Ghana's CPP), Nigerian nationalism split along ethnic lines.
 NCNC: Became seen as an Igbo-dominated party (East).
 Action Group (AG) (1951): Formed by Obafemi Awolowo as a Yorubadominated
party (West).
56
 Northern People's Congress (NPC) (1951): Formed by Ahmadu Bello
as a Hausa-Fulani-dominated party (North). The North feared that
independence would mean domination by the more-educated South.
5. Independence (1960): The three regional parties (which were often fighting each
other) finally managed to agree on a federal constitution (which gave a lot of
power to the regions) and formed a coalition government (NPC + NCNC).
6. Nigeria gained independence on October 1, 1960, as a "compromise" between
three regional blocs, not as a single, unified nation (which would lead to problems
later).
9.4 CASE STUDY: KENYA
 Pattern: Violent, "White Settler" model.
 The Situation: Kenya had a large, rich, and powerful white settler population (less than
1%) who owned all the best farmland (the "White Highlands") while the native Kikuyu
people were forced onto "reserves" or lived as poor "squatters."
 The Story:
1. Early Nationalism: The Kikuyu Central Association (KCA), formed in the
1920s and later led by Jomo Kenyatta, peacefully petitioned for the return of
their "stolen lands." They were ignored for 30 years.
2. Radicalization (1952): By the 1950s, the Kikuyu had lost all hope in peaceful
methods. A secret, radical, armed group formed, which the British called the
"Mau Mau."
3. "Mau Mau" War (1952-1960):
 This was a brutal guerrilla war (a "war of liberation").
 The Mau Mau (mostly Kikuyu) attacked white settlers and, more often,
"loyalist" (pro-British) Africans.
 The British declared a "State of Emergency," arrested Kenyatta (who they
falsely claimed was the leader), and waged a counter-insurgency. They
rounded up over 1.5 million Kikuyu into "detention camps" (concentration
camps) where torture and abuse were widespread.
4. Outcome:
57
 Military Defeat: The British militarily defeated the Mau Mau fighters in
the forest.
 Political Victory: The war politically broke the will of the British
government. It was too expensive, too bloody, and too embarrassing.
 The British government realized that multi-racial settler rule was
impossible. They abandoned the white settlers, released Kenyatta from
prison, and negotiated independence.
5. Independence (1963): Kenya became independent with Jomo Kenyatta as its
first leader. They had to fight for their freedom.

WEEK 10: EXTERNAL INFLUENCES ON AFRICAN NATIONALISM
10.1 INTRODUCTION
The rise of nationalism was an internal African phenomenon, but it was powerfully aided and
influenced by ideas and events from outside the continent.
10.2 PAN-AFRICANISM
 Definition: An intellectual and political movement based on the idea that all people of
African descent (both on the continent and in the Diaspora - USA, Caribbean) share
a common heritage, a common destiny, and a common struggle against racism and
colonialism.
 Motto: "Africa for the Africans."
 Early Leaders (The "Diaspora"): The early leaders were not in Africa; they were in the
USA and the Caribbean.
o W.E.B. Du Bois (USA): An African-American sociologist. He organized the
early Pan-African Congresses (1919, 1921, etc.) where Black leaders met to
demand rights.
o Marcus Garvey (Jamaica/USA): A charismatic leader who founded the
"Universal Negro Improvement Association" (UNIA). He preached "Black
Pride" and a "Back to Africa" movement.
 The 5th Pan-African Congress (Manchester, 1945):
o This was the most important one. It took place right after WWII.
o Crucial Change: For the first time, African leaders from the continent (like
Kwame Nkrumah (Ghana), Jomo Kenyatta (Kenya), Nnamdi Azikiwe
(Nigeria)) were at the centre.
o New Goal: The old congresses asked for "rights." The 1945 Congress (inspired
by Nkrumah) declared a new, radical goal: total independence for Africa, using
"all means necessary," including strikes and boycotts.
59
o Impact: This congress was the "planning meeting" for the radical nationalism
that freed Africa. The leaders went home from this meeting and started the parties
(CPP, etc.) that won independence.
10.3 THE UNITED NATIONS (UNO)
 Formation: Founded in 1945 after WWII to ensure global peace.
 Impact:
1. Anti-Colonial: The UN Charter (like the Atlantic Charter) promoted selfdetermination.
2. A "Platform" for Nationalists: The UN gave African nationalists a global stage.
They could travel to the UN in New York and petition the "Trusteeship
Council" and the "Committee on Decolonization," embarrassing their colonial
masters in front of the whole world.
3. Pressure: The growing "Afro-Asian Bloc" (led by India) in the UN General
Assembly passed resolutions that put constant diplomatic pressure on Britain and
France.
10.4 IMPACT OF THE WORLD WARS
 (As covered in Week 6).
 Shattered white invincibility.
 Gave nationalists the "Atlantic Charter" as a legal/moral argument.
 Weakened the colonial powers (Britain/France).
 Raised up new anti-colonial superpowers (USA/USSR).
10.5 THE ORGANIZATION OF AFRICAN UNITY (OAU)
 Formation: Founded in Addis Ababa (Ethiopia) in 1963.
 Role: The OAU was formed after most countries were independent, but it played a
crucial role in helping the last ones.
 The "Liberation Committee": The OAU provided money, weapons, training, and
diplomatic support to the "Liberation Movements" fighting violent settler wars in:
60
o Angola and Mozambique (fighting Portugal).
o Zimbabwe (fighting Ian Smith's white settler regime).
o South Africa (fighting Apartheid).
 The OAU helped to keep the "frontline states" (like Zambia, Tanzania) afloat as they
supported these wars.
10.6 GLOBAL SOLIDARITY (USA & USSR)
The "Cold War" (c. 1947-1991) between the USA and the USSR also helped nationalism.
 The USA (Capitalist): Was officially anti-colonial (it was their "first colony"). They put
pressure on Britain/France to decolonize, fearing that if they didn't, the nationalists would
turn to communism.
 The USSR (Communist): Was aggressively anti-colonial. They gave money, weapons,
and training to the most radical liberation movements (like in Angola and Mozambique)
to turn them into communist allies.
 Impact: African nationalists were able to play the two superpowers against each other to
get support.

SS3 NIGERIAN HISTORY LESSON NOTES
THIRD TERM
WEEK 1: PRELUDE TO APARTHEID
1.1 HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Apartheid ("apartness" in Afrikaans) was not an invention of 1948. It was the legal culmination
of over 300 years of racial segregation and white minority rule in South Africa.
 1652: Dutch Arrival: The Dutch East India Company established a small refreshment
station at the Cape of Good Hope, led by Jan van Riebeeck. These settlers became
known as Boers (Dutch for "farmers") and later, Afrikaners.
 Boer-Calvinist Ideology: The Boers were strict Calvinist Protestants. They developed a
deep-seated belief that they were God's "chosen people" in Africa, and that their
superiority over the indigenous Khoikhoi, San, and Bantu-speaking peoples was
divinely ordained.
 1806: British Takeover: Britain seized control of the Cape Colony during the
Napoleonic Wars.
 Conflict of Cultures: The British and the Boers clashed. The British, influenced by the
abolitionist movement, (1) abolished the slave trade in 1807, (2) abolished slavery itself
in 1834, and (3) promoted a (limited) degree of non-racialism.
 The Great Trek (1830s-1840s): In response, thousands of deeply conservative Boers
(Voortrekkers) embarked on a mass migration away from British control into the interior.
They defeated indigenous groups (like the Zulu at the Battle of Blood River, 1838) and
established their own independent Boer Republics: the Transvaal and the Orange Free
State.
 Discovery of Minerals:
o Diamonds (Kimberley, 1867): This sparked a massive rush of European miners
and capital, led by figures like Cecil Rhodes.
o Gold (Witwatersrand, 1886): This transformed the Transvaal from a poor,
agrarian republic into the richest state in Southern Africa.
64
 South African War (Boer War, 1899-1902): The British, seeking to control the gold,
went to war with the Boer republics. The British won the war, but in the peace treaty,
they made a fatal compromise to appease the defeated Afrikaners: they promised that the
rights of Black Africans would not be considered until after South Africa was given selfgovernment.
 The Union of South Africa (1910): Britain granted independence to the Union of South
Africa, effectively handing over total political power to the white minority (both British
and Afrikaner).
 Early Segregation Laws: The new white-minority government immediately passed
segregationist laws:
o Natives' Land Act (1913): This was the original sin of Apartheid. It reserved
93% of South Africa's land for whites (who were less than 20% of the population)
and restricted all Black Africans to just 7% of the land in "reserves."
1.2 CAUSES AND IMPLEMENTATION
 Afrikaner Nationalism: The Afrikaners felt defeated and humiliated by the British after
the Boer War. They developed a strong, defensive, and racist nationalism.
 The "Swaart Gevaar" (Black Peril): After WWII, many Black Africans moved to the
cities for industrial jobs. This "urban influx" frightened poor, working-class whites, who
feared losing their jobs and "racial purity."
 The Election of 1948: The National Party (NP), led by D.F. Malan, ran a political
campaign based only on the idea of "Apartheid" as the solution to the "Black Peril." They
won a surprise victory, took power, and immediately began to build their system.
 Implementation: Apartheid was implemented as a total, all-encompassing legal
framework. It was not just segregation; it was a system of total social, political, and
economic engineering.

1. Who were the Boers/Afrikaners, and what was their core religious belief?

WEEK 2: APARTHEID LEGISLATION AND SUPPRESSION
2.1 THE PILLARS OF APARTHEID LEGISLATION
The National Party government passed a series of laws to control every aspect of life. These laws
can be divided into "Petty Apartheid" and "Grand Apartheid."
A. PETTY APARTHEID (Social Segregation)
These laws were designed to ensure complete social separation and to humiliate non-whites.
1. Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act (1949): Made it illegal for whites to marry people
of other races.
2. Immorality Act (1950): Made sexual relations between whites and non-whites a crime.
3. Reservation of Separate Amenities Act (1953): Legalized "separate but equal" (which
meant separate and grossly unequal) facilities: parks, beaches, buses, toilets, park
benches, etc., were all segregated.
B. GRAND APARTHEID (Political and Territorial Segregation)
These laws were the core of the system, designed to make Black South Africans foreigners in
their own country.
1. Population Registration Act (1950): This was the "foundation" of the system. It
classified every single person in South Africa into one of four racial categories: White,
Coloured (mixed-race), Indian (Asian), or Bantu (Black African).
2. Group Areas Act (1950): This law designated specific urban areas for each racial group.
It was used to forcibly destroy vibrant, non-racial neighbourhoods (like Sophiatown in
Johannesburg) and move millions of non-whites to segregated townships (like Soweto -
SOuth WEstern TOwnships).
3. Pass Laws: These laws required all Black men (and later women) to carry a "pass book"
(dompas) at all times. This book contained their racial ID, employment status, and
permission to be in a "white" area. Failure to produce the pass on demand meant
immediate arrest.
67
4. Bantu Authorities Act (1951) & Promotion of Bantu Self-Government Act (1959):
This was the "master plan" of Grand Apartheid.
o It divided the Black population into 10 distinct "tribal" groups (e.g., Zulu, Xhosa,
Tswana).
o It declared that the 13% of land set aside in 1913 would be their "Homelands" or
"Bantustans."
o The government's stated goal was that these Bantustans would eventually become
"independent countries."
o The Real Goal: To strip all Black South Africans of their citizenship. A Xhosa
person, even if they were born in Soweto and had never seen the "homeland,"
would be declared a "citizen" of the Transkei. They could then be treated as a
foreign migrant worker in the "white" 87% of South Africa, with no rights at all.
5. Bantu Education Act (1953): This law put all Black education under state control. Its
architect, Hendrik Verwoerd, stated that its purpose was to teach Black children that
they were "hewers of wood and drawers of water" and that they had no place in "white"
society above the level of "certain forms of labour."
2.2 RESISTANCE MOVEMENTS
Resistance to white rule was old, but Apartheid galvanized it.
1. The African National Congress (ANC): Founded in 1912, it was initially a moderate,
"elite" group that used petitions. By the 1950s, its Youth League (led by Nelson
Mandela, Oliver Tambo, Walter Sisulu) pushed the party in a more radical direction.
2. The Defiance Campaign (1952): The ANC and Indian Congress (SAIC) launched a
non-violent campaign of mass civil disobedience. Tens of thousands of volunteers
courted arrest by deliberately breaking Apartheid laws (e.g., entering "whites-only" areas).
3. The Freedom Charter (1955): A "Congress of the People" (ANC, SAIC, and
white/coloured allies) met in Kliptown and adopted the Freedom Charter, a visionary
document declaring that "South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white."
4. Pan-Africanist Congress (PAC): Formed in 1959 by Robert Sobukwe, who broke
away from the ANC. The PAC was "Africanist"—they rejected the ANC's multi68
racialism ("South Africa belongs to all...") and argued for "Africa for the Africans" (a
Black-only struggle).
5. The Sharpeville Massacre (1960): This was the major turning point. The PAC
organized a mass protest against the Pass Laws. In the township of Sharpeville, police
opened fire on an unarmed, peaceful crowd, killing 69 people (many shot in the back as
they fled).
6. The Shift to Armed Struggle:
o After Sharpeville, the government declared a state of emergency and banned the
ANC and PAC.
o Non-violent protest was now impossible.
o The ANC formed a military wing: Umkhonto we Sizwe (MK) ("Spear of the
Nation"), led by Mandela.
o The PAC formed a military wing: Poqo.
7. Rivonia Trial (1963-64): Mandela and other MK leaders were arrested at a farmhouse in
Rivonia. They were tried for treason. Mandela gave a famous speech from the dock,
ending, "It is an ideal for which I am prepared to die." He and the others were sentenced
to life imprisonment on Robben Island.
8. Later Resistance:
o Steve Biko & the Black Consciousness Movement (BCM) (1970s): A new
movement focused on psychological liberation, teaching Black people to
overcome their "slave mentality" and embrace "Black is Beautiful." Biko was
murdered in police custody in 1977.
o Soweto Uprising (1976): A mass protest by schoolchildren against the forced use
of the Afrikaans language in schools. Police opened fire, killing hundreds of
children (starting with 13-year-old Hector Pieterson). This re-ignited the global
anti-apartheid movement.

WEEK 3: THE OAU (AU) AND THE FIGHT AGAINST APARTHEID
3.1 OAU'S STANCE
The Organization of African Unity (OAU) was founded in Addis Ababa in 1963. The
liberation of all parts of Africa from colonialism and white-minority rule was a core principle of
its charter. Apartheid South Africa was seen as the "final boss" of African colonialism.
3.2 ROLE OF THE OAU
The OAU's fight against Apartheid was multi-faceted, involving diplomatic, military, and
economic support for the liberation movements.
1. The OAU Liberation Committee:
o This was the OAU's most practical tool. It was based in Dar es Salaam,
Tanzania.
o Its sole purpose was to coordinate the anti-colonial struggle.
o Funding: OAU member states contributed (in theory) to a special fund to support
liberation movements.
o Military Support: The committee provided weapons, military training, and
safe bases for the ANC and PAC.
o Diplomatic Recognition: The OAU recognized the ANC and PAC as the
legitimate "liberation movements," giving them observer status at OAU meetings.
2. The "Frontline States":
o These were the OAU-member states that shared a border with (or were close to)
South Africa: Tanzania, Zambia, Botswana, Angola, Mozambique, and (after
1980) Zimbabwe.
o These countries paid the highest price for the struggle.
o Role: They hosted ANC/PAC military bases and refugee camps.
o South Africa's Retaliation: South Africa waged a "total strategy" of
destabilization against them, including:
 Funding rebel groups (e.g., RENAMO in Mozambique, UNITA in
Angola).
71
 Bombing raids and assassinations in their capitals.
 Economic sabotage.
o The OAU's role was to provide financial and political support to these states to
help them withstand the pressure.
3. Diplomatic and Economic Isolation:
o The OAU (led by countries like Nigeria and Zambia) led the global campaign to
isolate South Africa.
o At the UN: The "Africa Bloc" at the UN General Assembly relentlessly pushed
for resolutions condemning Apartheid, leading to South Africa being suspended
from the General Assembly.
o Sanctions: The OAU pushed for full, mandatory economic and military
sanctions. This was difficult (often vetoed by the USA and UK in the Security
Council), but they succeeded in getting a mandatory arms embargo in 1977.
o At the Commonwealth: African members (led by Nigeria and Tanzania's Julius
Nyerere) forced South Africa to withdraw from the Commonwealth in 1961.
o Sports Boycotts: This was a highly effective OAU-led strategy. They organized a
boycott of South Africa from the Olympic Games (from 1964) and international
rugby and cricket. This deeply hurt the sports-obsessed white population.
4. International Organizations:
o United Nations (UN): Declared Apartheid a "crime against humanity."
o International Court of Justice (ICJ): Ruled that South Africa's occupation of
Namibia (South-West Africa) was illegal.
o This international pressure, coordinated by the OAU, combined with internal
resistance (Soweto, strikes) and the high cost of its wars, finally forced the white
government to the negotiating table in the late 1980s. Nelson Mandela was
released in 1990, and the first democratic, multi-racial election was held in 1994.

WEEK 4: NEO-COLONIALISM IN AFRICA
4.1 DEFINITION
Neo-colonialism ("new colonialism") is a term famously defined by Ghana's first president,
Kwame Nkrumah, in his 1965 book, Neo-Colonialism: The Last Stage of Imperialism.
It describes the post-independence situation where an African country is politically independent
(it has its own flag, national anthem, and UN seat) but remains economically and politically
dependent on, and controlled by, its former colonial master or other foreign powers.
In essence, it is colonialism in disguise—economic control without direct political rule.
4.2 POLITICAL EFFECTS (HOW IT WORKS)
1. "Puppet" Leaders: Foreign powers (like France, Britain, USA, or USSR during the
Cold War) interfere in local politics to ensure that "friendly" (i.e., compliant) leaders are
in power. They prop up these leaders with aid and military support, even if they are
corrupt dictators.
2. Foreign Military Bases: The former colonial power retains military bases in the
"independent" country, not to protect the country, but to protect its own economic
interests. (e.g., France has had a long-standing military presence in West Africa).
3. Political Destabilization: If an African leader tries to be truly independent (e.g., by
nationalizing foreign-owned mines or banks), the neo-colonial powers will act to
overthrow them.
o Classic Example: Patrice Lumumba, the first Prime Minister of Congo, was
assassinated in 1961 with the support of Belgium and the CIA. He was replaced
by the corrupt, pro-Western dictator Mobutu Sese Seko, who ruled (and looted)
the country for 32 years.
4. "Balkanization": The deliberate policy of keeping African states small, weak, and nonunited
to make them easier to control.
4.3 ECONOMIC EFFECTS (THE CORE OF NEO-COLONIALISM)
74
1. Domination by Multinational Corporations (MNCs): The independent country's
economy is still owned by foreign companies (e.g., Shell, Total, ExxonMobil in oil; De
Beers in diamonds; Unilever in consumer goods).
o These MNCs extract the raw materials (oil, cocoa, copper) and take the profits
back to Europe/America, leaving little wealth behind.
2. Continued Monoculture: The colonial economy (see SS3 Second Term, Week 4) is not
changed. The country is still forced to depend on exporting one or two cheap raw
materials, making it vulnerable to world price changes.
3. Unfavourable Terms of Trade: The "sell low, buy high" system continues. African
countries are forced to sell their raw materials (e.g., raw cocoa) at very low prices but
must buy expensive manufactured goods (e.g., chocolate, cars, phones) from the same
foreign powers.
4. Tied Foreign "Aid": Foreign powers give "aid" (loans or grants) that comes with
conditions. For example, "We will give you $100 million, but you must use it to buy our
country's tractors and hire our country's engineers." This makes the "aid" just a subsidy
for the donor's own economy.
5. Debt Trap & Structural Adjustment Programmes (SAPs):
o In the 1970s/80s, African countries were encouraged to take huge loans from the
World Bank and International Monetary Fund (IMF) (both controlled by
Western powers).
o When they could not repay, the IMF/World Bank forced them to accept
Structural Adjustment Programmes (SAPs).
o SAPs forced governments to:
 Devalue their currency (making imports expensive).
 End subsidies on food and fuel (hurting the poor).
 Privatize state-owned companies (selling them to foreign corporations).
 Cut spending on education and healthcare.
o These SAPs were seen as the ultimate tool of neo-colonialism, destroying Africa's
public services and re-asserting foreign control over their national budgets.

WEEK 6: POLITICAL AND ECONOMIC PROBLEMS OF INDEPENDENT AFRICAN
STATES
6.1 INTRODUCTION
The "Uhuru" (freedom) that came with independence in the 1960s brought great hope. However,
this hope was quickly confronted by a massive set of new problems. These problems were a
direct legacy of colonialism, but were also worsened by the failures of Africa's new ruling elite.
6.2 POLITICAL PROBLEMS
1. National Unity and Tribalism (Ethnicity):
o Problem: The "artificial borders" from the Berlin Conference lumped rival ethnic
groups into one "nation" (e.g., Nigeria).
o Result: People's loyalty was (and often still is) to their ethnic group (e.g.,
Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa) before their nation (Nigeria).
o Consequences:
 Politics: Political parties formed along ethnic lines (e.g., NPC-North, AGWest,
NCNC-East in Nigeria's First Republic).
 Conflict: Competition for jobs, resources, and census figures became a
"zero-sum game" between tribes.
 Civil War: The ultimate result (e.g., Nigerian Civil War 1967-70;
Rwanda Genocide 1994).
2. Corruption and Poor Leadership:
o Problem: The new African elite who took over from the colonialists often
adopted their masters' high-consumption lifestyles.
o "Kleptocracy" (Rule by Thieves): Many leaders (like Mobutu Sese Seko of
Zaire/Congo) saw the state treasury as their personal bank account.
o Patronage/Clientelism: Leaders stay in power by distributing state resources
(jobs, contracts) to their personal supporters and ethnic kinsmen, rather than on
merit. This is the root of most corruption.
3. Political Instability (Coups):
78
o Problem: When governments failed (due to corruption and tribalism), the
military (see Week 8) saw itself as the only "national" institution capable of
"saving" the nation.
o Result: A "plague" of coups d'état (military takeovers) swept the continent,
starting in the 1960s. This led to a cycle of instability (coup -> counter-coup).
4. "Sit-Tight" Leaders (One-Party States):
o Problem: Many independence leaders (e.g., Nkrumah in Ghana, Nyerere in
Tanzania) argued that multi-party democracy was a "Western luxury" that Africa
could not afford, as it promoted tribalism.
o "Solution": They created one-party states, arguing it would foster unity.
o Result: The one-party state quickly became a dictatorship. Without opposition,
leaders became "presidents-for-life" (e.g., Robert Mugabe in Zimbabwe, Paul
Biya in Cameroon), jailing opponents and crushing dissent.
6.3 ECONOMIC PROBLEMS
1. Poverty and Mass Illiteracy:
o At independence, the vast majority of the population was poor, rural, and illiterate.
o There was a massive skills shortage. Nigeria, at independence in 1960, had only
about 150 doctors and 100 lawyers in a population of over 50 million.
2. Economic Dependence (Monoculture):
o The colonial economic structure (see Week 4) was not changed.
o Nigeria remained dependent on agriculture (palm oil, cocoa), and then, after the
1970s, on crude oil.
o Zambia remained dependent on copper. Ghana on cocoa.
o Result: When the world price of that one commodity crashed, the entire national
economy collapsed.
3. The Debt Crisis:
o In the 1970s (especially after the "oil boom"), African nations took huge loans
from Western banks for big "white elephant" projects (new capitals, steel mills,
national airlines).
o When oil/commodity prices crashed in the 1980s, they could not repay.
79
o Result: This led to the IMF/World Bank Structural Adjustment Programmes
(SAPs), which forced governments to cut education and health, causing massive
suffering.
4. Unequal Development:
o Rural-Urban Gap: Development was focused only on the capital cities (Lagos,
Nairobi), while the rural areas (where 80% of people lived) were neglected.
o Result: Mass migration of youth from the villages to overcrowded city slums
(e.g., Makoko in Lagos), creating unemployment and crime.

WEEK 8: THE MILITARY INTERVENTION IN AFRICAN POLITICS
8.1 INTRODUCTION
A coup d'état (French for "stroke of state") is the illegal and forceful seizure of power from a
government, carried out by its own armed forces (the military). Between 1960 and 2000, Sub-
Saharan Africa experienced over 80 successful coups and countless failed attempts. This
"plague" of military intervention was the single biggest cause of political instability in postindependence
Africa.
8.2 CAUSES OF MILITARY INTERVENTION
Soldiers did not seize power in a vacuum. They intervened for a complex mix of reasons, some
patriotic, some personal.
1. Failure of Civilian Politicians: This is the most common excuse given by coup plotters.
o Corruption: The massive, open corruption of the First Republic politicians (e.g.,
in Nigeria and Ghana).
o Election Rigging: Widespread "rigging" of elections (e.g., Nigeria's 1964/65
elections) made the civilian government lose all legitimacy.
o Tribalism and Violence: Politicians fanning the flames of ethnic hatred (e.g., the
"Operation Wetie" violence in Western Nigeria).
o The army presented itself as a "neutral" and "patriotic" force, stepping in to "clean
up the mess."
2. Personal Ambition of Soldiers:
o The "Man on Horseback" syndrome. Ambitious, charismatic, and often powerhungry
officers saw the government as weak and believed they could do a better
job.
o Example: Idi Amin of Uganda (1971), a semi-literate army commander who
overthrew Milton Obote to become one of Africa's most brutal dictators.
3. Contagion Effect:
o Coups were "contagious." When one country had a coup, it gave soldiers in the
next country the idea.
82
o Example: The first Sub-Saharan coup in Togo (1963) (where Gnassingbé
Eyadéma killed Sylvanus Olympio) set a dangerous precedent. This was followed
by coups in Nigeria (1966), Ghana (1966), etc.
4. External (Cold War) Interference:
o During the Cold War (USA vs. USSR), both sides backed "their" clients in
African armies.
o If a civilian leader leaned "East" (communist), the West (e.g., CIA) might support
a "pro-Western" general to overthrow him (e.g., the overthrow of Nkrumah in
Ghana).
5. Internal Army Grievances (Corporate Interests):
o Sometimes, coups happened for purely "selfish" army reasons: soldiers were
angry about low pay, poor barracks, or ethnic-based promotions.
o Example: The 1966 July Counter-Coup in Nigeria was partly driven by
Northern soldiers seeking revenge for the killing of Northern officers in the
January coup.
8.3 EXAMPLES
 Nigeria (1966): The January Coup (led by Nzeogwu) overthrew the corrupt First
Republic. This was followed by the July Counter-Coup (led by Northern soldiers),
which brought Yakubu Gowon to power and set the stage for the Civil War.
 Ghana (1966): General Kotoka overthrew Kwame Nkrumah (who had become a
dictator), while Nkrumah was on a state visit to China.
 Uganda (1971): Idi Amin overthrew Milton Obote, beginning an 8-year "reign of
terror."
8.4 CONSEQUENCES OF MILITARY RULE
Military rule, far from "saving" the nation, was almost always a disaster.
1. End of Democracy and Human Rights:
o The first act of any coup is to suspend the constitution.
o All democratic institutions are banned (parliament, political parties).
83
o Civil liberties are abolished (freedom of speech, press, assembly).
o Critics are jailed, tortured, or killed. (e.g., Ken Saro-Wiwa under the Abacha
regime in Nigeria).
2. Economic Mismanagement:
o Soldiers are trained to fight, not to run an economy.
o They ruled by decree, were not accountable to anyone, and were often more
corrupt than the civilians they overthrew (e.g., the Abacha regime).
o They wasted money on "prestige" military hardware.
3. Loss of Military Professionalism:
o The army became "politicized." Soldiers became businessmen and politicians.
o Promotions were based on "loyalty" to the dictator, not on military skill.
o This led to a cycle of more coups (counter-coups) as rival factions in the army
fought each other for power (e.g., Nigeria's Babangida/Buhari/Abacha era).

WEEK 9: BOUNDARY DISPUTES AND AFRICAN UNITY
9.1 ROOT CAUSE: THE LEGACY OF BERLIN (1884-85)
The single root cause of almost all boundary disputes in Africa is the Berlin Conference. The
arbitrary, artificial borders drawn by Europeans:
 Ignored geography (e.g., drawing a straight line through a mountain range or desert).
 Ignored ethnic realities (e.g., splitting the Ewe, Somali, and Yoruba peoples).
 Lumped rivals together.
At independence, Africa inherited these illogical borders.
9.2 THE OAU'S CHARTER ON BORDERS (THE CAIRO DECLARATION, 1964)
In 1964, the OAU made a historic and painful decision. They adopted the principle of Uti
possidetis, ita possideatis (Latin: "as you possess, so may you possess").
 The Principle: This meant that all OAU members agreed to respect the colonial
borders as they existed at the moment of independence.
 The Reason (The "Pandora's Box"): African leaders (like Nyerere of Tanzania) wisely
argued that the borders were "absurd," but that trying to "fix" one border (e.g., to unite all
Somali people) would open a "Pandora's Box." It would give every country a reason to
invade its neighbour, leading to hundreds of wars and the total disintegration of the
continent.
 The Result: The OAU chose peace and stability over "ethnic justice." This principle has
been (mostly) successful in preventing large-scale inter-state wars.
9.3 CASE STUDY I: NIGERIA-CAMEROON (THE BAKASSI PENINSULA)
 Dispute: A dispute over the Bakassi Peninsula, a resource-rich (oil and fish)
swamp/peninsula on the border. The dispute was based on confusing, contradictory
treaties between Britain and Germany in the colonial era.
85
 Conflict: Nigeria (under military rule) occupied the peninsula in the 1980s and 1990s,
leading to military skirmishes.
 The Solution (A MAJOR SUCCESS):
1. No War: Instead of a full-scale war, Cameroon took Nigeria to the
International Court of Justice (ICJ) (the UN's world court) in 1994.
2. The ICJ Ruling (2002): The ICJ studied the old colonial treaties and ruled in
favour of Cameroon.
3. The Handover: The world watched to see if Nigeria (the "giant") would obey.
Nigeria's democratic president, Olusegun Obasanjo, in a major act of
statesmanship and respect for the rule of law, agreed to the ruling.
4. The Greentree Agreement (2006): Nigeria formally and peacefully handed over
the Bakassi Peninsula to Cameroon.
 Significance: This is a model example of how African boundary disputes should be
solved: using international law and diplomacy, not war.
9.4 CASE STUDY II: ETHIOPIA-ERITREA
 Dispute: Eritrea was an Italian colony, while Ethiopia was independent. In 1952, the UN
federated Eritrea with Ethiopia. In 1962, Emperor Haile Selassie annexed Eritrea,
sparking a 30-year war of independence.
 Independence: Eritrea won the war and became independent in 1993.
 The Problem: The "colonial-era" border between them was still poorly defined.
 The War (1998-2000) (A MAJOR FAILURE):
1. A dispute over the small, obscure border town of Badme escalated into a fullscale,
bloody, high-tech war.
2. It was a "World War I-style" trench war, costing tens of thousands of lives and
billions of dollars.
 Significance: This was a total failure of the OAU's principle. It was a tragic and
pointless war between two former allies, over the exact kind of colonial border dispute
the OAU charter was designed to prevent. (Peace was only formally declared in 2018).

WEEK 10: GLOBAL INSTITUTIONS AND AFRICA
10.1 INTRODUCTION
No African nation operates in a vacuum. Their development, politics, and security are all deeply
interconnected with a web of regional and global institutions.
10.2 REGIONAL INSTITUTIONS (AFRICAN)
1. The African Union (AU):
o Formation: Replaced the OAU in 2002.
o Goal: To be a more powerful, effective, and integrated organization, modeled on
the European Union (EU).
o Key Difference from OAU:
 OAU policy: "Non-interference" (A dictator like Idi Amin could kill his
own people, and the OAU could not stop him).
 AU policy: "Non-indifference" (The AU's Constitutive Act gives it the
right to intervene in a member state in cases of genocide, war crimes, or
mass human rights abuses).
o Institutions: Pan-African Parliament, African Court of Justice, African Standby
Force (a continental peacekeeping army).
2. ECOWAS (Economic Community of West African States):
o Formation: 1975, through the efforts of Nigeria (Gowon) and Togo (Eyadéma).
o Primary Goal: Economic integration (a "common market") and free movement
of people (the ECOWAS passport).
o Main Achievement: ECOMOG (ECOWAS Ceasefire Monitoring Group). This
is the region's military/peacekeeping wing.
o ECOMOG's Successes: ECOMOG (led by Nigeria) successfully intervened to:
 Stop the civil war in Liberia (1990s).
 Stop the civil war in Sierra Leone (1990s).
 Force out the dictator Yahya Jammeh in The Gambia (2017) after he
refused to accept election results.
88
o ECOWAS is considered one of Africa's most effective regional blocs.
10.3 GLOBAL INSTITUTIONS
1. The Commonwealth:
o Who: A voluntary association of 50+ countries, almost all of which are former
British colonies.
o Role: Promotes democracy, human rights, and good governance. Provides
educational opportunities (e.g., Commonwealth Scholarships).
o Power (Its "Teeth"): It can suspend member states that violate its democratic
principles.
o Examples: Nigeria was suspended (1995-1999) after the Sani Abacha regime
executed Ken Saro-Wiwa. Zimbabwe was suspended (and later withdrew) under
Robert Mugabe.
2. The United Nations (UNO):
o Role: The ultimate global body for peace, security, and development.
o UN Peacekeeping: Deploys "Blue Helmet" soldiers to maintain ceasefires in
African conflicts (e.g., DR Congo, Somalia, Liberia, Sudan).
o UN Agencies: These have a massive on-the-ground presence in Africa:
 WHO (World Health Organization): Fights diseases (e.g., Ebola,
Malaria).
 UNICEF (UN Children's Fund): Runs child health, vaccination, and
education programs.
 WFP (World Food Programme): Provides food aid during famines and
to refugees.
 UNESCO: Protects cultural heritage sites.
3. OPEC (Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries):
o Who: A "cartel" (a group of producers who collude) of the world's major oilexporting
countries.
o African Members: Nigeria, Algeria, Libya, Gabon, Congo, Angola, Equatorial
Guinea.
89
o Role: To try and control the global price of oil by setting production quotas for
its members.
o Impact: When OPEC is successful (like the 1973 Oil Embargo), it brings massive
wealth (the "oil boom") to its members. When it fails (or when members cheat on
quotas), the price of oil crashes, devastating their economies.

WEEK 11: AFRICANS IN THE DIASPORA AND GLOBAL CONTRIBUTIONS
11.1 DEFINING THE "AFRICAN DIASPORA"
The African Diaspora refers to the communities of people of African descent living outside the
continent of Africa. This group is generally divided into:
1. The "Old" Diaspora: Descendants of people taken from Africa during the Trans-
Atlantic Slave Trade. This includes communities in the United States, Brazil, the
Caribbean (Jamaica, Haiti, etc.), and parts of Latin America.
2. The "New" Diaspora: People who have migrated from Africa in the 20th and 21st
centuries for professional, economic, or educational reasons (e.g., a Nigerian doctor
working in the UK).
11.2 ROLE AND CONTRIBUTIONS OF THE DIASPORA
A. INSPIRING AFRICAN INDEPENDENCE
The intellectual and political foundations of 20th-century African independence were laid in the
Diaspora.
1. Pan-Africanism: This movement (see SS3 Second Term, Week 10) was founded by
Diaspora intellectuals.
o W.E.B. Du Bois (USA): An American sociologist who organized the early Pan-
African Congresses.
o Marcus Garvey (Jamaica): A charismatic leader who preached "Black Pride"
and "Africa for the Africans" through his UNIA.
2. Re-exportation to Africa: These ideas were "re-exported" to Africa by the very leaders
who would win independence.
o Kwame Nkrumah (Ghana) and Nnamdi Azikiwe (Nigeria) were educated in the
USA, where they were deeply involved in the Pan-African and Black civil rights
movements.
o Jomo Kenyatta (Kenya) was active in Pan-African circles in London.
91
o The 5th Pan-African Congress (Manchester, 1945), where these Africans met
with Diaspora leaders, was the "planning meeting" for the independence
movements.
B. THE CIVIL RIGHTS MOVEMENT AND ITS IMPACT
 The US Civil Rights Movement (1950s-60s), led by figures like Dr. Martin Luther
King Jr., and the Black Power movement (Malcolm X) had a two-way relationship with
Africa.
 Impact on Africa: The success of the Civil Rights movement inspired and energized
Africans, especially those fighting Apartheid (e.g., Steve Biko's Black Consciousness
Movement was directly inspired by "Black Power").
 Impact on the USA: The new, independent African nations (Ghana, Nigeria) in the UN
provided a powerful symbol of Black success that inspired African-Americans.
C. LITERARY AND CULTURAL MOVEMENTS
 The Harlem Renaissance (1920s, USA): A flowering of Black art, music (Jazz), and
literature (e.g., Langston Hughes) that asserted Black cultural pride.
 Négritude (1930s, Paris): A literary movement founded by French-speaking Black
intellectuals from Africa and the Caribbean.
o Leaders: Léopold Sédar Senghor (Senegal's first president), Aimé Césaire
(Martinique), Léon Damas (French Guiana).
o Goal: To reject French "Assimilation" and to celebrate the "Black soul,"
traditional African values, and a shared Black identity.
D. GLOBAL CONTRIBUTIONS (INDIVIDUALS)
People of African descent (both from the continent and the Diaspora) have had a monumental
impact on world history and culture.
 Politics & Diplomacy:
o Kofi Annan (Ghana): 7th Secretary-General of the UN (Nobel Peace Prize).
92
o Barack Obama (USA/Kenya): First Black President of the United States.
o Andrew Young (USA): US Ambassador to the UN, prominent in the antiapartheid
fight.
 Arts & Literature:
o Toni Morrison (USA) & Wole Soyinka (Nigeria): Nobel Prize winners in
Literature.
o Bob Marley (Jamaica): A global cultural icon whose music of liberation inspired
people from Zimbabwe to South Africa.
 Science & Academia:
o Cheikh Anta Diop (Senegal): A historian and scientist who argued for the
Black/African origins of Egyptian civilization.
 Sports & Culture:
o Muhammad Ali (USA): A global symbol of resistance, Black pride, and
religious conviction (his "Rumble in the Jungle" fight in Zaire/Congo was a key
Pan-African cultural event).
o Pelé (Brazil): The first global Black sports superstar.
11.3 MODERN IMPACT (THE "NEW DIASPORA")
 Economic: The "New Diaspora" sends billions of dollars in remittances back home,
often far exceeding foreign aid.
 "Brain Drain" vs. "Brain Gain": While Africa suffers a "brain drain" (its best
doctors/engineers leave), these professionals often form networks that send skills,
technology, and investment back to the continent.
