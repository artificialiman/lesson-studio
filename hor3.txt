Lesson Content:
1. What is Agriculture?
Agriculture is the science, art, and business of cultivating the soil, producing crops, and
raising livestock (animals) for human use. It is the backbone of human survival, providing
us with food, clothing, and shelter.
Agriculture is often divided into branches:
 Crop Production (which we study)
 Animal Husbandry/Production
 Fisheries
 Forestry
2. Crop Production vs. Horticulture
 A. Crop Production (Agronomy):
o Definition: This is the large-scale cultivation of field crops (like maize, rice,
cassava, yams, and cowpea) for food, animal feed, or industrial raw materials.
o Focus: It focuses on quantity, efficiency, and large-scale operations
(hectares of land).
 B. Horticulture:
o Definition: This is a specialized branch of crop production that deals with the
intensive cultivation of specific plants.
3
o Focus: It is often done on a smaller, more detailed scale and focuses on
quality, aesthetics, and high-value crops.
o Branches of Horticulture:
1. Pomology: The science of growing fruits (e.g., oranges, mangoes,
pineapples).
2. Olericulture: The science of growing vegetables (e.g., tomatoes,
peppers, okra, cabbage).
3. Floriculture: The art and science of growing flowers and ornamental
plants (e.g., hibiscus, roses, grasses for lawns).
3. Classification of Crops (By Use)
We can classify the crops we grow based on what we use them for:
1. Cereals: Grown for their edible grains. They are rich in carbohydrates (energy).
o Examples: Maize (corn), Rice, Sorghum, Millet.
2. Legumes (or Pulses): Grown for their seeds (in pods). They are rich in protein. They
also add nitrogen to the soil.
o Examples: Cowpea (beans), Groundnut, Soybean.
3. Tubers: Grown for their swollen underground "stems." Rich in carbohydrates.
o Examples: Yam, Irish Potato.
4. Roots: Grown for their swollen underground "roots." Rich in carbohydrates.
o Examples: Cassava, Sweet Potato, Carrot.
5. Vegetables: Grown for their leaves, stems, or fruits, providing vitamins and minerals.
o Examples: Tomato, Pepper, Okra, Onions, Cabbage, Amaranthus (green).
6. Fruits: Grown for their sweet, fleshy parts. Rich in vitamins.
o Examples: Orange, Mango, Pawpaw, Pineapple, Banana.
7. Spices: Used in small quantities to add flavour and aroma to food.
o Examples: Ginger, Garlic, Turmeric.
4. Basic Farm Tools and Implements
4
1. Cutlass: A long, flat blade used for clearing bushes and light vegetation. Also used
for harvesting some crops (e.g., sugar cane).
2. Hoe: A metal blade attached to a wooden handle, used for weeding, making
ridges/heaps, and tilling the soil.
3. Rake: A tool with "teeth" used for levelling the soil after ploughing and for
gathering leaves and debris.
4. Shovel/Spade: Used for lifting and moving loose soil, sand, or manure.
5. Watering Can: A container with a spout used for applying water (irrigating) small
plots, nurseries, and gardens.
6. Wheelbarrow: A single-wheeled cart used for transporting small loads (like fertilizer,
manure, or harvested crops) around the farm.
5. Farm Operations
These are the activities a farmer carries out on the farm.
 A. Pre-Planting Operations (Before Planting):
o Clearing: Removing bushes and trees (using a cutlass).
o Stumping: Removing tree stumps (roots).
o Ploughing/Tilling: Breaking up and loosening the soil (using a hoe or tractor).
o Harrowing: Breaking up large soil clumps and levelling the field (using a rake).
o Making Ridges/Heaps: Gathering the topsoil into mounds for planting tubers.
 B. Planting Operations:
o The act of putting seeds or seedlings into the soil.
 C. Post-Planting Operations (After Planting):
o Weeding: Removing unwanted plants (weeds) that compete with crops.
o Fertilizing: Applying manure or fertilizer to add nutrients.
o Irrigation: Applying water to the crops (e.g., with a watering can).
o Pest/Disease Control: Protecting crops from harm.
o Harvesting: Gathering the mature crops from the field.

WEEK 2: SOIL MANAGEMENT
Topic: Soil Management
Breakdown (Subtopics):
 Erosion control methods
 Crop rotation
 Cover cropping
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "Soil Management" and state its importance in crop production.
7
2. Define "Soil Erosion" and identify its primary causes.
3. List and explain three methods of controlling soil erosion.
4. Define "Crop Rotation" and explain two of its major benefits.
5. Define "Cover Cropping" and explain its main functions.
Lesson Content:
Soil management refers to all the practices and operations used to protect the soil from
damage (like erosion) and to improve its quality (its fertility) for successful crop production.
Good soil is the most important asset for a farmer, and managing it well ensures good
harvests year after year.
1. Soil Erosion
 Definition: Soil erosion is the gradual washing or blowing away of the rich, fertile
topsoil from a piece of land.
 Agents (Causes):
o Water Erosion: Caused by the impact of rain and the flow of running water,
especially on slopes.
o Wind Erosion: Caused by strong winds blowing across large, flat, bare fields,
lifting the dry soil particles.
 Problem: When the topsoil is lost, the land loses its most fertile part (which contains
humus and nutrients), making it very difficult for crops to grow. This leads to poor
yield and food scarcity.
2. Erosion Control Methods
These are practical methods farmers use to prevent soil erosion.
 A. Terracing:
o Function: This method is used on steep slopes and hillsides.
o How it works (Efficient): The farmer cuts a series of flat, level "steps" (called
terraces) into the slope. When it rains, the water is held by each step, allowing
8
it to soak into the soil slowly instead of rushing down the hill and washing the
soil away.
 B. Contour Ploughing (Contour Farming):
o Function: This is used on gentle slopes.
o How it works (Efficient): The farmer ploughs and plants across the slope,
following the natural contours (curves) of the land, rather than ploughing up
and down.
o Benefit: The small ridges created by ploughing act like tiny dams, trapping
rainwater and preventing it from flowing downhill quickly.
 C. Strip Cropping:
o Function: A method of planting different crops in alternate, narrow strips.
o How it works: A strip of a "row crop" (like maize or yam, which has lots of
bare soil) is planted next to a strip of a "cover crop" (like grass or beans, which
covers the soil densely). The dense cover crop slows down wind and water,
trapping any soil that erodes from the row crop strip.
 D. Planting Windbreaks (Shelterbelts):
o Function: Used primarily to prevent wind erosion.
o How it works: Farmers plant one or more rows of tall trees or shrubs along
the edges of their fields. These trees act as a "wall" to block the wind, reducing
its speed and preventing it from lifting the soil.
3. Crop Rotation
 Definition: Crop rotation is the practice of planting different types of crops in the
same field in a planned sequence, season after season or year after year. It is the
opposite of planting the same crop (monocropping) every single time.
 Example:
o Year 1: Plant Maize (a "heavy feeder" that uses a lot of nitrogen).
o Year 2: Plant Beans (a legume that adds nitrogen back into the soil).
o Year 3: Plant Yam (a "deep feeder" that has deep roots and benefits from the
improved soil structure).
9
 Benefits (Why it is important):
1. Pest and Disease Control: Many pests and diseases attack only one type of
plant (e.g., a yam pest). By changing the crop to beans, the yam pest's food
source is removed, and it starves, breaking its life cycle.
2. Improved Soil Fertility: Different crops use different nutrients. Legumes (like
beans, groundnuts, cowpea) have special nodules on their roots that "fix"
nitrogen from the air and add it to the soil, acting as a natural fertilizer for the
next crop.
3. Improved Soil Structure: Different crops have different root systems (some
deep, some shallow), which helps to break up the soil at different levels and
improves water infiltration.
4. Cover Cropping
 Definition: Cover cropping is the practice of planting crops not for harvest, but
specifically to cover and protect the soil when the main cash crops are not growing
(e.g., during the dry season or off-season).
 Function (Why it is used):
1. Erosion Control: The cover crop (e.g., grasses, legumes like mucuna or vetch)
forms a "mat" or "canopy" over the land. This "green blanket" protects the
bare soil from being hit by rain or blown by wind.
2. Weed Suppression: The cover crop grows quickly and "chokes out" or
smothers unwanted weeds by competing with them for sunlight and space.
3. Builds Soil Fertility (Green Manure): When it is time to plant the main crop,
the farmer can plough the cover crop back into the soil. As it rots, it adds
valuable organic matter (humus), making the soil richer.

WEEK 3: CROP PLANTING TECHNIQUES
Topic: Crop Planting Techniques
Breakdown (Subtopics):
 Advanced planting methods
 Spacing and depth
 Related operations (Thinning and Supplying)
12
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "planting" and list four common planting methods.
2. Differentiate between broadcasting, drilling, and dibbling.
3. Define "planting depth" and explain why it is important for seed germination.
4. Define "plant spacing" and state two reasons for correct spacing.
5. Explain the terms "Thinning" and "Supplying" as post-planting operations.
Lesson Content:
Planting (or sowing) is the act of putting seeds, or young plants (seedlings), into the soil so
they can grow. The method used depends on the crop, the tools available, and the size of the
farm.
1. Planting Methods
 A. Broadcasting:
o Definition: The method of scattering seeds randomly by hand over the entire
surface of the prepared field.
o How it works: The farmer takes seeds in his hand and throws them to cover
the land. The seeds are then lightly covered with soil using a rake.
o Crops: Rice (in paddy fields), many grasses, and some vegetables (like carrot).
o Pros: Very fast, requires no special equipment.
o Cons: Wasteful (birds eat seeds), leads to uneven spacing and competition.
 B. Drilling:
o Definition: The method of placing seeds in continuous furrows (shallow
trenches) at a uniform depth.
o How it works: A farmer (or a machine called a drill) opens a long, straight
row, drops the seeds in, and covers them.
o Crops: Maize, Wheat, Rice (upland), Sorghum, many vegetables.
o Pros: More uniform, less seed waste, easy to weed between rows.
13
 C. Dibbling:
o Definition: The method of planting seeds in specific holes (at specific
distances) made in the soil.
o How it works: A farmer uses a small tool (a dibbler) or a cutlass to make a
hole, drops 1-3 seeds in, and covers it. This is planting "at stake."
o Crops: Maize, Beans (Cowpea), Yam, Cassava (cuttings).
o Pros: Very accurate spacing, very little seed waste.
o Cons: Slow and labour-intensive.
 D. Transplanting:
o Definition: This is a two-stage method. Seeds are first planted in a small,
protected "nursery." When the young plants (seedlings) are strong enough,
they are carefully uprooted and moved to the main field.
o Crops: Tomato, Pepper, Onions, Cabbage, Rice (irrigated).
o Pros: Protects young plants, ensures only strong seedlings are planted, gives a
"head start."
2. Planting Depth
 Definition: Planting depth is how deep a seed is placed in the soil.
 Importance: This is critical for germination.
o If too shallow: The seed can be eaten by birds or insects, or it can dry out in
the sun.
o If too deep: The seed has a small amount of stored food. It may use up all its
energy trying to reach the surface and die before it sees sunlight.
 General Rule of Thumb: A seed should be planted at a depth of 2-3 times its own
diameter. (Small seeds = shallow, Large seeds = deeper).
3. Plant Spacing
 Definition: Plant spacing is the distance between plants in a row, and the distance
between the rows themselves (e.g., 90cm x 30cm for maize).
 Importance of Correct Spacing:
14
1. Reduces Competition: When plants are too close, they "fight" each other for
limited resources (sunlight, water, and soil nutrients). This leads to weak
plants and low yield.
2. Prevents Spread of Disease: Good spacing allows air to circulate freely
around the plants, which keeps the leaves dry and makes it harder for fungal
diseases to spread.
3. Ease of Operation: Proper rows make it easier for the farmer to walk, weed,
and apply fertilizer.
4. Related Post-Planting Operations
 A. Thinning:
o Definition: The process of removing excess, weak, or overcrowded seedlings
from the field, leaving only the strongest ones at the correct spacing.
o When: This is done after germination, when you plant 2-3 seeds per hole and
all of them grow.
 B. Supplying:
o Definition: The process of re-planting seeds or seedlings in the spots where
the original ones failed to germinate (failed to grow).
o Why: This ensures the farmer has a full, uniform "plant population" and no
gaps in the field, maximizing the yield.

5. What is "Thinning"? Why is it necessary?

WEEK 4: FERTILIZER MANAGEMENT
Topic: Fertilizer Management
Breakdown (Subtopics):
 Organic composting
 Fertilizer scheduling and application methods
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "fertilizer" and "manure."
17
2. Differentiate between organic and inorganic (chemical) fertilizers, giving an example
of each.
3. Define "composting" and list three materials needed to make a compost heap.
4. Explain the process of making compost.
5. Define "fertilizer scheduling" and describe two methods of fertilizer application.
Lesson Content:
Plants, like humans, need food (nutrients) to grow strong and healthy. The main nutrients
they get from the soil are Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), and Potassium (K). When the soil
is "poor" and lacks these nutrients, the farmer must add them using fertilizers.
1. Types of Fertilizers
 A. Organic Fertilizers (Manures):
o Definition: These are fertilizers made from natural, living (or once-living)
sources. They are bulky and release nutrients slowly.
o Examples:
 Farmyard Manure: A mix of animal dung (cow, goat) and urine with
straw/bedding.
 Poultry Manure: Droppings from chickens, turkeys, etc. (Very rich in
Nitrogen).
 Compost: Decomposed organic matter (see below).
 Green Manure: Cover crops that are ploughed into the soil to
decompose (see Week 2).
o Pros: Improves soil structure (helps it hold water), builds humus, slow-release
(won't burn plants), cheap.
o Cons: Bulky and difficult to transport, may contain weed seeds or diseases.
 B. Inorganic (Chemical) Fertilizers:
o Definition: These are man-made chemicals produced in a factory. They
provide specific nutrients in a concentrated form.
o Examples:
18
 NPK 15:15:15: A "complete" fertilizer that contains 15% Nitrogen,
15% Phosphorus, and 15% Potassium.
 Urea: Provides only Nitrogen (N).
 Single Superphosphate: Provides only Phosphorus (P).
o Pros: Concentrated (easy to carry), fast-acting, nutrient content is known
exactly.
o Cons: Expensive, can "burn" plants if over-applied, can make soil acidic, can
wash away (leach) and pollute water.
2. Organic Composting
 Definition: Composting is a natural process where micro-organisms (like bacteria
and fungi) break down organic materials (like leaves and kitchen scraps) into a rich,
dark, soil-like substance called compost or humus.
 Materials Needed (The "Recipe"):
1. "Greens" (Nitrogen-rich): Wet materials like vegetable scraps, fruit peels,
fresh grass clippings.
2. "Browns" (Carbon-rich): Dry materials like dry leaves, paper, cardboard,
wood chips.
3. Water: The heap should be moist, like a damp sponge (not soaking wet).
4. Air (Oxygen): The microbes need to breathe.
5. A little soil: To introduce the microbes.
 Process (The Heap Method):
1. Start: Begin with a bottom layer of "browns" (twigs) for air circulation.
2. Layer: Add alternating layers of "greens" and "browns."
3. Moisten: Sprinkle with water as you build, but don't soak it.
4. Turn: Use a pitchfork to "turn" or mix the pile every 1-2 weeks. This adds
oxygen, which speeds up decomposition and prevents bad smells.
5. Finish: In a few months, the pile will shrink and turn into dark, crumbly,
sweet-smelling compost, ready to be added to the farm.
3. Fertilizer Scheduling and Application
19
 A. Fertilizer Scheduling (WHEN to apply):
o This means applying fertilizer at the time the plant needs it most.
o At Planting (Basal Application): Fertilizer (especially Phosphorus for roots)
is applied in the hole or row before or during planting.
o Top Dressing: This is applying fertilizer (especially Nitrogen for leaf growth)
to the surface of the soil after the plants have already started growing. This is
often done 3-6 weeks after planting.
 B. Application Methods (HOW to apply):
1. Broadcasting: Spreading the fertilizer particles evenly over the entire field.
This is used for crops like rice or lawns.
2. Ring Method: Applying the fertilizer in a circle (a "ring") around the base of a
plant, but away from the stem to avoid burning it. This is used for individual
plants like fruit trees (orange, mango) or tomatoes.
3. Side/Band Placement: Applying the fertilizer in a "band" (a continuous line)
in a furrow beside the row of crops. This is very common for maize.
4. Foliar Application: Dissolving the fertilizer in water and spraying it on the
leaves of the plant. This is a special method used for giving plants a quick
"boost."

WEEK 6: CROP IRRIGATION
Topic: Crop Irrigation
Breakdown (Subtopics):
 Meaning and methods of irrigation
 Importance and maintenance
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "irrigation" and "drainage."
2. State three reasons why irrigation is important in crop production.
22
3. List two traditional (surface) methods of irrigation.
4. Describe two modern methods of irrigation (sprinkler and drip).
5. Explain one basic way to maintain an irrigation system.
Lesson Content:
Water is essential for plants. They need it for photosynthesis, to absorb nutrients, and to
stay cool. In many places, rainfall is not enough or does not come at the right time.
1. Meaning of Irrigation and Drainage
 A. Irrigation:
o Definition: Irrigation is the artificial application of water to farmland to
supply moisture for crops.
o Purpose: It is done to help crops grow, especially during the dry season or in
areas with low rainfall (arid regions).
 B. Drainage:
o Definition: Drainage is the opposite of irrigation. It is the removal of excess
water (waterlogging) from farmland.
o Purpose: Too much water in the soil blocks oxygen and can kill plant roots.
Drainage (e.g., digging channels or gutters) is needed to remove this standing
water.
2. Importance of Irrigation
1. All-Year-Round Farming: With irrigation, farmers are no longer dependent on rain.
They can plant and harvest crops even during the dry season, leading to more food
and income.
2. Increases Crop Yield: Plants that get the right amount of water at the right time will
be healthier, grow faster, and produce a much bigger harvest.
3. Farming in Arid Regions: Irrigation makes it possible to farm in deserts and other
dry lands that would normally be barren.
4. Cools the Soil: Water can help to cool the soil and the plant on very hot days.
23
3. Methods of Irrigation
 A. Traditional / Surface Methods (Less Efficient):
1. Watering Can Method: Using a watering can to apply water by hand.
 Use: Very small gardens, nurseries, and vegetable beds.
2. Flood / Furrow Irrigation: Water is brought to the field and allowed to flow
over the surface or down furrows (channels) between crop ridges.
 Pros: Very cheap, requires no special equipment.
 Cons: Very wasteful (a lot of water is lost to evaporation and runoff),
can cause soil erosion.
 B. Modern / Localized Methods (More Efficient): 3. Sprinkler Irrigation: * How
it works: Water is pumped through pipes and sprayed into the air from rotating
"sprinkler heads." The water falls on the crops like natural rain. * Pros: Covers a large
area, good for most crops (like maize, vegetables, lawns). * Cons: Can be expensive,
some water is lost to wind and evaporation before it hits the ground. 4. Drip /
Trickle Irrigation: * How it works: A network of plastic pipes with tiny holes
(called "emitters") is laid on the ground along the plant rows. Water drips out slowly
and directly onto the soil at the base of each plant. * Pros: Most efficient method.
Saves 90-95% of water. Water goes straight to the roots. Less weed growth. * Cons:
Very expensive to install, emitters can get clogged with dirt.
4. Maintenance of Irrigation Systems
To make irrigation systems last long, they must be maintained.
 For Watering Cans: Wash and dry them after use to prevent rust.
 For Sprinklers/Drips:
o Regularly check and clean the "emitters" or "nozzles" to remove any dirt or
algae that may be clogging them.
o Check all pipes and hoses for leaks and repair them.
o Keep the water source (tank, well, or dam) clean.
24

WEEK 8: PEST AND DISEASE CONTROL
Topic: Pest and Disease Control
Breakdown (Subtopics):
 Integrated pest management (IPM)
 Crop sanitation
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "pest" and "disease" in agriculture, giving one example of each.
26
2. List and explain three methods of pest and disease control (e.g., chemical, biological,
cultural).
3. Define "Crop Sanitation" and list two sanitation practices.
4. Define "Integrated Pest Management (IPM)."
5. Explain why IPM is considered a better approach than using only chemicals.
Lesson Content:
Pests and diseases are major problems for farmers, as they can destroy an entire harvest.
1. Definitions
 Pest: A pest is any organism (usually an animal) that damages crops and reduces the
quality or quantity of the harvest.
o Examples:
 Insects: Grasshoppers, Locusts, Stem Borers (eat maize stems),
Weevils (eat stored grains).
 Birds: Crows, Quelea birds (eat rice).
 Rodents: Rats, Mice (eat yam tubers, grains).
 Disease: A disease is a condition caused by a pathogen (a micro-organism) that
stops the plant from functioning normally, leading to poor growth or death.
o Examples:
 Fungi: Maize Rust, Black pod of cocoa.
 Bacteria: Bacterial wilt of tomato.
 Viruses: Cassava Mosaic Disease (causes yellow, mottled leaves).
2. Methods of Pest and Disease Control
1. Cultural Control:
o Definition: Using good farming practices (culture) to naturally prevent or
reduce pest problems.
o Examples:
27
 Crop Rotation (Week 2): Breaks the life cycle of pests that feed on
one crop.
 Timely Planting: Planting at the right time can help the crop avoid the
"peak" pest season.
 Using Resistant Varieties: Planting a type of maize that is naturally
"resistant" to stem borers.
2. Physical / Mechanical Control:
o Definition: Physically removing, trapping, or blocking pests.
o Examples:
 Hand-picking: Picking large insects (like caterpillars) off plants by
hand.
 Traps: Setting traps for rats.
 Fencing: Building a fence to keep rodents or goats out.
 Scarecrows: Used to scare birds away from a rice field.
3. Biological Control:
o Definition: Using a pest's natural enemies (predators or parasites) to control
it.
o Example: Releasing ladybugs (a "good" insect) into a garden to eat aphids (a
"bad" insect pest). This is using "nature to fight nature."
4. Chemical Control:
o Definition: Using man-made chemicals (poisons) to kill pests or stop diseases.
o Types: Pesticides (kill pests), Insecticides (kill insects), Fungicides (kill fungi),
Herbicides (kill weeds).
o Pros: Very fast and very effective in the short term.
o Cons: Can be expensive, can kill "good" insects (like bees), can pollute water,
can be dangerous to the farmer's health.
3. Crop Sanitation
 Definition: Crop sanitation simply means keeping the farm clean. It is one of the
best and cheapest forms of cultural control.
28
 Purpose: A clean farm removes the food and hiding places for pests and diseases,
preventing them from building up and spreading.
 Sanitation Practices:
1. Regular Weeding: Weeds are unwanted plants that hide pests (like insects
and snakes) and compete with crops.
2. Removing Diseased Plants: If one plant is sick, pull it out and burn or bury it
far away from the farm so the disease cannot spread.
3. Clearing Farm Tools: Cleaning soil and plant sap off your cutlass and hoe
after use so you don't carry diseases from one plant to another.
4. Destroying Crop Residues: After harvesting maize, the old, dry stalks should
be removed, as stem borers can hide in them until the next season.
4. Integrated Pest Management (IPM)
 Definition: IPM is a "smart," modern approach that combines multiple control
methods (cultural, biological, physical, and chemical) to manage pests safely and
effectively.
 How it works: An IPM farmer doesn't just spray chemicals at the first sign of a pest.
1. First, they use prevention (cultural control, sanitation).
2. Then, they monitor the field.
3. If a pest appears, they try biological or physical controls first.
4. Only as a last resort, if the pest is causing major damage, will they use a
small amount of the safest possible chemical, applied at the right time.
 Why it's better: It is more sustainable, better for the environment, safer for the
farmer, and prevents pests from becoming resistant to chemicals.

Lesson Content:
Harvesting is not the end of the farmer's work. What happens after harvest is just as
important. In many countries, 30-50% of food is lost after it leaves the field.
1. Post-Harvest Technology & Losses
 A. Post-Harvest Technology:
o Definition: This includes all the activities, operations, and technologies used
after a crop is harvested to protect it, preserve it, and prepare it for
consumption, storage, or sale.
 B. Post-Harvest Loss:
o Definition: This is the reduction in the quantity (amount) or quality (taste,
nutrition) of a crop after it has been harvested.
o Causes:
 Physical Damage: Bruising, cutting, or crushing during careless
harvesting and transport.
 Pests: Insects (like weevils) and rodents (rats) eating stored grains.
 Spoilage: Rot, mould, and decay caused by fungi and bacteria
(especially if the crop is not dried properly).
2. Key Post-Harvest Operations
1. Handling:
o This is the careful movement of harvested crops.
o Example: Tomatoes must be handled gently and put in padded crates, not
baskets, to prevent bruising.
37
2. Threshing / Shelling:
o The process of separating the desired grain from the stalk, cob, or pod.
o Examples: Beating rice stalks to release the grain; removing maize grains from
the cob.
3. Drying:
o This is the most important step for grains. It involves removing excess
moisture to a safe level (10-13%).
o Why: Wet grains will be attacked by mould (fungi) and rot in storage.
o Methods: Sun-drying (on a mat or patio), using an artificial crop dryer.
4. Cleaning:
o Removing stones, dirt, chaff, and insects from the grain.
3. Storage
 Definition: Storage is the process of keeping the crop in a safe, clean, and dry place
until it is needed for food or sale.
 Good Storage Conditions: A good storage place must be:
o Dry: To prevent mould.
o Cool: To slow down spoilage.
o Pest-proof: To keep out rats and insects.
o Well-ventilated: To allow air to circulate.
 Storage Methods:
o Traditional Methods:
 Yam Barns: A structure made of poles and thatch used to tie and store
individual yam tubers.
 Rhombus: A local grain crib (store) made of mud and straw, raised off
the ground.
o Modern Methods:
 Silos: Large, airtight metal or concrete towers used to store huge
amounts of grain (maize, wheat) on commercial farms.
38
 Cold Rooms: A large, refrigerated room (like a walk-in freezer) used to
store perishable fruits and vegetables (like tomatoes, peppers).
4. Processing
 Definition: Processing is the changing of a crop from its raw form into a new,
different, or more valuable product.
 Examples of Processing:
o Cassava $\rightarrow$ Gari, Fufu, Starch, Cassava flour (Lafun)
o Maize $\rightarrow$ Pap (Ogi), Cornflakes, Maize flour, Cooking oil
o Oil Palm Fruit $\rightarrow$ Palm Oil, Palm Kernel Oil
o Tomato $\rightarrow$ Tomato Paste, Ketchup
o Groundnut $\rightarrow$ Groundnut Oil, Kulikuli
 Importance of Processing:
1. Increases Shelf-Life (Preservation): Processing makes food last much longer.
Example: Fresh cassava rots in 3 days, but Gari can last for a year.
2. Adds Value: Processed goods sell for much more money than raw ones.
Example: A bottle of palm oil sells for more than the fresh palm fruits used to
make it.
3. Reduces Bulk: Makes the product easier to transport and store (e.g., Gari is
lighter and smaller than fresh cassava).
4. Improves/Changes Taste: Makes food more convenient or better tasting.

Topic: Crop Improvement
Breakdown (Subtopics):

 Selection, breeding, hybridization
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

46

Lesson Content:
1. Meaning and Goals of Crop Improvement
 Definition: Crop Improvement is the science of developing new varieties of crop
plants that are better than the existing ones. It is the process of "improving" a plant's
genetics to make it more useful to humans.
 Goals (What we want to achieve):
1. High Yield: To produce more food (e.g., more maize grains per cob, or bigger
yam tubers).
2. Disease/Pest Resistance: To develop plants that can naturally fight off
diseases (like Cassava Mosaic Disease) or pests (like stem borers).
3. Better Quality: To improve the taste, nutritional value, or appearance (e.g., a
sweeter orange, or rice with more protein).
4. Drought Resistance: To develop crops that can survive with very little water.
5. Early Maturity: To develop crops that are ready to harvest in a shorter time
(e.g., a 90-day maize vs. a 120-day maize).
2. Methods of Crop Improvement
There are three traditional methods used to improve crops.
 A. Introduction:
o Definition: This is the simplest and oldest method. It involves taking a highquality
crop variety from another country or region and introducing it to a
new area.
o Example: The Tenera variety of oil palm, which is very high-yielding, was
introduced to Nigeria from Malaysia.
47
 B. Selection:
o Definition: This is the process of choosing the "best" plants from a large
population (based on their appearance) and using their seeds to grow the next
generation.
o Types of Selection:
1. Mass Selection: The farmer walks through a field and picks all the
best-looking plants (e.g., the tallest maize with the biggest cobs). He
mixes all their seeds and plants them next year. This is simple but not
very accurate.
2. Pure-Line Selection: A more scientific method. The farmer picks the
single best plant, grows its seeds in a separate plot, and repeats this for
many generations until he gets a "pure line" where all plants are
excellent.
 C. Hybridization (or Breeding):
o Definition: This is the most powerful method. It is the process of crossing
two different parent plants that have different desirable traits, in order to
produce an offspring (a "hybrid") that has both good traits.
o How it works:
1. Parent A: Has very high yield, but gets sick easily.
2. Parent B: Has low yield, but is very disease-resistant.
3. Cross-Pollinate: The breeder takes pollen from Parent A and puts it
on the flower of Parent B.
4. Result (Hybrid C): The seeds from this cross will grow into a new
"hybrid" plant that is both high-yielding and disease-resistant.
o Hybrid Vigor (Heterosis): This is the name for the common result where the
hybrid offspring is bigger, stronger, and better than both of its parents.

1. Seed Production
 Definition: Seed production is a specialized, large-scale business of growing crops

 Seed vs. Grain:

 Definition: Seed certification is a quality-control process. It is a system where a

ready to harvest at the same time. This makes managing the farm much easier.
4. Farmer Confidence: The farmer is confident that what they are planting is what the

Lesson Content:
1. Meaning of Organic Farming
 Definition: Organic farming is a method of crop production that avoids the use of
all artificial (synthetic) chemicals. This includes chemical fertilizers (like NPK and
Urea) and chemical pesticides (like insecticides and fungicides).
 Core Idea: It is a "back-to-nature" system that focuses on building healthy soil and a
healthy environment. It uses natural methods that our great-grandparents used, but
with modern scientific understanding.
 Organic vs. Conventional:
o Conventional Farming: Uses chemical fertilizers (NPK) and chemical
pesticides to get high yields. This is the most common type of farming today.
o Organic Farming: Uses compost and biological pest control (IPM) to get
good yields.
2. Key Principles of Organic Farming
An organic farmer lives by these rules:
1. Feed the Soil, Not the Plant: Instead of giving the plant "fast food" (NPK), the
farmer builds healthy soil using compost, farmyard manure, and green manure
(cover crops). A healthy soil will then feed the plant.
2. No Chemical Pesticides: The farmer uses cultural controls (crop rotation, sanitation)
and biological controls (IPM, using natural enemies) to manage pests (see Term 1,
Week 8).
3. No Chemical Herbicides (Weed-killers): Weeds are controlled by hand-weeding,
mulching (covering the soil), or cover cropping.
55
4. Promote Biodiversity: An organic farmer encourages "good" insects, birds, and soil
microbes to live on the farm, creating a balanced ecosystem.
5. Use Organic Seeds: (When possible) planting seeds that were also grown organically.
3. Benefits of Organic Farming
 A. Benefits to Health:
o The food produced is free from chemical residues, which many people
believe is healthier to eat.
o It is safer for the farmer, who is not exposed to dangerous pesticide sprays.
 B. Benefits to the Environment:
o It does not pollute water (rivers, streams) with chemical runoff.
o It protects "good" insects like bees (which are needed for pollination) and
earthworms.
 C. Benefits to the Soil:
o It builds soil structure and increases humus (organic matter), making the soil
better at holding water and resisting erosion.
4. Challenges of Organic Farming
If organic farming is so good, why doesn't everyone do it?
1. More Labour-Intensive: It often requires more physical work (e.g., hand-weeding
instead of spraying, making compost).
2. Lower Yields (Initially): When a farmer first switches from chemical to organic,
their crop yield might drop for a few years as the soil recovers.
3. Certification is Difficult: To legally label your food "Certified Organic," you must
pass a very strict and expensive inspection.
4. Appearance: Organic fruits and vegetables may not look as "perfect" or shiny as
conventional ones (they might have some insect bites), even though they are healthy.

Lesson Content:
1. Meaning of Greenhouse Technology
 Definition: A greenhouse is a framed building (structure) covered with a
transparent or translucent material (like glass, plastic, or insect-proof netting).
 Purpose: The main purpose of a greenhouse is to create an artificial, controlled
environment for growing high-value crops. It allows the farmer to control the
temperature, humidity, and water, and to protect the plants from pests and harsh
weather.
 Types:
o Greenhouse: Fully enclosed with plastic or glass to trap heat (used in cold
countries, or to control heat/humidity).
o Screenhouse (Net house): Enclosed with fine insect-proof netting (very
common in hot places like Nigeria) to block insects and reduce harsh sun while
allowing air to flow. We often use the word "greenhouse" for both.
2. Components of a Greenhouse/Screenhouse
1. Frame: The "skeleton" that holds the structure. It can be made of treated wood,
galvanized steel, or PVC pipes.
2. Covering (Glazing): The "skin" that lets in light.
o Plastic Film (Polyethylene): Most common, cheap, but needs replacing every
few years.
o Insect-proof Netting: Used for screenhouses to keep pests out.
59
o Polycarbonate (Plastic sheets): More expensive, but stronger.
o Glass: Very expensive, heavy, but lasts forever (not common in Nigeria).
3. Ventilation System: To control heat. This can be as simple as side-vents (roll-up
"curtains") or as complex as large electric fans.
4. Irrigation System: This is almost always Drip Irrigation (from Term 1, Week 6) to
deliver water and fertilizer directly to each plant.
5. Growing Beds or Bags: Plants are rarely grown in the natural ground. They are
grown in "grow-bags" or special beds filled with a clean, sterile soil mix (called
substrate).
3. Advantages in Horticulture (Olericulture/Floriculture)
Greenhouses are perfect for growing high-value horticultural crops (vegetables and flowers).
1. Year-Round Production: A farmer can grow tomatoes or peppers even in the middle
of the heavy rainy season (when they are normally scarce) because the rain cannot
hit them.
2. Excellent Pest and Disease Control: The net/plastic stops most insects (like
whiteflies and Tuta absoluta on tomato) from entering. This dramatically reduces the
need for chemical pesticides.
3. Higher Yield and Quality: Because the plants are protected and get the perfect
amount of water and nutrients, they produce more fruit, and the fruit looks better
(cleaner, no blemishes).
4. Water and Fertilizer Efficiency: Using drip irrigation means almost no water or
fertilizer is wasted.
5. Protection from Weather: Protects plants from heavy rain (which spreads disease),
strong winds, and harsh sun.
4. Disadvantages/Challenges
1. High Initial Cost: Building a proper greenhouse is very expensive.
60
2. Heat Build-up: If not ventilated properly, it can get too hot inside, and the plants will
die.
3. Requires High Skill: A greenhouse farmer must be well-trained to manage the
special irrigation and environment.

3. Planning for Success: SWOT Analysis
An idea is not enough. You must test if it is a good idea. The best tool for this is a SWOT
Analysis.
 Definition: SWOT is a planning tool that helps an entrepreneur identify the
Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats related to their business idea.
 Internal vs. External:
o Internal Factors (About YOU and your business): Strengths, Weaknesses.
o External Factors (About the WORLD outside): Opportunities, Threats.
 The Four Components:
o Strengths (Internal, Positive): What are you good at? What advantages do you
have?
 Example: "My father has 1 acre of land I can use for free." "I am very
good at record keeping."
o Weaknesses (Internal, Negative): What are you bad at? What disadvantages do
you have?
 Example: "I have no start-up money (capital)." "I am not good at
marketing."
o Opportunities (External, Positive): What good conditions exist in the market or
environment?
 Example: "The government is giving low-interest loans to young
farmers." "A new hotel just opened and needs a vegetable supplier."
o Threats (External, Negative): What bad conditions or competitors exist?
 Example: "There are three other women already selling pap on my
street." "A new disease is attacking maize in my area."
88
How to Use it: A smart entrepreneur uses the SWOT to make a plan: How can I use my
Strengths to take advantage of the Opportunities? And how can I manage my Weaknesses to avoid
the Threats?

Lesson Content:
Bookkeeping (Week 3) is about recording what happened in the past. Financial literacy is
about understanding that information to plan for the future.
1. What is Financial Literacy?
 Definition: Financial literacy is the ability to understand and use financial skills
like budgeting, investing, and managing money. It means "being smart with money."
 An agripreneur who is financially literate knows how to use their profit (from Week
3) to grow their business, instead of just spending it.
2. Budgeting
 Definition: A budget is a plan for your money before you spend it.
 Bookkeeping (Past): "I spent N1000 on feed."
 Budgeting (Future): "I plan to spend N1000 on feed next month."
 Importance: A budget is your main tool for controlling your spending. It stops you
from wasting money and ensures you have enough for the important things (like
buying seeds).
3. Cost Management: Fixed vs. Variable Costs
To create a good budget, you must understand your costs.
 Definition: Cost management is the process of planning and controlling your
expenses to increase your profit.
 There are two main types of costs:
96
 A. Fixed Costs (or "Overhead"):
o Definition: These are costs that do not change, no matter how much you
produce. You must pay them even if you grow 0 yams or 1,000 yams.
o Examples:
 Rent for the farmland or shop.
 Salary of your permanent manager.
 Cost of buying a big machine (like a tractor).
 Security bill for the farm.
 B. Variable Costs:
o Definition: These are costs that change directly with your level of production.
If you grow more, you spend more.
o Examples:
 Cost of seeds or chicks (If you want 2x the chickens, you pay 2x for
chicks).
 Cost of feed, fertilizer, and pesticides.
 Cost of packaging (nylon bags, cartons).
 Wages for temporary workers you hire just for harvesting.
4. Calculating Profit (The Full Formula)
The full formula for profit uses these new terms:
 Total Revenue (Sales): The total money you get from customers.
 Total Cost: Fixed Costs + Variable Costs.
 PROFIT = Total Revenue - Total Costs
Example: A poultry farmer has a shop.
 He pays N10,000 per month for rent (Fixed Cost).
 He buys feed and chicks for N30,000 (Variable Costs).
 He sells all his chickens and eggs for N50,000 (Total Revenue).
 Total Costs: N10,000 (Fixed) + N30,000 (Variable) = N40,000
97
 Profit: N50,000 (Revenue) - N40,000 (Total Costs) = N10,000

Lesson Content:
Starting a business is one thing; running it is another. That is "management."
1. What is Business Management?
 Definition: Business Management is the process of planning, organizing, leading,
and controlling the resources of a business (like people, money, and materials) to
achieve its goals.
 If your goal is to make N10,000 profit (Week 4), management is the work you do
every day to make that happen.
2. The Four Functions of Management
A manager has four main jobs:
1. Planning: This is the "thinking" part. It involves setting goals (e.g., "We will harvest
50 bunches of vegetables") and creating a plan to achieve them (e.g., "We need to
buy seeds on Monday, plant on Tuesday...").
2. Organizing: This is the "arranging" part. It involves arranging all your resources.
(e.g., "I will put Femi in charge of watering, Aisha in charge of weeding. We need to
get N5,000 from the budget for fertilizer").
3. Leading: This is the "motivating" part. It involves directing and inspiring your team
to do the work well. (See below).
4. Controlling: This is the "checking" part. It involves monitoring your progress (using
your records from Week 3) and comparing it to your plan. (e.g., "Our plan said we
100
would harvest 50 bunches, but our records show we only harvested 30. What went
wrong?").
3. Leadership
 Definition: Leadership is the ability to inspire and motivate a group of people to
work together enthusiastically towards a common goal.
 Manager vs. Leader:
o A Manager tells people what to do (e.g., "Femi, weed that row. That is your
job.").
o A Leader shows people why it's important (e.g., "Team, if we all work hard to
weed these rows, our harvest will be bigger, and everyone's profit will
increase!").
o A good agripreneur must be both a manager and a leader.
4. Teamwork
 Definition: Teamwork is the collaborative effort of a group of people working
together to achieve a common goal. It is the result of good leadership.
 Importance of Teamwork in Agribusiness:
1. Increases Efficiency: Many hands make light work. A team can plant or
harvest a field much faster than one person.
2. Better Problem-Solving (Week 2): When a problem happens (e.g., "Pests are
eating the leaves!"), a team can brainstorm more ideas (IPM, biological
control, etc.) than one person.
3. Specialization: Teamwork allows people to specialize in what they are good at
(e.g., one person is good at bookkeeping, another is good at marketing,
another is good at the physical farming).
4. Improves Morale: Working together is less lonely and more motivating for
everyone.

Lesson Content:
In Term 2 (Week 9), we learned about the 4 Ps of Marketing (Product, Price, Place,
Promotion). This week, we focus on the "Place" P, which is Distribution.
1. Market Research (Before You Do Anything)
 Definition: Market Research is the process of gathering, analyzing, and
interpreting information about your customers and your competitors.
 Purpose: To make smart decisions before you start. You don't guess what people want;
you ask them.
 Key Questions for Market Research:
o Who are my customers? (e.g., Students, mothers, hotels?)
o What do they want? (e.g., Do they want fresh tomatoes or tomato paste?)
o What price are they willing to pay?
o Who is my competition? (e.g., How many other people sell this?)
 Methods of Market Research:
1. Surveys/Questionnaires: Creating a list of questions and asking people to
answer them (e.g., "How often do you buy gari? What size bag do you
prefer?").
2. Observation: Simply watching and counting (e.g., "I will stand at the market
junction for one hour and count how many people buy yams.").
3. Focus Groups: Bringing a small group of 5-8 people together, giving them
your product (e.g., your kulikuli), and asking for their honest opinions.
2. Distribution (Logistics)
 Definition: Distribution (also called "Logistics") is the entire process of moving your
product from the farm to the final customer. It is the "Place" P.
104
 This includes: Harvesting, cleaning, packaging, storing (Term 1, Week 10),
transporting, and finally, selling.
3. Distribution Channels
 Definition: A "distribution channel" is the path your product takes to reach the
customer. The channel you choose affects your profit.
 Channel 1: Direct-to-Customer (DTC)
o Path: Farmer  Final Customer
o Example: Selling your eggs at a small shop at your own farm gate, or selling
your vegetables at the local school market.
o Pro: You keep 100% of the profit.
o Con: You sell in small quantities, and it takes a lot of your time.
 Channel 2: The Retail Channel
o Path: Farmer  Retailer (e.g., Shoprite, local shop)  Final Customer
o Example: You supply your fresh tomatoes to a supermarket in your town.
The supermarket then sells them.
o Pro: You sell more at once than DTC.
o Con: The retailer takes a "cut" (profit margin), so your profit per item is less.
 Channel 3: The Wholesale Channel (The "Middleman")
o Path: Farmer  Wholesaler  Retailer  Final Customer
o Example: You take your 1,000 yams to a bulk trader (wholesaler) at a large
market (like Mile 12). He buys everything at once (at a low price) and then sells
it to the small market women (retailers).
o Pro: You sell all your products in one day. Very fast, low risk of spoilage.
o Con: You get the lowest profit per item because the wholesaler and retailer
both take a large cut.

Lesson Content:
We have learned how to farm and how to make money. But what if our farming destroys the
land for our children? This is the final, most important lesson.
1. What is Sustainable Agriculture?
 Definition: Sustainable Agriculture is a method of farming that meets the world's
need for food today, without damaging the environment or the resources that future
generations will need to feed themselves.
 The Three Pillars: For farming to be truly sustainable, it must be:
1. Economically Profitable: The farmer must make a good profit (Weeks 4, 10).
2. Environmentally Sound: It must protect the soil, water, and air.
3. Socially Just: It must be good for the community, the workers, and the
customers.
2. What is Climate-Smart Agriculture?
 Definition: Climate-Smart Agriculture is a part of sustainability. It is an approach to
farming that specifically tries to solve the problem of climate change (e.g., longer dry
seasons, unpredictable rain, heavy floods).
 Its 3 Goals:
1. Increase farm productivity (grow more food).
2. Help farmers adapt to climate change (e.g., use drought-resistant seeds).
108
3. Reduce greenhouse gas emissions from farming (e.g., use compost instead of
chemical fertilizer, which releases gasses).
3. Sustainable Farming Practices (How to do it)
The good news is that we have already learned all the most important sustainable practices
this year! "Good farming" is sustainable farming.
 Practices that Protect the Soil (Environmentally Sound):
o Crop Rotation (Term 1, Week 2): Builds soil fertility naturally.
o Cover Cropping (Term 1, Week 2): Prevents erosion and builds organic
matter.
o Organic Farming (Term 2, Week 3): Using compost (Term 1, Week 4)
instead of chemicals builds healthy soil and stops pollution.
o Terracing / Contour Ploughing (Term 1, Week 2): Stops water erosion.
 Practices that Conserve Water (Climate-Smart):
o Drip Irrigation (Term 1, Week 6): The most water-efficient method.
o Mulching: Covering the soil to stop evaporation.
 Practices that Protect Biodiversity (Environmentally Sound):
o Integrated Pest Management (IPM) (Term 1, Week 8): Uses biological and
cultural controls instead of harsh chemicals that kill bees and good insects.
 Practices that Use Better Resources (Economically Profitable):
o Using Certified Seeds of Improved Varieties (Term 2, Weeks 1-2) that are
drought or disease-resistant.
4. Why is This Important?
If we use un-sustainable methods (like "conventional farming" with too many chemicals and
no crop rotation), we will:
 Destroy the soil (erosion).
 Pollute our drinking water.
 Kill all the bees needed for pollination.
109
 In 50 years, the land will be a "desert," and we will not be able to grow any food.
Sustainable agriculture protects the land and makes a profit, ensuring we can farm forever.
