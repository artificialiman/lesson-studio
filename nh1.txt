SS1 NIGERIAN HISTORY LESSON NOTES
FIRST TERM:
WEEK 1: INTRODUCTION TO HISTORY
Topic: Meaning, Scope, and Relevance of History
1. Content: The Meaning and Scope of History
 A. What is History? (Beyond "The Study of the Past")
o Simple Definition: History is the study of past events, particularly those related
to human affairs.
2
o A More Accurate Definition: History is a systematic and interpretive inquiry
into the human past. It is not just what happened, but how and why it happened,
who was involved, and what its short-term and long-term consequences were.
o Key Distinction: It's crucial to differentiate "The Past" from "History."
 The Past: Everything that has ever happened (events, thoughts, actions).
It is vast, chaotic, largely unrecorded, and gone forever.
 History: The academic discipline and the narrative that attempts to find,
analyze, organize, and present a coherent, evidence-based argument about
specific parts of the past. History is what historians write.
o E.H. Carr's Definition: A famous historian, E.H. Carr, defined history as "an
unending dialogue between the present and the past." This means our
understanding of the past is always shaped by the questions, values, and problems
of the present (e.g., today we are very interested in environmental history, a topic
historians 100 years ago ignored).
o History vs. Other Disciplines:
 vs. Social Studies: Social Studies is a blend of many disciplines
(geography, economics, civics, history) aimed at creating good citizens.
History is a distinct academic discipline focused on the past with its own
rigorous methodology.
 vs. Archaeology: Archaeology is a source for history, a "tool in the
toolbox." It provides the material evidence (artifacts) that historians then
interpret.
 B. The Scope of History (What Does History Cover?)
o The scope of history is as broad as the human experience itself. It is not just about
"great men," wars, and politics ("top-down" history). Modern history includes
"history from below" (the study of ordinary people).
o Political History: The study of power, government, laws, leadership, statebuilding,
and conflicts (e.g., the rise of the Oyo Empire, the amalgamation of
Nigeria).
3
o Diplomatic History: A sub-set of political history focusing on the relationships
between states (e.g., pre-colonial diplomacy between the Benin Kingdom and its
neighbours).
o Social History: The study of everyday life, family structures, gender roles, class
relations, urbanisation, and customs (e.g., the role of women in pre-colonial Igbo
society, the history of festivals).
o Economic History: The study of how people make a living, trade, agriculture,
technology, industry, and wealth distribution (e.g., the trans-Saharan trade, the
economic impact of the palm oil trade).
o Cultural History: The study of art, music, literature, religion, and ideas (e.g., the
significance of Ife bronze heads , the spread of Islam in Northern Nigeria).
o Intellectual History: The study of how ideas themselves (e.g., nationalism, Pan-
Africanism) have evolved and shaped society.
o Environmental History: A newer field studying the interaction between humans
and their natural environment over time (e.g., how desertification in the North
shaped migration patterns).
o Historiography: This is the "history of history." It studies how our understanding
of a historical event (e.g., the Nigerian Civil War) has changed over time as new
evidence emerges or new questions are asked.
2. Context: Importance and Relevance of History in Nation-Building
 A. General Importance of History:
1. Understanding Identity: History tells us who we are and where we come from.
It is the collective memory of our family, our community, and our nation.
2. Learning from the Past (Contextualising): It provides a vast "laboratory" of
human successes and failures. While history doesn't "repeat" exactly, it provides
context and shows us the range of human possibilities, allowing us to (in theory)
avoid repeating mistakes.
3. Developing Critical Thinking: History is the best discipline for developing
critical analysis. It teaches us to analyze evidence, evaluate different arguments,
4
detect bias, and build a coherent argument based on evidence—skills vital for any
field (law, medicine, business).
4. Understanding Change and Continuity: History is the only discipline that
explains how societies change over time, and also why some things (like cultural
beliefs) stay the same.
 B. Specific Relevance to Nation-Building (especially for Nigeria):
o "Nation-building" is the complex process of constructing a unified national
identity, loyalty, and consciousness, especially in a diverse, multi-ethnic, postcolonial
country like Nigeria.
2. Forging a Common Identity: History provides the shared stories, heroes
(Herbert Macaulay, Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti, Queen Amina), struggles
(colonialism, the fight for independence), and triumphs that can unite diverse
peoples under a single "Nigerian story."
3. Promoting National Unity & Countering Myth: By studying the long-standing
interactions, trade links, and inter-marriages between different groups (e.g.,
Hausa-Yoruba trade, Benin-Ife links) before colonialism, we can scientifically
counter the dangerous myth that Nigeria's groups are "natural enemies" who were
"forced together."
4. Providing Context for Current Conflicts: History helps us understand the deep
roots of contemporary problems (e.g., resource control, boundary disputes,
farmer-herder clashes, ethnic tensions). Understanding their origins is the first
step to resolving them.
5. Informing Public Policy: Past government policies on agriculture, education, or
governance (e.g., Operation Feed the Nation) can be studied to see what worked
and what failed, helping to inform better policies today.
6. Instilling Patriotism and National Consciousness: By celebrating national
achievements (e.g., the 1996 Olympic football gold) and understanding the
sacrifices made for independence and unity, history can foster a sense of pride and
belonging.
5
7. Fostering Reconciliation: Studying difficult periods like the Civil War from all
perspectives, with empathy and honesty, is essential for national healing and
building a "more perfect union."

WEEK 2: WHY WE TEACH HISTORY
Topic: Values of Studying History
1. Content: Educational, Moral, Political, and Social Values
 This week builds on "importance" by focusing on the specific values that historical study
imparts to an individual and society.
 A. Educational Values (The "Skills")
1. Critical Thinking: History is not about memorizing dates. It is about analysis. It
teaches you to evaluate evidence (sources), identify bias, compare conflicting
accounts, and build a logical, evidence-based argument. This is the primary skill
for identifying "fake news" and propaganda.
7
2. Research Skills: History trains you to find information, organize it, and present it
coherently. You learn how to use libraries, archives, footnotes, bibliographies,
and digital sources effectively.
3. Communication Skills: To be a good historian, you must be a clear and
persuasive writer and speaker. The discipline demands that you explain complex
ideas and narratives in a compelling way.
4. Intellectual Curiosity: History encourages you to ask "Why?" and "How?" It
fosters a lifelong habit of inquiry about the world around you, moving beyond
simple "what" questions.
 B. Moral Values (The "Character")
1. Empathy: History forces us to step into the shoes of people who lived in different
times and circumstances. We learn to understand their motivations, fears, and
beliefs, even if we don't agree with them. This is different from sympathy (feeling
sorry for them). Empathy is an intellectual understanding.
2. Ethical Reflection: Studying events like the trans-Atlantic slave trade, the
Holocaust, or the Nigerian Civil War forces us to confront difficult moral
questions about human nature, justice, oppression, and responsibility.
3. Humility: History shows us that our own time is not the "end of history." We see
how past civilizations (like Benin, Ife, Oyo) rose and fell, which can give us
perspective on our own achievements and challenges. It teaches us that our
"modern" beliefs may seem strange to future generations.
4. Inspiration: We study the lives of individuals who demonstrated great courage,
integrity, and resilience (e.g., Queen Amina of Zazzau, King Jaja of Opobo,
Margaret Ekpo), which can inspire us to act with integrity in our own lives.
5. Historical Perspective: The ability to understand that people in the past did not
know what we know now. We must judge them by the standards and knowledge
of their time, not ours. This is the opposite of "anachronism."
 C. Political Values (The "Citizen")
1. Informed Citizenship: To be an effective citizen, you must understand the
history of your nation's political systems, constitution, and laws. You learn why
we have a presidency, a federal system, state boundaries, etc.
8
2. Understanding "Spin" and Propaganda: History is full of propaganda. By
learning to detect bias in historical sources, we become better at detecting it in
modern political speeches, advertisements, and media.
3. Holding Leaders Accountable: A citizen who knows the historical context of
political promises and actions is better equipped to ask tough questions and
demand accountability.
4. Understanding International Relations: History explains the origins of
alliances, conflicts, and Nigeria's foreign policy (e.g., why Nigeria took a leading
role in ECOMOG or the fight against Apartheid in South Africa).
5. National Consciousness: Studying a shared history is what binds citizens
together and turns a "population" into a "nation" with a common sense of destiny.
 D. Social Values (The "Community")
1. Cultural Awareness and Tolerance: History introduces us to the vast diversity
of human cultures. In Nigeria, it helps us appreciate the rich traditions of our 250+
ethnic groups, promoting tolerance and respect instead of prejudice.
2. Understanding Social Change: History shows us how social structures (like the
family, gender roles, or class) are not fixed or "natural," but evolve over time.
This helps us navigate the changes in our own society.
3. Combating Prejudice: History teaches us that stereotypes and prejudices are
often a product of specific historical events or political manipulation (e.g., "divide
and rule" policies in colonialism). It shows they are learned, not "natural," and
can therefore be unlearned.
4. Global Citizenship: By studying Nigerian, African, and World History, we
understand our nation's place in the interconnected global community.

WEEK 3: SOURCES OF HISTORY I
Topic: Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary Sources
1. Content: Oral Tradition, Written Sources, Archaeology, Linguistics, etc.
 Historians are like detectives; they cannot study the past directly. Instead, they must rely
on evidence left behind. This evidence is called Sources.
o Primary Sources: Materials created at the time of the event, or by an
eyewitness/participant. They are raw, unfiltered data. This is the "stuff" of history.
o Secondary Sources: Materials created after the event, by historians interpreting
and analyzing primary sources. They are arguments about the past. (e.g., your
textbook, a biography of Lord Lugard).
11
o Tertiary Sources: Materials that summarize or index secondary sources (e.g.,
encyclopedias, dictionaries, bibliographies).
 This week, we focus on the types of primary sources.
 A. Oral Tradition
o Definition: Historical information passed down by word of mouth from one
generation to the next. It is the most important source for pre-colonial Nigerian
history.
o Types:
1. Myths: Symbolic stories explaining the origin of a people, their
relationship with God, or natural phenomena (e.g., the Oduduwa myth).
2. Legends: Stories about heroic figures, often based on a real person or
event, but embellished over time (e.g., the Bayajidda legend).
3. Folktales & Proverbs: Moral stories and wise sayings that reveal the
values and beliefs of a society.
4. Songs, Chants, and Poems: (e.g., Ijala - Yoruba hunter's chants) that
preserve history and cultural knowledge.
5. King Lists & Genealogies: Lists of rulers or family trees recited from
memory by specialists (e.g., Griots).
o Pros: Often the only source available for pre-literate societies. Provides rich
cultural context and the people's own perspective ("insider view").
o Cons: Prone to distortion (memory lapses), exaggeration, "telescoping"
(compressing time, e.g., making 10 kings into 5), and deliberate manipulation for
political reasons (e.g., to legitimize a new king).
o Methodology: Historians must collect multiple versions from different
informants and cross-reference them with other sources (like archaeology).
 B. Oral History
o Definition: Different from oral tradition. Oral history is the collection of
memories from people who witnessed or participated in recent historical events
(e.g., interviewing veterans of the Nigerian Civil War, or participants in the Aba
Women's Riots).
 C. Written Sources (Archives)
12
o Definition: Any information from the past that is written, printed, or inscribed.
o Examples in Nigerian History:
1. Arabic Manuscripts: (e.g., The Kano Chronicle, works by Uthman dan
Fodio). Very important for the history of the Northern states and the
Sokoto Caliphate.
2. Missionary Records: Diaries, letters, and reports from 19th-century
missionaries.
3. Colonial Government Records: Memos, reports, laws, and letters from
colonial officers (e.g., Lugard's reports).
4. Company Records: Ledgers and diaries from European (e.g., UAC,
Royal Niger Company) and Nigerian traders.
5. Newspapers: (e.g., West African Pilot). Crucial for the nationalist
movement.
6. Personal Documents: Diaries, letters, autobiographies (e.g., My Life by
Ahmadu Bello).
o Pros: Can be preserved with high accuracy. Can be dated precisely.
o Cons: Can be highly biased (e.g., a colonial officer's report on a riot will be
different from a participant's). Often only reflects the views of the literate,
wealthy, and powerful (the "elite").
 D. Archaeology
o Definition: The study of the past through the recovery and analysis of material
remains (artifacts) dug from the ground.
o Examples in Nigerian History:
1. Nok Terracotta Heads: (c. 1500 BC - 500 AD) Proves the existence of a
sophisticated ancient society in the Middle Belt.
2. Igbo-Ukwu Bronzes: (c. 9th Century AD) Exquisite bronze objects that
show a complex society with long-distance trade links.
3. Ife Terracotta & Bronze Heads: (c. 12th-15th Century AD) Worldfamous
naturalistic sculptures.
4. Benin Walls: A massive, ancient earthwork.
13
5. Dufuna Canoe: (c. 6500 BC) Discovered in Yobe State, it is the oldest
known boat in Africa and one of the oldest in the world, proving ancient
habitation.
o Pros: Provides tangible evidence for pre-literate societies. Can reveal technology,
diet, trade, and settlement patterns. Can be scientifically dated.
o Cons: Expensive. The act of digging is destructive. Artifacts can be difficult to
interpret without other sources (e.g., we have Nok art, but we know little of their
language or beliefs).
 E. Linguistics
o Definition: The scientific study of language.
o How it's used:
1. Glottochronology: By comparing vocabulary, grammar, and sound
changes, historians can trace the relationships ("family tree") and
migration patterns of different groups.
2. Loanwords: Studying "borrowed" words reveals historical contact (e.g.,
the presence of Hausa words in Yoruba, or Portuguese words in Efik).
o Example: Linguistic evidence shows that Yoruba, Edo, and Igala languages are
related (part of the Kwa language sub-family), suggesting they all split from a
common "ancestor" language thousands of years ago.
o Pros: Reveals deep historical connections (thousands of years) that are not
preserved in any other source.
o Cons: Highly technical. Provides a relative timeline, not exact dates.
 F. Ethnography
o Definition: The study and description of living human cultures. Historians
sometimes use ethnographic studies of modern societies to cautiously infer what
past societies with similar technology or organization might have been like.

WEEK 4: SOURCES OF HISTORY II AND DATING
Topic: Determining Historical Time
1. Content: Chronology and Dating Methods
16
 History is built on two pillars: Evidence (sources) and Chronology (time). Chronology is
the science of arranging events in their order of occurrence in time. Without it, history is
just a jumble of stories.
 We use two types of dating:
o Relative Dating: Placing events in a sequence. (e.g., "The Nok culture came
before the Ife culture.") We don't know the exact year, but we know the order.
o Absolute Dating: Assigning a specific calendar date or range to an event. (e.g.,
"Nigeria gained independence on October 1, 1960.")
 A. Calendar Systems (for Absolute Dating)
o Definition: Systems created by societies to organize long periods of time.
o Examples:
1. Gregorian Calendar: The most widely used calendar today (January-
December). It is a solar calendar. It numbers years based on a (nowcorrected)
calculation of Jesus Christ's birth. Dates are given as A.D.
(Anno Domini, "in the year of the Lord") or B.C. (Before Christ).
2. B.C.E. / C.E.: Many historians prefer the neutral terms C.E. (Common
Era) and B.C.E. (Before the Common Era). The numbering is exactly the
same (e.g., 2024 AD = 2024 CE; 500 BC = 500 BCE).
3. Islamic (Hijri) Calendar: A lunar calendar (based on the moon's cycles)
starting from 622 CE, the year of the Hijra. Used in many Arabic records.
Challenge: A lunar year is ~11 days shorter than a solar year, making
direct conversion complex.
4. Traditional Calendars: (e.g., The Igbo 4-day market week: Eke, Orie,
Afor, Nkwo). Used to date local events.
 B. Radiocarbon Dating (C-14 Dating) (for Absolute Dating)
o Definition: A scientific method used to date organic materials (things that were
once alive, like wood, bone, cloth, seeds, charcoal).
o How it Works (Simplified):
1. All living things absorb a radioactive form of carbon called Carbon-14
(C-14) from the atmosphere.
2. When the organism dies, it stops absorbing C-14.
17
3. The C-14 it already has begins to decay at a fixed, known rate.
4. The half-life of C-14 is approximately 5,730 years (meaning after 5,730
years, half of the C-14 will be gone; after another 5,730 years, half of the
remainder will be gone, etc.).
5. Scientists measure the remaining C-14 in an artifact (e.g., a wooden mask,
a bone) to calculate how long ago it died.
o Relevance: This is how we have absolute dates for archaeological finds like the
Nok culture (dated to as early as 1500 BCE) and Igbo-Ukwu (dated to the 9th
Century CE).
o Limitations: Only works on organic material. Only reliable for up to ~50,000
years. It gives a range of dates (e.g., 900 CE +/- 70 years), not an exact day.
 C. Genealogical Methods (for Relative Dating)
o Definition: Using king lists (dynastic lists) or family trees (genealogies) to create
a timeline. This is often part of oral tradition.
o How it Works: A historian collects a king list (e.g., a list of Obas of Benin or
Oonis of Ife). They then assign an average number of years (a "generation," e.g.,
25-30 years) to each reign to estimate a timeline.
o Pros: Provides a framework for chronology in oral societies.
o Cons: Highly unreliable. Names can be forgotten (creating gaps), reigns can be
very short or very long, and the "average generation" is just a guess. Often
politically motivated.
 D. Other Methods
1. Stratigraphy: (Relative) The archaeological principle that in an undisturbed site,
the deepest layers of earth (strata) are the oldest. An artifact found in a lower layer
is older than one found in a higher layer.
2. Linguistics (Glottochronology): (Relative) Estimates how long ago two related
languages split apart, based on changes in their core vocabulary.
3. Thermoluminescence: (Absolute) A scientific method used to date inorganic
material that was heated to a high temperature, such as pottery or burnt flint. It
measures the energy trapped in the object since it was last fired.
18
4. Dendrochronology: (Absolute) Tree-ring dating. Counting the annual growth
rings in wood. Very precise, but less useful in tropical Nigeria where trees may
not have distinct annual rings.

WEEK 6: HISTORICAL SKILLS
Topic: Approaches to Historical Analysis
1. Content: Ancient and Modern Approaches; Critical Thinking
 This topic examines how the method (historiography) of writing history has changed over
time.
 A. Ancient Approaches to Historical Analysis (Pre-19th Century)
o Characteristics:
1. Chronicling: The main goal was often to simply record events (annals or
chronicles), especially the deeds of kings, heroes, and warriors.
20
2. Moral Lessons: History was seen as a "teacher." Historians (like the
Roman historian Livy) used the past to provide moral examples of good
and bad behavior for citizens.
3. Praise & Legitimacy (Hagiography): Court historians (in Nigeria, Griots
or royal oral historians) had the job of praising the current ruler and
reciting his genealogy to prove his legitimate right to rule.
4. Supernatural Causes: Events were often explained as the will of God,
the gods, or fate, rather than human actions or economic/social forces.
5. Cyclical View: Many ancient societies saw history as a repeating cycle
(e.g., birth, growth, decay, death, rebirth).
o Examples: The Greek historian Herodotus (often included myths and legends), or
the court historians of the Oyo or Benin kingdoms.
o Weakness: Little to no analysis of sources. Myths were often accepted as fact.
Bias was open and expected.
 B. Modern Approaches to Historical Analysis (Scientific/Critical History)
o Origin: Began in the 19th century, famously with the German historian Leopold
von Ranke. His motto was to write history "as it actually happened" (wie es
eigentlich gewesen).
o Characteristics:
1. Emphasis on Primary Sources: A "fetish" for primary sources. The rule
is: "No documents, no history." (This was later modified by Africanists
like Jan Vansina to include oral tradition as a valid primary source).
2. Objectivity: The historian must try to be a neutral, "scientific" observer
and minimize personal, political, or national bias. (Though we now accept
perfect objectivity is impossible, as we all have biases).
3. Critical Evaluation of Evidence: This is the core skill. The historian
must act like a lawyer/detective and cross-examine their sources.
 External Criticism: Is the source authentic? (Is this document a
forgery? Is this Nok head real or a modern fake?)
21
 Internal Criticism: Is the source credible? (Was the author an
eyewitness? What was their motive or bias? Did they have a reason
to lie? Does their account match other, independent sources?)
4. Causation: Modern history is obsessed with "Why?" It looks for multiple,
complex causes (long-term, short-term, trigger) for every event. It
distinguishes between correlation (two things happen at the same time)
and causation (one thing caused the other).
5. Interpretation: History is not just a list of facts. It is an argument about
what the facts mean. This is why two modern historians can look at the
same event (e.g., the amalgamation) and write two different books about it.
o Later Developments:
 Marxist History: Focuses on economic factors and class struggle as the
main "engine" of history.
 Annales School (French): Focuses on long-term social, environmental,
and cultural history ("history from below"), rather than just politics.
 C. The Role of Critical Thinking and Evaluation of Evidence
o This is the practical skill set of a modern historian.
o Key Skills:
0. Distinguishing Fact from Opinion: A fact is "Lord Lugard amalgamated
the Northern and Southern Protectorates in 1914." An opinion is "The
amalgamation was a terrible mistake."
1. Identifying Bias: Bias is a strong, one-sided perspective. A missionary's
letter calling local customs "barbaric" is heavily biased. A good historian
doesn't discard the source, but uses the bias as evidence of the
missionary's worldview.
2. Corroboration: The most important rule. Never trust a single source. A
historian must find multiple (and different types of) sources that point to
the same conclusion. (e.g., an oral tradition, a missionary letter, and an
archaeological find all suggest a major battle occurred).
22
3. Understanding Context: Evaluating an event or statement based on the
standards and knowledge of its own time, not ours.
4. Avoiding Anachronism: The error of applying modern ideas, morals, or
technology to the past. (e.g., "Why didn't King Jaja just send an email?")
2. Evaluation (Week 6)
 Objective Questions:
1. The 19th-century historian who championed the "scientific" approach to history
was: (a) Herodotus (b) E.H. Carr (c) Leopold von Ranke (d) Lord Acton.

WEEK 8: PROSPECTS OF ICT IN HISTORICAL STUDIES
Topic: ICT in History
1. Content: Use of ICT in Data Collection, Storage, and Presentation
25
 ICT stands for Information and Communication Technology. This refers to computers,
the internet, software, and digital devices. ICT has revolutionized the study of history.
This new field is often called "Digital Humanities."
 A. ICT in Data Collection
1. Digitized Archives & Online Access: Millions of primary sources (documents,
newspapers, photos) are now scanned and available online. A historian in Nigeria
can access records from the British National Archives or the US Library of
Congress without leaving their desk.
2. Digital Oral History: Using high-quality digital audio recorders and video
cameras to conduct and preserve oral tradition interviews. This is clearer, more
durable, and easier to share and transcribe than old cassette tapes.
3. Archaeology:
 Ground-Penetrating Radar (GPR): Can "see" underground to find
buried structures before digging.
 Satellite Imagery: Can identify large-scale ancient settlements or
irrigation systems from space.
 LiDAR: Uses lasers to create 3D maps of the ground, revealing structures
hidden under forest canopies.
4. Databases & "Big Data": The ability to access and search massive online
databases (e.g., slave trade databases) to identify large-scale patterns.
 B. ICT in Data Storage and Analysis
1. Databases: Historians can build massive databases to manage and analyze large
sets of information (e.g., a database of all slaves taken from a specific port, with
their names, ages, and origins).
2. Digital Preservation: Creating high-resolution digital copies (scans, photos) of
fragile documents, books, and artifacts to preserve them from decay.
3. Text-Mining Software: Using computer programs to search thousands of
digitized newspapers or documents at once for specific keywords or patterns, a
task that would take a human a lifetime.
26
4. GIS (Geographic Information Systems): Special software used to create maps
that layer historical data (e.g., mapping the spread of the Sokoto Caliphate, or
mapping trade routes over time).
5. Cloud Storage: Safely backing up research and making it accessible from
anywhere.
 C. ICT in Data Presentation and Dissemination
1. Academic Websites & Blogs: Historians can share their research directly with
the public, bypassing traditional publishers.
2. Digital Museums & Virtual Exhibits: Creating interactive 3D models of
artifacts (like an Ife head) or virtual reality (VR) reconstructions of historical sites
(like the Benin palace).
3. Multimedia: Using ICT to create historical documentaries, podcasts, and
interactive timelines that are more engaging than a traditional textbook.
4. Social Media: Historians and archives use platforms like Twitter (X) and
Instagram to share historical photos and facts with a wide audience.
5. Historical Gaming: Video games (like Age of Empires or Assassin's Creed) are a
(controversial) but very popular way that millions of people consume historical
information.
 D. Context: Challenges of ICT in Nigeria
o While the prospects are bright, there are significant challenges:
1. Erratic Power Supply: A major obstacle to all digital work.
2. Cost & Access (Digital Divide): High cost of equipment and internet data
creates a gap between "haves" and "have-nots."
3. Digital Literacy: Need for training for both historians and students.
4. Digitization Gap: A vast amount of Nigeria's own historical records (in
our national archives) are not yet digitized and are decaying.
5. Data Integrity & Loss: Digital files can be corrupted, hacked, or become
obsolete as technology changes.

WEEK 9: LAND AND PEOPLES OF NIGERIA
Topic: Geographical and Ethnic Diversity
1. Content: Geography and its Impact on History
 Core Concept: Geography is the stage upon which history unfolds. The climate, vegetation,
and landscape of Nigeria profoundly shaped the societies that developed within it.
29
o Environmental Determinism (Outdated View): The old theory that geography
dictates human culture (e.g., "hot climates make people lazy"). This is considered racist
and wrong.
o Possibilism (Modern View): The correct view that geography offers possibilities and
sets limitations. Humans then use their culture and technology to choose from those
possibilities (e.g., the forest limited the use of horses, but offered yams as a food
source).
 A. Location and Significance
o Location: Nigeria is located in West Africa, on the coast of the Gulf of Guinea. It is
bordered by Benin, Niger, Chad, and Cameroon.
o Significance: It is a "crossroads" region.
1. North-South Axis: It connects the arid, camel-based trade of the Sahara
Desert (Trans-Saharan Trade) to the forest and coastal regions.
2. East-West Axis: It is the eastern end of the "Sudan" savanna belt, which
facilitated movement and empire-building across West Africa.
3. Coastal Access: The southern coast opened Nigeria up to maritime trade
with Europeans (leading to the trans-Atlantic slave trade and later, the
palm oil trade).
 B. Climate and Vegetation (The Major Zones)
o 1. Southern Nigeria: The Forest Zone
 Climate: Tropical Rainforest climate (high rainfall, high humidity).
 Vegetation: Mangrove Swamp (coast) and dense Rainforest.
 Historical Impact:
 Tsetse Fly: The fly, which thrives in forests, carries sleeping sickness
(trypanosomiasis), which is fatal to horses and cattle.
 Military: Prevented the use of cavalry, limiting the size of armies and
empires (e.g., Benin, Oyo).
 Agriculture: Land was cleared for farming root crops (yams, cassava) and
tree crops (kola nuts, palm oil).
30
 Settlement: People lived in forest clearings, leading to more decentralized
political units (villages, small kingdoms) compared to the north.
 Defence: The dense forest was a natural defense against invasion.
o 2. Northern Nigeria: The Savanna Zone
 Climate: Tropical Savanna climate (distinct wet and dry seasons). Becomes
drier as you move north (Sahel).
 Vegetation: Open grasslands with scattered trees.
 Historical Impact:
 Cavalry: No tsetse fly. This allowed for the use of horses, which were the
"tanks" of the ancient world. Cavalry was key to building and controlling
large-scale, centralized empires (e.g., Kanem-Borno, Sokoto Caliphate).
 Agriculture: Suited for growing grains like millet, sorghum, and rice.
Supported large populations.
 Livestock: Open grassland supported cattle-herding (e.g., the Fulani).
 Trade & Communication: Open terrain made travel and communication
easy, linking the region to the Trans-Saharan trade.
o 3. The Middle Belt (A "Shatter Zone")
 Location: The hilly, diverse region between the North and South (e.g., Jos
Plateau).
 Historical Impact: This region was a "shatter zone" and "buffer zone." It was
home to many smaller, non-Muslim, non-centralized groups (e.g., Tiv, Gwari,
Mumuye). They used the hills (like the Jos Plateau) as a natural fortress to resist
the cavalry-based empires of the north.
 C. Major River Systems
o Niger River and Benue River: These were the "highways" of pre-colonial Nigeria.
o Impact: They facilitated trade, migration, and communication between different groups.
The point where they meet, the Confluence (at Lokoja), has always been a strategic
and cultural melting pot.
 D. Ethnic and Linguistic Diversity
o Nigeria is home to over 250 (some estimates say 400+) distinct ethnic groups. This
diversity is a product of centuries of migration and settlement.
31
o Three Largest Groups:
0. Hausa-Fulani: Predominantly in the North. This is a cultural-political
term. The Hausa are historically sedentary traders and farmers. The
Fulani are historically nomadic pastoralists (cattle-herders) who led the
19th-century jihad that founded the Sokoto Caliphate, fusing the two
groups politically.
1. Yoruba: Predominantly in the Southwest. Historically organized into
powerful centralized city-states and kingdoms, each led by an Oba (e.g.,
Ife, Oyo, Ijebu, Egba).
2. Igbo: Predominantly in the Southeast. Historically, most Igbo
communities were politically decentralized or "acephalous" (headless),
living in autonomous villages governed by councils of elders and titleholders.
o Other Major Groups (Examples):
 Kanuri (Northeast, Borno Empire)
 Ijaw (Niger Delta)
 Edo (Benin Kingdom)
 Tiv & Nupe (Middle Belt)
 Ibibio & Efik (Cross River)
o Key Point: These groups were not isolated. They had centuries of trade, diplomacy,
and inter-marriage before Nigeria was created.

WEEK 10: TRADITIONS OF ORIGIN AND HISTORICAL LINKS
Topic: Myths, Legends, and Oral Accounts of Origin
1. Content: Analyzing Origin Myths
 Key Concept: Origin myths are not literal, factual history. A historian does not ask "Did
Oduduwa really come from heaven?" Instead, a historian asks, "What is the purpose of
this story? What does it tell us about the people who believe it?"
 Origin myths are historical sources (oral tradition) that explain a people's "truth" about
their identity, their right to their land, and their political/social relationships with their
neighbors. They are a "social charter."
 A. The Yoruba (Oduduwa Myth)
34
o The Story (Ife Version): Olodumare (God) sent his son, Oduduwa, from heaven
on a chain. Oduduwa landed on the primordial water at Ile-Ife. He had a handful
of earth, which he sprinkled on the water, and a cockerel, which spread the earth
to create the land. Ile-Ife became the first land, and Oduduwa became the first
Ooni (King). His sons and grandsons (e.g., Oranmiyan) later traveled out to found
the other major Yoruba kingdoms (like Oyo) and even the Benin dynasty.
o Alternative Story (Migration Version): Another tradition, popular in Oyo,
claims Oduduwa was a migrant prince from "the East" (possibly Mecca or Egypt),
who fled and settled in Ife.
o Historical Interpretation (What it means):
1. Political Legitimacy: It gives all "crowned" Yoruba Obas a divine right
to rule, as they are all "descendants" of Oduduwa.
2. Cultural Unity: It establishes a "family" relationship between the
different, and often competing, Yoruba kingdoms.
3. Spiritual Homeland: It establishes Ile-Ife as the orirun (cradle) and
spiritual capital of the entire Yoruba-speaking world.
4. Historical Debate: The "migration" version suggests historical links
between the Yoruba and peoples to the East, possibly reflecting the
ancient trans-Saharan trade routes.
 B. The Hausa (Bayajidda Legend)
o The Story: A prince named Bayajidda fled from Baghdad (in modern-day Iraq).
He traveled across the desert and came to Daura. He found the people were
terrorized by a great snake named "Sarki," which lived in the well and only
allowed them to fetch water on Fridays. Bayajidda killed the snake, married the
Queen of Daura, and had a son named Bawo. Bawo, in turn, had seven sons who
founded the "Hausa Bakwai" (The Seven True Hausa States: Daura, Kano,
Katsina, Zaria, Rano, Gobir, and Biram). Bayajidda's other sons (by a concubine)
founded the "Hausa Banza" (The Seven "Illegitimate" States, e.g., Nupe, Gwari,
Yoruba—groups that were influenced by the Hausa but not "of them").
o Historical Interpretation (What it means):
35
1. Political & Cultural Link: It explains the origin of the Hausa states and
links them together as "brothers" in a common political and cultural
framework.
2. External Validation: By claiming the founder came from Baghdad (the
center of the Islamic Caliphate), it links the Hausa states to the prestigious
and powerful Islamic world long before the 19th-century jihad.
3. Explaining Relationships (Social Charter): It creates a clear hierarchy
and framework for understanding the relationship between the "true"
Hausa states and their non-Hausa neighbors (the "Banza" states).
4. Gender: It may represent a historical shift from a matriarchal society
(ruled by the Queen) to a patriarchal one (ruled by Bayajidda and his
sons).
 C. The Igbo (Decentralized and Nri Myths)
o The Context: Unlike the Yoruba and Hausa, most Igbo communities do not have
a single, grand origin myth. This itself is historical evidence reflecting their
history of political decentralization.
o Common Traditions:
1. Autochthony: Many Igbo villages simply state, "We have always been
here," or that their founding ancestor "sprang from the earth" (esi n'ala
puta). This is a powerful political claim to their land.
2. Short-Distance Migration: Most village traditions point to a founder who
came from a nearby village just a few miles away.
o The Nri Myth (An Exception): The Nri kingdom (a spiritual center, not a
military one) has a myth. It states that Eri was sent from the sky by Chukwu (God)
and landed at the confluence of the Niger and Anambra rivers. His sons founded
Nri and other related groups.
o Historical Interpretation (What it means):
1. The lack of a single myth confirms the historical pattern of political
fragmentation. Identity was local.
2. The "autochthony" myth asserts an unchallengeable, ancient right to their
land.
36
3. The Nri myth explains and legitimizes the special spiritual role of Nri
priests, who traveled across Igboland to perform rituals.
 D. Historical Link: Benin and Ife
o The Story: The Benin tradition states that after a period of misrule (the Ogiso
dynasty), the people of Benin sent to the Ooni of Ife, asking for a prince to rule
them. The Ooni sent his son (or grandson), Oranmiyan. Oranmiyan came to
Benin, but found it difficult to rule. He had a son with a local princess, Eweka I,
and left him to rule, returning to Ife. Eweka I became the first Oba of the new
(and current) Benin dynasty.
o Historical Interpretation (What it means):
1. This is a clear statement of a direct historical and political link between
Benin and Ife.
2. It shows the new Benin dynasty drew its legitimacy from the prestigious,
older, and spiritually potent Ife tradition (descended from Oduduwa).
o Corroborating Evidence: This oral tradition is strongly supported by art history.
The world-famous bronze-casting techniques of Benin and Ife are so similar,
especially in the early periods, that art historians believe the skill was likely
introduced from Ife to Benin around the time this political link was forged.

WEEK 1: EARLY CENTRES OF CIVILIZATION IN NIGERIA
Topic: Nok, Igbo-Ukwu, Ife, and Benin Civilizations
1. Content: Archaeological Findings and Significance
42
 Introduction: This week examines the tangible proof of ancient, sophisticated societies
within Nigeria, long before European contact. These "civilizations" are defined by their
advanced art, technology, social organization, and (in some cases) trade networks.
 A. Nok Culture (c. 1500 BC - 500 AD)
o Location: Found over a vast area of the Middle Belt, particularly the Jos Plateau.
First discovered in the village of Nok.
o Archaeological Findings:
1. Terracotta Sculptures: Life-sized and small-scale human and animal
figures.
2. Style: Highly distinct style: triangular or semi-circular eyes, perforated
pupils, elaborate hairstyles, and beaded ornaments.
3. Iron Smelting: Dozens of iron-smelting furnaces (slag, tuyeres) have
been found alongside Nok art.
o Significance:
1. Art: It is the oldest known tradition of sophisticated figural art in Sub-
Saharan Africa.
2. Technology: It represents the earliest known evidence of iron smelting in
West Africa (and possibly all of Sub-Saharan Africa). This "Iron Age"
transition allowed for better tools (farming) and weapons (warfare).
3. Society: The complexity and similarity of the art over a vast area suggest
a well-organized, socially stratified society, not just scattered huntergatherers.
 B. Igbo-Ukwu Civilization (c. 9th - 10th Century AD)
o Location: In the forest region of Southeastern Nigeria, near modern-day Awka
(Anambra State).
o Archaeological Findings: Discovered in three sites (Isaiah, Richard, and Jonah
Anozie's compounds):
1. Elaborate Bronzes: Incredibly intricate bronze objects (bowls, staff heads,
pendants) made using the "lost-wax" (cire-perdue) method.
43
2. Grave Goods: One site was the burial chamber of a high-status individual
(a priest-king or Eze Nri), buried with a crown, staff, and thousands of
trade beads.
3. Materials: The bronzes were made of high-lead copper, a material not
found locally.
o Significance:
1. Technology: Shows a mastery of bronze casting completely independent
of European influence and stylistically unique from Ife or Benin.
2. Trade: The thousands of carnelian and glass beads (identified as coming
from as far as India and Egypt) prove that Igbo-Ukwu was part of a vast,
long-distance trade network.
3. Society: It reveals a highly wealthy, stratified, and complex society in the
Igbo heartland centuries before the rise of the great centralized kingdoms,
suggesting an alternate path to complexity (based on spiritual/economic
power, not military might).
 C. Ife Civilization (c. 12th - 15th Century AD)
o Location: Ile-Ife, considered the spiritual "cradle" (orirun) of the Yoruba people.
o Archaeological Findings:
1. Naturalistic Sculptures: World-famous sculptures in both terracotta and
bronze (brass).
2. Style: Unlike Nok (stylized) or Benin (idealized), Ife art is remarkably
naturalistic and realistic, capturing individual portraits.
3. Urbanism: Evidence of a large city-state, including paved courtyards and
roads made of "potsherds" (broken pottery).
o Significance:
1. Artistic "Golden Age": Represents a peak of artistic achievement and
technical skill in all of Africa.
2. Political "Mother-Culture": As the center of the Oduduwa myth, Ife's
political and spiritual model influenced the founding of many other
kingdoms, most notably Benin and Oyo.
44
3. Technology: The bronze casting (lost-wax) skill is believed to have been
transferred from Ife to Benin.
 D. Benin Civilization (c. 12th Century AD - 1897)
o Location: Benin City, capital of the Edo people.
o Archaeological Findings:
1. Benin Bronzes: A vast collection of bronze and brass plaques,
commemorative heads, and sculptures.
2. Royal Art: Unlike Ife art (naturalistic), Benin art is more stylized,
hierarchical, and "idealized." It was a royal art form, used to decorate the
Oba's palace and record the kingdom's history, politics, and rituals.
3. Benin Walls (Iya): A massive complex of earthworks (walls and moats)
surrounding the city, one of the largest man-made structures in the world.
o Significance:
1. Political Power: The art and walls are testaments to a highly centralized,
wealthy, and powerful military empire, ruled by a divine Oba.
2. Historical Record: The bronze plaques functioned as a kind of historical
archive, recording kings, court events, and interactions with Europeans.
3. Continuity: It is a civilization whose oral traditions (like the Ife-Benin
link) are directly corroborated by archaeological and art-historical
evidence.

 Objective Questions:
1. The earliest known iron-smelting culture in West Africa is: (a) Ife (b) Nok (c)

WEEK 2: STATE FORMATION PROCESS I
Topic: Concept of Centralized and Non-Centralized States
1. Content: State Formation and Political Organization
 A. What is a "State"?
47
o A state is an organized political community living under a single system of
government, occupying a defined territory.
o Key Features: A state has a government that has sovereignty (the authority to
make laws, collect taxes, raise an army, and manage relations with other states).
 B. Factors in State Formation (How do states form?)
o States don't just appear; they evolve. Key "ingredients" include:
1. Population Growth: More people create a need for more complex
organization.
2. Agricultural Surplus: When farmers can grow more food than they need,
it frees up other people to become specialists (soldiers, priests, kings,
artists, blacksmiths).
3. Trade: The need to organize and control long-distance trade routes (like
the Trans-Saharan) often leads to the rise of a powerful state (e.g., Kanem-
Borno).
4. Warfare/Defense: The need to defend against external enemies or to
conquer neighbors encourages people to unite under a strong leader.
5. Charismatic Leadership: A powerful warrior or religious leader can
unite different groups.
6. Religion/Ideology: A shared religion or belief system (like the Oduduwa
myth) can provide the ideological "glue" to hold a state together and
legitimize its rulers.
 C. Concept of Centralized States (Kingdoms/Empires)
o Definition: A state where power and authority are concentrated in the hands of a
single ruler (King, Emir, Oba, Mai) or a small, elite group at the center.
o Political Organization & Features:
1. Clear Hierarchy: A pyramid-like structure: King (at the top), followed by
nobles/chiefs, then commoners, then slaves.
2. Recognized Leader: A single, recognized head of state (e.g., Alaafin of
Oyo, Oba of Benin, Sarki of Kano).
3. Taxation: The central government has the power to collect taxes or tribute
from all parts of the state.
48
4. Standing Army: A professional army, loyal to the king, used for defense
and conquest (e.g., the Oyo cavalry, the Borno musketeers).
5. Bureaucracy: A system of appointed officials (ministers, governors) who
help the king run the government.
6. Codified Laws: A single set of laws, often based on royal decree or
religious code (e.g., Sharia), that applies to everyone.
o Examples in Nigeria: Kanem-Borno, Hausa States, Oyo, Benin, Nupe, Jukun.
 D. Concept of Non-Centralized States (Acephalous/Segmentary)
o Definition: "Acephalous" means "headless." These are societies that function
without a single, central ruler or "head." Power is diffused (spread out) among
different groups and institutions.
o Note: It is wrong to call them "stateless" or "societies without government." They
have government, but it is organized differently.
o Political Organization & Features:
1. Kinship (Family): The lineage or clan (extended family) is the most
important political, social, and economic unit.
2. No Single Ruler: No king, no standing army, no central tax collection.
3. "Checks and Balances": Power is balanced between multiple institutions.
4. Governing Institutions:
 Council of Elders: (e.g., Amala or Oha-na-Eze in Igboland) – The
main decision-making body.
 Age Grades: Groups of men (and sometimes women) of the same
age. They function as the public workforce (building roads), the
police (enforcing laws), and the army (defending the village).
 Secret Societies: (e.g., Ekpe in Calabar, Ogboni in parts of
Yorubaland) – Acted as a powerful judiciary and legislature, often
with more power than any individual.
 Priests/Oracles: Religious leaders (like the Arochukwu oracle)
could act as a "supreme court" to settle disputes.
o Examples in Nigeria: Igbo, Tiv, Idoma, Ibibio, Ijaw.
49

WEEK 3: CENTRALIZED STATES I: KANEM AND BORNU TO 1800
Topic: Kanem-Bornu Empire
1. Content: Political Organization, Trade, and Religion
 A. Location and Origin:
51
o Located in the Lake Chad Basin. One of the longest-lasting empires in history (c.
8th Century - 1893).
o Kanem (Phase 1): The state was founded east of Lake Chad by the Sefawa
dynasty, a nomadic group.
o Bornu (Phase 2): In the 14th century, the Sefawa were forced to move west of
Lake Chad, establishing their new capital, Ngazargamu, in Bornu. The empire
reached its peak here.
 B. Political Organization:
o The Mai (King): The Mai was a divine king, at the top of the political structure.
He held absolute power in theory.
o Council of Twelve: The Mai was advised by a council of 12 great nobles (often
from his own family), who helped administer the provinces.
o The Magira (Queen Mother): The mother of the Mai held immense political
power and influence. She had her own palace, officials, and territory.
o The Magara (King's Sister): The Mai's official "first sister" also held significant
power. (This highlights the important political role of royal women).
o Provincial Administration: The empire was divided into provinces, ruled by
governors appointed by the Mai.
o Military: The military was the key to the state's survival and expansion. It was
built on a powerful cavalry (horse-riding warriors) and, later, firearms.
 C. The Role of Trade (The Economy):
o Trans-Saharan Trade: Kanem-Bornu's location made it a critical crossroads for
the Trans-Saharan trade.
o Exports (South to North): The empire exported slaves, ivory, ostrich feathers,
and kola nuts.
o Imports (North to South): The empire imported horses (vital for the cavalry),
salt (vital for life), textiles, and later, muskets (firearms) from the Ottoman Empire
(North Africa).
o Control of Trade: The Mai grew wealthy by controlling and taxing this trade.
This wealth paid for the army and the bureaucracy.
 D. The Role of Religion (Islam):
52
o Adoption: The Sefawa dynasty adopted Islam around the 11th century (Mai
Humai).
o Significance:
1. Administration: Islam provided a system of law (Sharia) and literacy
(Arabic), which made the government more efficient.
2. Diplomacy: It connected Kanem-Bornu to the wider Islamic world (North
Africa, Middle East), allowing for diplomatic and trade alliances.
3. Education: Rulers like Mai Idris Alooma built mosques and sponsored
Islamic scholarship.
 E. Mai Idris Alooma (c. 1571 - 1603) - The "Golden Age":
o He is the most famous of all the Mais.
o Military Reformer: He imported muskets and Turkish military instructors from
the Ottoman Empire, creating the first army in West Africa to use firearms
effectively.
o Administrator: He built mosques, replaced traditional law with Sharia law, and
built a new capital at Ngazargamu.
o Diplomat: He established embassies in Tripoli and with the Ottoman Sultan.

WEEK 4: CENTRALIZED STATES II: HAUSA STATES TO 1800
Topic: Hausa City-States
1. Content: City-States, Economy, and Leadership
 A. Origin and Location:
55
o Located in the savanna of Northern Nigeria (recap Bayajidda legend from Term
1).
o Hausa Bakwai: The "Seven True Hausa States" (Daura, Kano, Katsina, Zaria,
Rano, Gobir, Biram).
o Hausa Banza: The "Seven Illegitimate States" (Nupe, Gwari, Yoruba, etc.) –
places that were influenced by Hausa culture but not "original."
 B. Political Organization: The City-State Model
o City-State (Birni): Unlike Kanem-Borno (a single empire), the Hausa states were
independent, rival city-states. Each state consisted of a large, walled city (birni)
and the farmlands surrounding it.
o Lack of Unity: They were unified by language and culture, but politically they
were rivals. They constantly fought each other for control of trade routes. This
lack of unity made them vulnerable to outside empires like Kanem-Borno and,
later, the Sokoto Caliphate.
o Leadership (The Sarki):
 Each state was ruled by a King, the Sarki.
 The Sarki was not divine like the Mai or Oba. He was a political and
military leader.
o Administration: The Sarki was assisted by a complex bureaucracy of officials:
 Sarkin Kasa (Chief of the Land)
 Madawaki (Commander of the Cavalry)
 Galadima (Head of Administration)
 Sarkin Fada (Head of Palace Officials)
 C. Economy: A System of Specialization
o The Hausa states were the industrial and commercial center of West Africa.
o Specialization: Each city-state specialized in a different economic activity:
 Kano: Became the most powerful. Famous for industry (textiles, leather
goods, dyeing) and was the primary market (Trans-Saharan trade).
 Katsina: A major trade center (competing with Kano) and also a famous
intellectual center, with many Islamic scholars.
56
 Zaria (Zazzau): Located further south. Specialized in agriculture (grain)
and capturing slaves from the Middle Belt.
 Gobir: The northernmost state, focused on military defense against desert
nomads.
 D. Religion (Islam and Bori)
o Islam was introduced via the Trans-Saharan trade (c. 14th Century).
o It became the religion of the Sarkis and the urban merchant class. It brought
literacy (Arabic) and law (Sharia).
o Syncretism: However, among the common people, Islam was mixed with
traditional Hausa beliefs, a spirit-possession religion called Bori.
o Tension: By 1800, this "impure" mixing of Islam and traditional beliefs created
tension, which was a key cause of the 1804 Fulani Jihad.
 E. Queen Amina of Zazzau (c. 16th Century)
o A famous warrior-queen from Zaria (Zazzau).
o She is remembered in oral tradition as a brilliant military commander who
expanded Zaria's power, built many walled camps (known as Ganuwar Amina or
"Amina's Walls"), and controlled trade routes as far as Nupe and Kano.
o She is a powerful example of female leadership in pre-colonial Nigeria.

WEEK 6: CENTRALIZED STATES III: OTHER STATES
Topic: Nupe, Jukun, Ife, Oyo, Benin, and Efik
A. Nupe Kingdom
59
o Location: Middle Belt, on the Niger River (Confluence area).
o Leadership: Ruled by the Etsu Nupe (King).
o Significance: A "bridge" state between North and South. Famous for its powerful
cavalry (like the North) but also its river-based trade. Known for bronze and glass
making. Was a major rival of the Oyo Empire.
 B. Jukun Kingdom (Kwararafa)
o Location: Middle Belt (Benue River Valley).
o Leadership: Ruled by a divine king, the Aku Uka.
o Significance: Kwararafa was a powerful, confederacy (loose empire) feared for
its military. Its cavalry famously raided the Hausa states (including Kano) in the
17th century. Its king was seen as a major spiritual power.
 C. Ife Kingdom
o Recap (Week 1): Ooni (King). Not a large military empire, but the undisputed
spiritual and cultural "cradle" of the Yoruba people. Its political importance
comes from its legitimizing power (e.g., the Benin and Oyo dynasties traced their
right-to-rule to Ife).
 D. Oyo Empire
o Leadership: Ruled by the Alaafin (King).
o Political Organization (A System of "Checks and Balances"):
1. The Alaafin: The King, in theory, was absolute.
2. The Oyo Mesi: A council of 7 great chiefs, led by the Bashorun (Prime
Minister). They were the kingmakers and had the power to depose
(remove) the Alaafin by sending him a "calabash of parrot's eggs" (a death
sentence).
3. The Ogboni: A powerful, secret society of elders, priests, and nobles that
acted as a check on the power of the Oyo Mesi, preventing them from
becoming too powerful.
o Military: The Are-Ona-Kakanfo (Generalissimo). Oyo's power came from its
cavalry, which allowed it to dominate the savanna portion of its empire.
o Economy: Grew rich by controlling trade routes from the interior to the coast
(port of Ajase/Porto Novo), especially in slaves.
60
 E. Benin Empire
o Recap (Week 1): Ruled by the Oba (divine king).
o Political Organization: Highly centralized and bureaucratic.
o Three Groups of Chiefs:
1. The Uzama N'ihiron: The seven hereditary "kingmakers" (the oldest
nobility).
2. Palace Chiefs: (e.g., Iwebo, Ibiwe) – Appointed by the Oba, loyal to him,
and lived in the palace.
3. Town Chiefs: (e.g., Iyase - Prime Minister) – Appointed by the Oba,
represented the people.
o The Oba's Power: The Oba skillfully balanced the Palace and Town chiefs
against each other to maintain his supreme power.
o Economy: Royal guilds (craftsmen) and trade (pepper, ivory, slaves) at the port
of Ughoton.
 F. Efik States (Old Calabar)
o Location: Cross River estuary (Southeastern coast).
o Political Organization: Not a single kingdom. A collection of rival city-states
(e.g., Duke Town, Creek Town).
o The Ekpe (Leopard) Society: This was the true government. It was a graded,
secret society that all free men of wealth had to join.
o Functions of Ekpe:
1. Legislative: It made the laws.
2. Judicial: It settled disputes and punished criminals.
3. Economic: It regulated trade, collected debts, and enforced contracts.
o Economy: Grew extremely wealthy by dominating the trans-Atlantic slave trade
in the Bight of Biafra, later switching to palm oil.

WEEK 8: NON-CENTRALIZED STATES I
Topic: Social and Political Organization of Tiv, Idoma, Igbo, Ibibio, and Ijaw
 A. Introduction (Recap Week 2):
o These societies are "acephalous" (headless). Power is diffused, not concentrated.
o They are not anarchies. Government is maintained through a balance of social and
political institutions, with kinship (family) as the central organizing principle.
63
 B. The Igbo
o Location: Southeastern forest region.
o Political Unit: The village or village-group was the highest level of political
organization.
o Political Institutions (How power was balanced):
1. Council of Elders (Oha-na-Eze or Amala): A general assembly of all
adult men (or just family heads) where decisions were made by consensus
(general agreement), not by a king's decree.
2. Title-Holders (Ozo): Wealthy, respected men who had taken titles. They
formed an influential "inner council."
3. Age Grades: Groups of men of the same age. They functioned as the
village militia (defense), police (enforcing laws), and public workforce
(clearing paths).
4. Priests: Custodians of the village deity/shrine.
5. Umuada: The "daughters of the lineage" (married women) formed a
powerful social and political group that could settle disputes and protect
women's rights.
o Exceptions: Some Igbo towns on the Niger River (like Onitsha and Aboh) did
have kings (Obi), a practice borrowed from their Benin neighbors.
 C. The Tiv
o Location: Middle Belt (Benue Valley).
o Political Unit: Based purely on genealogy (family tree).
o Political Organization: All Tiv trace their ancestry to a single founder, "Tiv."
Society is segmented into lineages.
o Leadership: No chiefs. Decisions were made by councils of elders (Jir) at the
family or village level. There was no single Tiv leader until the British created the
office of the Tor Tiv in the colonial period.
 D. The Idoma
o Location: Middle Belt (West of the Tiv).
o Political Organization: Similar to the Igbo. Lived in autonomous villages.
64
o Leadership: Each village had a head (Oche), but his power was balanced by a
council of elders. The paramount leader, the Och'Idoma, is a modern (colonial)
creation to have a single "spokesman."
 E. The Ibibio
o Location: Cross River region (Southeastern coast).
o Political Organization: Very similar to the Igbo (village-groups, councils of
elders).
o Key Institution: The Ekpo Society (a "ghost" society, not the same as Efik Ekpe).
This secret society of elders had immense political, judicial, and religious power,
and enforced its laws through members wearing masks representing ancestors.
 F. The Ijaw
o Location: The creeks of the Niger Delta.
o Political Organization (The "House System"):
 Geography: The delta environment (swamps, small islands) was not
suitable for large states.
 The "House" (Wari): The primary political and economic unit was the
"Canoe House."
 What it was: A "House" was not just a family. It was a trading
corporation, built around a single, powerful trader (the Amanyanabo or
House Head) and his "canoe boys" (who were often slaves or servants).

o Function: These Houses competed fiercely for control of the rivers and trade
(first slaves, then palm oil) with the Europeans at the coast. The most successful
Houses (like Bonny, Opobo, Nembe) became powerful, fortified city-states.

WEEK 9: NON-CENTRALIZED STATES II
Topic: Aspects of their History and Inter-group Interactions
 A. Common Features (Revisited)
o Judicial Systems:
 Disputes were settled by elders through mediation and arbitration (finding
a compromise), not by a king's absolute decree.
67
 Oaths and Ordeals: To find a "guilty" party, people were often made to
swear an oath on a powerful shrine (mbiam). It was believed the juju
(spirit) would kill the person who lied.
o Religious Sanctions: Priests and elders could enforce laws by threatening
ostracism (banning someone from the community) or by placing a religious curse.
o Defense: No standing army. All able-bodied men (usually in an age grade) were
the militia, mobilized only during a threat.
 B. The Role of Oracles (Inter-group Interaction)
o In a land with no "Supreme Court," powerful religious oracles evolved to fill that
role.
o The Arochukwu Oracle (Ibini Ukpabi):
 Location: In Arochukwu (an Igbo/Cross River town).
 Function: This oracle became the most famous and feared "court of
appeal" in Southeastern Nigeria. People from all over Igboland, Ibibioland,
and beyond would travel to it to settle difficult disputes.
 The "Secret": The oracle was controlled by the Aro people. They used
their position as priests to build a vast commercial network across the
region.
o The Aro Confederacy:
 This was not a traditional empire. It was a confederacy (alliance) of Aro
trading posts, all linked by their control of the Ibini Ukpabi oracle.
 Economic Power: The Aro used the oracle to their advantage. They
would often declare one party in a dispute "guilty," and the "fine" would
be to sell them (or their relatives) as slaves.
 Role in Slave Trade: The Aro Confederacy became the primary inland
network for capturing and transporting slaves to the coastal Ijaw and Efik
city-states, who then sold them to Europeans. They were the most
powerful middlemen in the region.
 C. Other Inter-group Interactions
o Warfare: While common, it was often localized and had "rules." It was fought
over land, resources, or personal insults.
68
o Trade: Constant, local trade was the norm. Specialized markets (e.g., the 4-day
Igbo market week: Eke, Orie, Afor, Nkwo) were the primary point of contact
between villages.
o Marriage: Inter-marriage between villages was very common and was the main
way of building alliances and ensuring peace.

WEEK 10: INTER-GROUP RELATIONS I
Topic: Economic and Cultural Exchanges
 A. Introduction: The Myth of Isolation
o It is a myth that pre-colonial Nigerian groups lived in isolation. The entire region
was connected by a complex web of economic, cultural, and political
relationships.
70
 B. Economic Inter-group Relations (The Engine of Interaction)
o Trade: This was the most important form of interaction, driven by geographical
specialization (recap Week 9, Term 1).
o Major Trade Routes:
1. North-South Trade:
 From the North (Savanna): Salt (vital), horses, livestock (cattle),
natron, leather goods, textiles.
 To the North (From the Forest): Kola nuts (a mild stimulant,
essential for Hausa society), palm oil, yams, ivory, and (from the
1600s) slaves.
2. Riverine Trade: The Niger and Benue rivers were the "highways" for
trade.
 The Ijaw brought fish and salt from the delta upriver.
 The Igbo and Nupe brought yams and textiles downriver.
3. Coastal Trade: (From the 1500s) Efik, Ijaw, and Itsekiri traders acted as
middlemen between inland producers (like the Aro) and European ships,
exchanging slaves and palm oil for guns, gin, and textiles.
o Marketplaces: Famous "melting pot" markets (like Onitsha, on the Niger) were
places where Igbo, Ijaw, Igala, and Benin traders met peacefully.
o Currencies: Trade was conducted using various currencies, including cowrie
shells, manillas (metal bracelets), salt, and iron bars.
 C. Cultural Inter-group Relations
o Religion:
 Islam spread from Kanem-Borno and Hausaland south along trade routes,
influencing Nupe and parts of Yorubaland.
 The Ibini Ukpabi oracle (Igbo) was used by Ibibio, Ijaw, and Efik peoples.
 Oracles and deities were often "borrowed" from one group to another.
o Language:
 The spread of loanwords (e.g., Hausa words in Yoruba, Portuguese words

 Hausa became a lingua franca (common language of trade) across much
of the savanna.
o Art:
 The most famous example is the Ife-Benin connection. The skill of bronze

o Settlement:
 Many towns and villages (like Onitsha) have oral traditions of being
founded by migrants from another group (e.g., Onitsha founded by

 D. Political and Diplomatic Relations
o Warfare: Wars were common (over territory, trade routes, or slaves), but they

WEEK 1: INTER-GROUP RELATIONS II
Topic: The Impact of Migrations, Wars, and Politics
1. Content: Non-Economic Inter-group Relations
 Introduction: In Term 2 (Week 10), we established that pre-colonial Nigerian groups
were deeply connected by economic (trade) and cultural links. This week, we examine
the other powerful forces that shaped their relationships: politics, migration, and warfare.
 A. Political and Diplomatic Relations
o Diplomacy: Centralized states did not live in a vacuum. They practiced
sophisticated diplomacy:
76
1. Embassies: Kings sent ambassadors (Ilaris in Oyo) to other courts to
negotiate treaties, deliver messages, and spy. The Alaafin of Oyo and the
Oba of Benin had famous, long-standing (if rivalrous) diplomatic relations.
2. Royal Marriages: A very common way to build political alliances. A
king would marry his daughter to the ruler of another state to create a
"family bond" and ensure peace (e.g., this was common among the Hausa
Sarkis and between Oyo and other Yoruba kingdoms).
o Alliances: States would form political and military alliances to fight a common
enemy. For example, two Hausa city-states might temporarily ally to fight a third
that was dominating a trade route.
 B. The Impact of Migrations
o Definition: A large-scale movement of people from one area to another.
o "Push" and "Pull" Factors:
 Push Factors (Why they left): War, natural disasters (drought, famine),
overpopulation, political disputes (e.g., a prince loses a power struggle and
leaves with his followers).
 Pull Factors (Why they came): Better farmland, new trade opportunities,
safety in a new location (e.g., a hilltop).
o Consequences of Migration:
0. Founding of New States: Many oral traditions are migration stories (e.g.,
the Bayajidda legend, the Oduduwa-from-the-East legend). The founding
of Onitsha by migrants from Benin is a classic example.
1. Cultural Mixing: When migrants settle in a new area, they mix with the
"host" population. This leads to the blending of languages, religions, and
technologies.
2. Conflict: Migration can also cause conflict, as newcomers compete with
original inhabitants for land and resources (e.g., the long-term migration
of Fulani pastoralists into Hausa lands, a key factor in the 1804 Jihad).
 C. The Impact of Warfare
77
o War as an "Engine of Change": War was not just random violence; it was a
major force that reshaped societies.
o Common Causes:
0. Economic: To control trade routes (e.g., Oyo vs. Nupe), to raid for slaves
(especially after 1600), or to control resources (farmland, salt).
1. Political: To expand an empire and force neighbors to pay tribute (e.g.,
Benin's expansion), or to resist an empire (e.g., the Tiv resisting Jukun).
o Consequences of Warfare:
0. State Formation: The threat of war often caused states to form, as
scattered villages united under a single military leader for defense.
1. Rise of Military Elites: Successful generals (like the Are-Ona-Kakanfo in
Oyo) became extremely powerful, sometimes even challenging the king.
2. Destruction and Displacement: Wars led to the destruction of towns and
the creation of large numbers of refugees.
3. Founding of Refugee Towns: Many modern cities were founded as
refugee settlements. The most famous example is Abeokuta, which was
founded in the 1830s by Egba refugees fleeing the wars that followed the
collapse of the Oyo Empire.

WEEK 2: INDIGENOUS TECHNOLOGY AND INDUSTRIES
Topic: Iron, Weaving, Pottery, Carving, and Blacksmithing
1. Content: Pre-colonial Technology
 Introduction: "Technology" is how a society uses its knowledge of tools and crafts to
control and adapt to its environment. Pre-colonial Nigeria had highly advanced and
specialized industries.
 A. Iron Smelting and Blacksmithing
o Significance: This was the most important technology. It was the foundation of
all other activities (farming, war, hunting).
o Recap: The Nok culture (c. 1500 BC) provides the earliest evidence.
80
o The Blacksmith (Ogun):
 The blacksmith was one of the most respected and often feared members
of society.
 They were seen as magical figures who could control the "dangerous"
elements of fire and earth.
 In Yorubaland, they had their own deity, Ogun, the god of iron and war.
o Products:
 Agriculture: Hoes, machetes (cutlasses) – allowed for clearing the forest
and growing more food.
 Warfare: Spears, arrowheads, swords.
 Household: Knives, needles.
 Currency: Iron bars were a form of money in some areas.
 B. Weaving (Textile Industry)
o Materials:
 Cotton: Grown widely in the savanna (Hausaland, Yorubaland).
 Raffia: From the raffia palm tree, used in the forest and delta regions.
o Centers of Excellence:
 Kano: Famous for its cloth, dyed with deep blue indigo in dye pits. Kano
cloth was a major export on the Trans-Saharan trade.
 Yorubaland (Aso-Oke): Woven on a horizontal loom (by men) into rich,
heavy cloth for ceremonial wear.
 Akwete (Igbo): Woven on a vertical loom (by women) into intricate
patterns.
o Significance: Clothing was a major sign of wealth and status.
 C. Pottery
o Significance: Essential for storage and cooking. In most societies, this was an
industry run by women.
o Products:
 Domestic: Pots for carrying water, cooking soup, storing grain, and palm
oil.
 Artistic: The Ife civilization produced world-class terracotta sculptures.
81
 Architectural: Used to create "potsherd" pavements in Ife.
 D. Carving
o Wood: The most common material.
 Function: Not just "art," but functional items with spiritual power.
 Products: Masks (for masquerades), stools (for chiefs), doors (for
palaces), figures of ancestors (ikenga in Igboland), canoes.
o Ivory: A luxury good, carved by specialists for kings (e.g., in Benin).
o Calabash: Decorating and carving gourds was a widespread art form.
 E. Other Industries
o Bronze/Brass Casting: (Recap Week 1, Term 2) A highly specialized, royal
industry in Ife, Benin, and Igbo-Ukwu.
o Leatherwork: Centered in the Hausa states (Kano, Katsina), using goat skin
("Moroccan" leather) to make bags, cushions, and sandals, which were exported
to North Africa.
o Glassmaking: The Nupe kingdom (Bida) was famous for making glass beads and
bangles.
o Salt Production: A critical industry. Salt was extracted by evaporating brine
(salty water) in the delta or from salt springs (e.g., in the Middle Belt).

WEEK 3: EARLY EXTERNAL INFLUENCES
Topic: Contact with North Africa and Introduction of Islam
1. Content: The "Northern Door"
 Introduction: For centuries, pre-colonial Nigeria had two "front doors" to the wider
world:
1. The "Northern Door": The Sahara Desert, connecting to North Africa and the
Islamic world. This door opened first.
2. The "Southern Door": The Atlantic Ocean, connecting to Europe and the
Americas. This door opened later (c. 1472).
 This week focuses on the Northern Door.
 A. Contact with North Africa
84
o The "Barrier" and the "Bridge": The Sahara Desert is the world's largest hot
desert. For millennia, it was a massive barrier.
o The "Ship of the Desert": The introduction of the Camel (c. 100 AD)
revolutionized this. The camel could survive for days without water, allowing it to
"bridge" the Sahara.
o The Berbers and Tuaregs: These were the nomadic peoples of the Sahara. They
were the "truck drivers" of the desert—they owned the camels, knew the routes,
and managed the oases (watering holes).
o Initial Contact: The first contacts between Nigeria (specifically the Kanem
region and Hausaland) and North Africa were purely for trade.
 B. The Introduction of Islam
o The "Vehicle" of Islam: Islam was not spread by war or conquest (like the 1804
Jihad, which came much later). It was carried peacefully by the Berber and
Tuareg traders and by scholars (missionaries) who traveled with them.
o Order of Adoption (By Rulers):
1. Kanem-Bornu (c. 11th Century): The Sefawa dynasty (Mai Humai) was
the first major royal court in the Nigerian area to adopt Islam.
2. Hausaland (c. 14th Century): Rulers of Kano and Katsina adopted Islam,
brought by missionaries from Mali and Kanem.
o Why did Rulers Adopt Islam? (The "State-Building Kit"):
 Rulers adopted Islam less for personal faith and more for its political and
economic advantages. Islam provided a "kit" for building a more efficient
state:
2. Diplomacy: It made them part of the vast, wealthy, and powerful Islamic
World. A Mai of Bornu could send an ambassador to the Sultan of Egypt
as an "equal."
3. Literacy (Arabic): It brought the Arabic language, which was used for
record-keeping, taxes, and writing down laws (replacing memory).
4. Law (Sharia): It provided a single, unified legal code (Sharia) to govern a
large, diverse empire, replacing thousands of different village customs.
85
5. Specialists: It brought Ulama (scholars) who could serve as judges,
administrators, and secretaries for the king.
o Initial Impact: For the first few centuries, Islam was an urban religion (of the
king, his court, and the merchants). The common people in the countryside
continued to practice their traditional religions (like Bori).

WEEK 4: THE TRANS-SAHARAN TRADE
Topic: Routes, Goods Exchanged, and Impact
1. Content: The Great Desert Trade
 A. Definition: The Trans-Saharan Trade was a network of trade routes connecting Sub-
Saharan West Africa (including Nigeria) to the Mediterranean coast of North Africa (and
from there, to Europe and the Middle East).
 B. Major Trade Routes
o The trade was not a single road. It was a web of routes connecting major oases.
o Major Terminals in Nigeria:
1. Kano: The most important. It was the southern terminal of the major route
from Tripoli (in modern Libya).
87
2. Katsina: A major terminal, especially before Kano's rise.
3. Kukawa (in Bornu): The capital of Bornu, it controlled the route from
Fezzan (Libya).
 C. Goods Exchanged (The Great Exchange)
o From Nigeria (South to North):
 Gold: From sources further west (Ghana, Mali), but traded through
Nigerian states.
 Slaves: (A major item) Captives from raids on non-Muslim states (e.g., in
the Middle Belt) were marched north.
 Kola Nuts: (From the forest) Highly valued in the North as a stimulant.
 Ivory: For carving.
 Ostrich Feathers: For decoration in Europe.
 Leather Goods: The famous "Moroccan Leather" sold in Europe was
actually Hausa leather, exported from Kano.
o To Nigeria (North to South):
 Salt: The most valuable import. The savanna and forest had no local salt,
making it as precious as gold.
 Horses: The "tanks" of pre-colonial warfare. Essential for the cavalry of
Oyo, Kanem-Borno, and the Hausa states.
 Weapons: Swords, daggers, and (by the 16th C.) firearms for states like
Kanem-Bornu.
 Luxury Goods: Fine cloth, paper, books (especially the Qur'an), and coral
beads.
 D. Impact of the Trade
1. Economic Impact:
 It created enormous wealth for the states that controlled the routes and
terminals (Kano, Katsina, Kanem-Bornu).
 This wealth (from taxes) funded the entire state.
2. Political Impact:
88
 It allowed for the creation of large, centralized empires (recap Week 2,
Term 2).
 The trade (especially in horses) created a powerful military cavalry, which
was used to build and control these empires.
3. Cultural/Social Impact:
 Islam: It was the "vehicle" for the spread of Islam (recap Week 3).
 Literacy & Education: It brought Arabic, books, and Islamic scholars,
leading to the rise of intellectual centers (like Katsina).
 Urbanization: It led to the growth of massive, wealthy, and cosmopolitan
cities (Kano, Kukawa).

WEEK 6: ISLAM IN KANEM-BORNU AND HAUSA LAND
Topic: Spread, Influence, and Contributions
1. Content: Islam as a State Religion
 A. Recap:
o How it came: Peacefully, via traders and scholars from North Africa.
o Who adopted it: The Mai of Kanem-Bornu (11th C.) and the Sarkis of Hausaland
(14th C.).
 B. Influence on Governance (The "State-Building Kit" in action)
o Kanem-Bornu:
 Recap Mai Idris Alooma (16th C.): He used Islam to its fullest political
extent.
90
 He replaced traditional law with Sharia Law, enforced by Qadis (Islamic
judges).
 He used Arabic as the language of diplomacy, sending letters to the
Ottoman Sultan.
 He used Islamic funds to build mosques and hostels for Bornu pilgrims in
Mecca.
o Hausaland:
 The Ulama: The Sarkis (kings) began to rely on a new class of people: the
Ulama (Islamic scholars).
 New Political Offices: The Ulama were appointed as Qadis (judges),
Imams (spiritual leaders), and scribes (for keeping records).
 Law: Sharia law began to run parallel to traditional Hausa customary law,
creating a dual legal system.
 Literacy: The Sarkis could now issue written orders and have their
histories recorded (e.g., the Kano Chronicle), giving them more power.
 C. Contributions to Education and Scholarship
o Qur'anic Schools: The first formal schools were Qur'anic schools, where
children learned to read and write Arabic and memorize the Qur'an.
o Centers of Higher Learning: Cities like Katsina and Kano became famous
"university towns." Scholars from all over West Africa (like Timbuktu) would
come to study.
o Local Scholarship: A class of local scholars emerged, writing their own books
on theology, law, and history in Arabic.
 D. Social Influence and The Problem of Syncretism
o Syncretism (Mixing): For centuries, Islam was mixed with traditional beliefs.
o Example: A Sarki would go to the mosque on Friday, but he would also perform
traditional Hausa rituals to ensure a good harvest. The common people continued
to worship traditional spirits (the Bori).
o The Tension (The Seed of Jihad):
 By the 18th century, a new group of Fulani Islamic scholars (reformers)
began to preach against this "impure" Islam.
91
 They felt the Hausa Sarkis were not true Muslims—that they were still
"pagans" who used Islam only when it was convenient.
 This tension between the "reformers" (like Uthman dan Fodio) and the
"syncretic" rulers (the Hausa Sarkis) would lead directly to the 1804
Fulani Jihad (a key topic for SS2).

WEEK 8: EARLY EUROPEAN CONTACTS
Topic: Arrival of the Portuguese and other Europeans
1. Content: The "Southern Door" Opens
 A. The "Age of Exploration" - Why they Came
o Context: In the 1400s, the Trans-Saharan trade was controlled by North African
Muslims, who were rivals of Christian Europe.
o Primary Motives:
1. Find a Sea Route to India: To bypass the Muslim "middlemen" and get
direct access to the spices of Asia.
93
2. Find Gold: They had heard legends of the West African gold empires
(Ghana, Mali).
3. Find "Prester John": A mythical Christian king they hoped to find in
Africa to help them fight the Muslims.
 B. The Portuguese (The First to Arrive)
o Pioneers: Led by Prince Henry the Navigator, they developed new ships (the
caravel) that could sail against the wind.
o First Contact: The Portuguese explorer Ruy de Sequeira was the first European
to reach the Nigerian coast, arriving at the port of Benin (Ughoton) in 1472.
o The First Trade (Goods, not Slaves): The Oba of Benin welcomed them. They
traded:
 Europeans brought: Brass and copper bracelets (manillas), cloth, guns,
coral beads.
 Benin offered: Pepper (this was the main prize at first), ivory, and gum.
o Diplomacy Between Equals: This was not a colonial conquest. The Oba of
Benin was a powerful king, and the Portuguese treated him as such.
 c. 1486: The Oba of Benin sent an ambassador to Portugal to establish
formal relations.
o Missionary Activity:
 Catholic priests traveled with the traders to convert the Oba and his people.
 They had very little success in Benin, as the Oba's power was tied to his
own traditional religion.
 They had more success in Warri, where they converted a prince (who
became a local Olu) and established a small Catholic community.
 C. The Arrival of Other Europeans
o Breaking the Monopoly: For about 100 years, the Portuguese dominated the
trade.
o The English: Arrived in Benin in 1553 (Captain Thomas Wyndham). They were
shocked by the power and wealth of the Oba.

WEEK 9: TRANS-ATLANTIC SLAVE TRADE I
Topic: Origin and Organization
1. Content: The "Triangular Trade"
 A. Origin of the Atlantic Trade
o Pre-existing Slavery: Slavery already existed in Nigeria (as war captives, debtors,
or criminals) and in the Trans-Saharan trade (slaves were sent north).
o The New Demand: The "New World" (North & South America, Caribbean) was
colonized by Europeans. They created huge plantations (sugar, tobacco, cotton).
o Labor Force: The native populations died from disease. Europeans wouldn't do
the work. The plantations needed a massive, cheap, forced labor force.
o The "Switch": The trade on the "Southern Door" (Atlantic) switched from goods
(pepper, ivory) to a massive, industrial-scale trade in human beings.
96
 B. Organization: The "Triangular Trade"
o This was the system that connected three continents: Europe, Africa, and the
Americas.
o Leg 1: Europe to Africa
 Ships left European ports (like Liverpool, UK) filled with manufactured
goods.
 The "Slave-Guns Cycle": The most important goods were Guns,
Gunpowder, Alcohol (Gin), and textiles.
o Leg 2: Africa to the Americas (The "Middle Passage")
 The European goods were exchanged on the Nigerian coast for captured
Africans.
 The "Middle Passage": This was the horrific 2-3 month voyage across
the Atlantic. People were packed into ships, chained, and kept in
unimaginable filth. Disease, suicide, and starvation were rampant. 15-20%
died on the journey.
o Leg 3: The Americas to Europe
 In the Americas (e.g., Brazil, Jamaica, USA), the surviving Africans were
sold at auction for plantation goods.
 The ships then loaded up with Sugar, Cotton, and Tobacco and sailed
back to Europe to sell them for a huge profit. The cycle then began again.
 C. The African Role (Middlemen)
o Fact: Europeans did not go inland to capture slaves. They were afraid of disease
(malaria) and African armies. They stayed on their ships or in coastal forts
("factories").
o African Middlemen: The trade was controlled by powerful African kings and
merchants who acted as middlemen.
1. Coastal States (The "Sellers"): Rulers of Ijaw houses (Amanyanabo), Efik city-states
(Ekpe society), and kingdoms like Benin and Oyo controlled the ports.
2. Inland States (The "Suppliers"): They captured people from the interior.
 Oyo Empire: Its cavalry raided villages to capture people to sell.
97
 Aro Confederacy: (Recap Week 9, Term 2) They used the Ibini
Ukpabi oracle to condemn people, then sold them into the trade via
their inland network.
o The "Guns-for-Slaves" Cycle: African states had to participate. If your rival
(Oyo) bought guns, you (Nupe) also had to buy guns to defend yourself. And the
only thing Europeans wanted in exchange for guns... was slaves. This created a
terrible, unstoppable cycle of violence.

WEEK 10: TRANS-ATLANTIC SLAVE TRADE II
Topic: Effects of the Slave Trade
1. Content: Consequences of the Trade
 A. Political Effects
1. Rise of "Predator States": The trade created new, powerful states whose entire
existence was based on capturing and selling people.
 Oyo Empire: Used its cavalry to become the dominant "slave-raiding"
power in the interior.
 Coastal City-States: Bonny, Opobo, Calabar (Efik), and Itsekiri (Warri)
became rich and powerful by acting as the middlemen.
99
 The "Canoe House" (Ijaw): This system (recap Week 8, Term 2) was
perfected for the slave trade, as "Houses" competed to capture and sell the
most people.
2. Increased Warfare and Instability: The "guns-for-slaves" cycle (Week 9) led to
constant, large-scale warfare. This was no longer about territory; it was about
capturing human beings.
3. Destruction and Collapse of States: This constant warfare destroyed smaller
villages and destabilized large empires. The eventual collapse of the Oyo Empire
in the early 1800s was caused by internal conflicts, which were made worse by
the pressures of the slave trade.
 B. Economic Effects
1. Distortion of the Economy: All economic energy shifted to the most profitable
activity: capturing and selling people.
2. Decline of "Legitimate" Industries: Local industries like cloth weaving, iron
smelting, and farming declined. Why make your own cloth or pots when it was
cheaper to buy them from Europeans with slaves?
3. Introduction of New Crops: This was one of the few positive effects. New food
crops were brought from the Americas, including Cassava and Maize (Corn).
These crops grew well in African soil and are now staple foods.
 C. Social and Demographic Effects
1. Depopulation: An estimated 10-12 million people were forcibly removed from
Africa. The Nigerian coast was a major center. This was the loss of the youngest
and strongest people (the main workforce).
2. Fear and Insecurity: Raids were a constant threat. People could not travel, farm,
or trade safely. This destroyed social trust.
3. Shift in Social Structures: The trade created a new class of super-wealthy,
powerful (and often very cruel) "merchant princes" (like King Jaja of Opobo, who
was himself a former slave).
4. Creation of the African Diaspora: The trade created vast, new Africandescended
cultures in Brazil, Cuba, Haiti, the USA, and the Caribbean. Many of
100
these (like the Orisha religion in Brazil/Cuba) preserved their Nigerian (Yoruba,
Igbo) cultural roots.
 D. Abolition (Why it Ended)
o Economic Reasons: (By 1800s) The Industrial Revolution made slave labor
seem less profitable than "legitimate" trade (e.g., selling palm oil to lubricate
machines). Britain wanted Africa as a market to sell goods to, not a place to get
slaves from.
o Moral/Humanitarian Reasons: The rise of Christian reformers (like William
Wilberforce in Britain) who argued that the trade was a terrible sin.
o The British (1807): Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807 and used its
powerful navy (the "Naval Squadron") to patrol the West African coast and stop
slave ships. This ended the trade and began a new era of "legitimate trade" (in
palm oil).
