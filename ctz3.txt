WEEK 1: REVIEW OF CITIZENSHIP CONCEPTS
1.1 INTRODUCTION
Welcome to SS3 Citizenship and Heritage Studies. This is your final year, and it builds upon all
the foundational knowledge from SS1 and SS2. This first week is dedicated to refreshing our
understanding of the core concepts that define the relationship between an individual and the
state.
1.2 CORE CONCEPTS REVIEW
1. THE STATE AND NATION
 State: A political entity with defined characteristics:
1. Population: A permanent group of people.
2. Territory: A clearly defined geographical boundary.
3. Government: A system or body that makes, implements, and enforces laws.
4. Sovereignty: The supreme and independent power to rule, free from external
control. (Nigeria is a sovereign state).
 Nation: A group of people who share a common identity, such as language, culture,
history, or ethnicity (e.g., the Yoruba nation, the Igbo nation).
 Nation-State: A state where the population (the citizens) overwhelmingly share a single
national identity (e.g., Japan, Iceland). Nigeria is a multi-national state as it comprises
many distinct nations within one state boundary.
2. CITIZEN AND CITIZENSHIP
 Citizen: A legally recognized, full, and responsible member of a state. A citizen holds
allegiance to their state and is entitled to protection and rights from that state.
2
 Citizenship: The status of being a citizen. It is the "social contract" that defines the
relationship, granting rights in exchange for duties.
3. MODES OF ACQUIRING CITIZENSHIP (RECAP)
1. Citizenship by Birth (Jus Sanguinis / Jus Soli):
o Jus Sanguinis (Law of Blood): Acquired by being born to parents (or at least one
parent) who are citizens. This is the primary basis in Nigeria (as per the 1999
Constitution).
o Jus Soli (Law of Soil): Acquired by being born within the territory of a state,
regardless of parents' citizenship (common in the USA, Canada).
2. Citizenship by Registration (Marriage):
o Acquired by an individual (typically a foreign woman) who marries a citizen of a
state. This requires an application and swearing an oath of allegiance.
3. Citizenship by Naturalization (Long Residence):
o The legal process by which a non-citizen (foreigner) acquires the citizenship of a
state after fulfilling specific requirements set by law.
o Requirements in Nigeria:
 Lived in Nigeria for a continuous period of 15 years.
 Deemed to be of good character.
 Has shown a clear intention to be domiciled in Nigeria.
 Has made (or is capable of making) a useful contribution to Nigeria.
 Has sworn the Oath of Allegiance.
4. RIGHTS, DUTIES, AND OBLIGATIONS
 Rights of a Citizen: These are the claims and entitlements guaranteed to a citizen, often
enshrined in the constitution (e.g., Chapter IV of the 1999 Constitution).
o Civil Rights: Right to life, liberty, fair hearing, freedom of expression, freedom
of movement.
o Political Rights: Right to vote and be voted for, right to form political parties.
3
o Socio-Economic Rights: (Often listed as "objectives" in Nigeria) Right to
education, healthcare, and gainful employment.
 Duties & Obligations of a Citizen: These are the responsibilities a citizen owes to the
state.
o Obey the law and respect the constitution.
o Pay taxes honestly and promptly.
o Vote during elections.
o Respect national symbols (flag, anthem).
o Assist law enforcement agencies.
o Defend the country (e.t., NYSC).

WEEK 2: NATIONAL IDENTITY & CITIZENSHIP
2.1 INTRODUCTION
National identity is the feeling of belonging to a specific state or nation. It is a collective sense of
"we-ness," a shared identity that rises above individual, ethnic, or religious differences.
Citizenship is the legal basis, while national identity is the emotional and psychological bond.
2.2 COMPONENTS OF NATIONAL IDENTITY
These are the symbols, values, and institutions that bind a diverse population together.
1. National Symbols (The Tangibles):
o The National Flag: (Green, White, Green) Represents agriculture/wealth (Green)
and peace/unity (White).
o The National Anthem: ("Arise, O Compatriots") A song of loyalty and a call to
service, adopted in 1978.
o The National Pledge: A personal vow of loyalty and service to the Federal
Republic of Nigeria.
o The Coat of Arms: A visual representation of national unity and resources.
 Black Shield: Nigeria's fertile soil.
 Wavy 'Y' Pall: The confluence of the Rivers Niger and Benue.
 Horses (Supporters): Dignity and pride.
 Eagle: Strength.
 Wreath (Green & White): Agriculture.
 Motto: "Unity and Faith, Peace and Progress."
o The Constitution: The supreme law that binds all citizens and governments
together.
o National Currency: The Naira (₦), a symbol of economic sovereignty.
o National Passport: A document identifying a person as a Nigerian citizen to the
world.
2. Shared Experiences and Values (The Intangibles):
5
o Shared History: A common history of pre-colonial empires, colonization by
Britain, the struggle for independence (led by nationalists), and the civil war.
These events, both good and bad, shape a collective memory.
o National Language: English serves as the official lingua franca, a unifying
language for education, government, and commerce, bridging the gap between
over 250 ethnic groups.
o National Institutions: Institutions that serve all Nigerians, such as the Armed
Forces and the National Youth Service Corps (NYSC), which was specifically
created to foster national unity.
o National Pride: Shared moments of collective pride, such as achievements in
sports (e.g., winning the AFCON, 1996 Olympics), literature (Wole Soyinka), or
music (Afrobeats).
2.3 CHALLENGES TO NATIONAL IDENTITY IN NIGERIA
As an SS3 student, it is crucial to analyze why a strong national identity is difficult to achieve.
1. Ethnicity/Tribalism: When citizens show greater loyalty to their ethnic group (e.g., Igbo,
Hausa, Yoruba, Ijaw) than to the Nigerian state. This manifests as nepotism and ethnicbased
politics.
2. Religious Intolerance: Conflicts and suspicions between different religious groups
(mainly Christianity and Islam) which can undermine national unity.
3. Corruption: When citizens see leaders embezzling public funds, it erodes their trust and
loyalty to the state, making the "call to service" in the anthem sound hollow.
4. Socio-Economic Inequality: A vast gap between the rich and the poor makes it hard to
foster a sense of "we-ness." A citizen struggling for basic survival may feel disconnected
from the state.
2.4 CITIZENSHIP'S ROLE IN FORGING IDENTITY
A strong, unified national identity is not automatic; it must be built. Active citizenship is the
primary tool.
6
 Political Participation: When citizens vote based on merit and issues (not ethnicity), it
strengthens national identity.
 Patriotism: A citizen who respects the flag, pays taxes, and obeys the law is actively
building the nation's identity.
 National Service (NYSC): This program directly attempts to build national identity by
forcing young graduates to live and work in parts of the country different from their own,
breaking down stereotypes.

WEEK 3: GLOBAL PEACE & CITIZENSHIP
3.1 INTRODUCTION: FROM NATIONAL TO GLOBAL CITIZENSHIP
While national citizenship binds us to a state, Global Citizenship is the idea that all people have
rights and duties as members of a larger, global community. It implies that our actions in Nigeria
can affect people in Brazil (e.g., climate change) and that global problems (e.g., pandemics,
terrorism) require global cooperation. A key aspect of this is maintaining global peace.
3.2 NIGERIA'S FOREIGN POLICY AND PEACEKEEPING
 Afrocentrism: The "centerpiece" of Nigeria's foreign policy since independence. This is
the belief that Nigeria's own stability, prosperity, and dignity are directly linked to the
stability and liberation of the entire African continent.
 Concentric Circles: This policy is often visualized as three circles:
1. Inner Circle (The "Ring"): Nigeria's immediate neighbours (ECOWAS region -
West Africa). This is our highest priority.
2. Middle Circle (The "Continent"): The rest of Africa (The African Union - AU).
3. Outer Circle (The "World"): The global community (United Nations - UN, The
Commonwealth).
 Peacekeeping as a Tool: Nigeria's commitment to Afrocentrism means it cannot be
stable if its neighbours are on fire. Therefore, Nigeria adopted peacekeeping as a major
tool of its foreign policy.
3.3 NIGERIA'S ROLE IN PEACEKEEPING OPERATIONS
Nigeria has a long and proud history of contributing troops to global peace, earning it the
nickname "the Giant of Africa."
1. Congo (1960-1964): Immediately after independence, Nigeria sent troops as part of the
UN Operation in the Congo (ONUC) to help stabilize the new nation.
2. Liberia (1990-1999): This is Nigeria's most significant operation. The country was torn
by civil war.
8
o ECOMOG (ECOWAS Cease-Fire Monitoring Group): Nigeria led the
creation of ECOMOG. It was not a UN mission but a regional force, largely
funded and staffed by Nigeria.
o Role: ECOMOG was not just a peacekeeping force; it became a peaceenforcement
force, actively fighting to separate warring factions and restore order.
o UNMIL (UN Mission in Liberia): After ECOMOG, Nigeria contributed troops
to the UN mission that stabilized the country for elections.
3. Sierra Leone (1997-2000):
o ECOMOG: As in Liberia, Nigeria led the ECOMOG intervention to oust the
RUF rebels and restore the democratically elected government.
o UNAMSIL (UN Mission in Sierra Leone): Nigeria was the largest troop
contributor to the subsequent UN mission.
4. Sudan (2004-2011): Nigeria played a key role in the African Union Mission in Sudan
(AMIS) and the later UN-AU Hybrid Mission in Darfur (UNAMID), sending troops and
police officers.
3.4 BENEFITS AND COSTS OF PEACEKEEPING
 Benefits:
o National Prestige: Enhances Nigeria's reputation as a responsible regional power.
o Military Professionalism: Gives the Nigerian Armed Forces valuable
international experience.
o Regional Stability: Achieved the goal of stabilizing West Africa, which is in
Nigeria's direct interest.
 Costs (Burdens):
o Human Cost: The loss of life of hundreds of brave Nigerian soldiers.
o Financial Cost: Peacekeeping is extremely expensive. Nigeria spent billions of
dollars on ECOMOG, money that some argued could have been used at home.
o Domestic Criticism: Citizens sometimes question why Nigeria is "policing the
world" when it has its own internal security challenges.

WEEK 4: JUSTICE AND FAIRNESS
4.1 INTRODUCTION
Justice and fairness are the cornerstones of a stable, peaceful, and civilized society. They are the
foundation upon which the "social contract" between citizens and the state is built.
4.2 DEFINING KEY CONCEPTS
 Justice: The concept of giving every individual their "due" or "what they deserve." It
involves the impartial application of law, the rewarding of good, and the punishment of
wrong. It is rooted in moral rightness.
 Fairness: The quality of treating people impartially, free from bias, discrimination, or
favouritism. Fairness is about process – ensuring the "rules of the game" are applied
equally to all. You cannot have justice without fairness.
 Equity vs. Equality: This is a crucial SS3-level distinction.
o Equality: Treating everyone the same. Giving everyone the same size shoe.
o Equity: Treating people fairly by giving them what they need to succeed. It is
"levelling the playing field." It means giving everyone a shoe that fits.
o Example: Equality is building a staircase. Equity is building a ramp beside the
staircase so a wheelchair user has the same access as an able-bodied person.
 Social Justice: This moves beyond individual justice. It is justice in society, focusing on
the fair distribution of wealth, opportunities, and privileges.
o Social Justice Issues: Tackling systemic poverty, ensuring equal access to quality
education and healthcare, ending discrimination (ethnic, gender, religious), and
protecting worker's rights.
4.3 THE RULE OF LAW
The "Rule of Law" is the main principle and mechanism for achieving justice and fairness in a
state. It is the opposite of the "Rule of Man" (where a king, dictator, or "big man" can do
whatever they want).
11
The Rule of Law, as defined by A.V. Dicey, has three main pillars:
1. Supremacy of the Law:
o The law is the highest authority in the land; it is supreme.
o No person can be punished unless they have been proven to have breached a law
that was already in place.
o This prevents arbitrary rule (e.g., a leader can't just imprison you because they
dislike you).
2. Equality Before the Law:
o No one is above the law.
o All citizens, from the President to the poorest farmer, are subject to the same laws
and the same court system.
o In Nigeria, this principle is often challenged by "immunity clauses" for high
officials.
3. Presence of Fundamental Human Rights:
o The law must recognize, protect, and enforce the basic rights and freedoms of the
citizens (e.g., those in Chapter IV of the 1999 Constitution).
o This is upheld by an independent judiciary – a court system that is free from
pressure from the government (Executive) or lawmakers (Legislature).
4.4 IMPORTANCE OF JUSTICE, FAIRNESS, AND THE RULE OF LAW
 Peace and Stability: When people feel justly treated, they are less likely to resort to
violence or self-help. Most social unrest (e.g., Niger Delta militancy, #EndSARS protests)
is rooted in a perceived injustice.
 Economic Development: Foreign and domestic investors will only risk their money in a
country where the Rule of Law is strong, contracts are enforced, and they know they will
get a fair hearing in court.
 Protection of Rights: It is the only thing that protects the weak from the strong.
 Good Governance: It holds government leaders accountable for their actions.

WEEK 6: HERITAGE IN MODERN NIGERIA
6.1 INTRODUCTION
Heritage is the "inheritance" of a society. It is the sum of all past traditions, practices, artifacts,
beliefs, and places that are passed down from generation to generation and shape a group's
identity.
6.2 TYPES OF HERITAGE
1. Tangible Heritage (Material):
o Things we can touch and see.
o Artifacts: Objects of cultural or historical significance, e.g., the Nok Terracottas
(one of the oldest sculptures in Sub-Saharan Africa), Benin Bronzes (worldfamous
plaques and sculptures), Igbo-Ukwu bronze works.
o Monuments & Buildings: Physical structures, e.g., the Ancient Kano City
Walls, the Sukur Cultural Landscape (a UNESCO World Heritage Site),
colonial-era buildings in Lagos and Calabar.
o Sites: Locations of historical or spiritual importance, e.g., the Osun-Osogbo
Sacred Grove (a UNESCO World Heritage Site).
2. Intangible Heritage (Non-Material):
o Things we practice and express.
o Traditions & Oral History: Folklore, proverbs, myths, and legends passed down
by word of mouth.
o Social Practices & Festivals: e.g., the Eyo Festival (Lagos), the Argungu
Fishing Festival (Kebbi), New Yam Festivals (Igboland).
o Language: The hundreds of indigenous languages spoken in Nigeria are a key
part of our heritage.
o Performing Arts: Traditional music, dance, and forms of drama.
o Knowledge & Skills: e.g., traditional medicine (herbalism), cloth-weaving
techniques (Aso-Ofi, Akwete), blacksmithing.
6.3 THE INFLUENCE OF TRADITION ON CONTEMPORARY LIFE
15
Our heritage is not just in a museum; it profoundly influences our modern, everyday lives, for
better and for worse.
 Positive Influences:
o Identity & Pride: Serves as a source of identity, history, and cultural pride in a
globalized world.
o Artistic Inspiration: Traditional art, music (e.g., Fela Kuti's use of traditional
rhythms), and fashion (e.g., modern use of Ankara, Adire) are major global
exports.
o Tourism: Festivals and heritage sites attract tourists, creating economic value.
o Social Cohesion: Traditional systems (e.g., respect for elders, community
meetings) still play a strong role in maintaining social order, especially in rural
areas.
o Knowledge: Traditional medicine (herbalism) is often used alongside (or instead
of) modern medicine.
 Negative/Controversial Influences (Challenges):
o Harmful Traditional Practices: Certain practices that are now illegal and violate
human rights, e.g., Female Genital Mutilation (FGM), child marriage, and some
widowhood rites.
o Superstition & "Juju": Beliefs in witchcraft or "juju" can sometimes be used to
explain events (like illness or business failure) that have scientific or economic
causes, hindering progress.
o Ethnic Prejudice: Negative stereotypes and historical prejudices between
different groups are part of an "inherited" mindset that challenges modern national
unity.
o Gender Roles: Traditional patriarchal systems can limit the opportunities and
rights of women in modern society.
6.4 PRESERVING OUR HERITAGE
16
 Role of Institutions: The National Commission for Museums and Monuments
(NCMM), UNESCO (United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural
Organization).
 Methods: Museums, documentation (oral history projects), education, promoting
festivals, repatriating stolen artifacts (like the Benin Bronzes).

WEEK 8: CIVIC ENTREPRENEURSHIP
8.1 INTRODUCTION
This is an advanced, modern concept. Let's break it down:
 Civic: Relates to the community, the citizen, and public life.
 Entrepreneurship: The process of identifying a problem, designing an innovative
solution, taking a risk, and launching a new venture (usually a business) to create value.
 Civic Entrepreneurship: The process of identifying a public or social problem (e.g.,
poor sanitation, lack of data, political apathy) and applying the risk-taking, innovativethinking
mindset of an entrepreneur to create a solution that builds the community or
strengthens the nation.
A civic entrepreneur is an active citizen who doesn't just complain about a problem but creates
the solution.
8.2 CIVIC SKILLS FOR NATION BUILDING
Nation-building requires an active, skilled, and engaged citizenry, not just a competent
government.
 What are Civic Skills?
o Critical Thinking & Problem Analysis: The ability to see a problem (e.t.,
"plastic waste is blocking our gutters") and analyze its root causes.
o Communication & Advocacy: The ability to clearly articulate the problem and
the solution to the public, government, and investors.
o Organizing & Mobilization: The ability to bring people together (e.g.,
volunteers, community members) to work on the solution.
o Financial Literacy & Management: The ability to raise funds, create a budget,
and manage resources transparently (whether for-profit or non-profit).
o Resilience: The "never-give-up" spirit of an entrepreneur, needed to navigate
bureaucracy and setbacks.
19
8.3 EXAMPLES OF CIVIC ENTREPRENEURSHIP IN NIGERIA
This is not theory; it is happening now.
1. Social Enterprises (For-Profit with a Social Mission):
o Example: WeCyclers (Lagos):
 Problem: Poor sanitation, plastic waste blocking drains.
 Solution: A social enterprise that uses low-cost cargo bikes ("Wecycles")
to go into poor neighbourhoods, buy recyclable waste from residents, and
then sell the bulk waste to big recycling companies.
 Impact: Cleans the environment, provides income (nation-building), and
is a sustainable business.
2. Non-Profit Organizations (NGOs) & Advocacy:
o Example: BudgIT (Lagos):
 Problem: Government budgets are complex and non-transparent; citizens
don't know how public money is being spent.
 Solution: An NGO that uses technology, data analysis, and simple
infographics to simplify the budget and track government spending.
 Impact: Empowers citizens (civic skill) to hold government accountable
(nation-building).
o Example: #EnoughIsEnough Nigeria (EiE):
 Problem: Political apathy among youth.
 Solution: A non-partisan group that uses media and technology to
mobilize young people to register to vote, ask questions, and get involved
in politics.
 Impact: Increases political participation and accountability (nationbuilding).
8.4 CIVIC ENTREPRENEURSHIP VS. TRADITIONAL POLITICS
 A traditional politician often asks for your vote to solve a problem.
20
 A civic entrepreneur often asks for your participation (or investment) to solve a
problem themselves.
 Both are necessary for nation-building.

WEEK 9: ETHICS & INTEGRITY
9.1 INTRODUCTION
These two terms are the foundation of good governance and a functional society. They are often
used together, but they are slightly different.
 Ethics: A set of moral principles or a code of conduct that governs the behavior of an
individual or a group (e.g., "Medical Ethics," "Workplace Ethics"). It is the theory of
what is right and wrong.
21
 Integrity: The practice of being honest and showing a consistent, uncompromising
adherence to strong moral and ethical principles. It is "walking the talk," "practicing what
you preach." It is consistency of character.
o A person has integrity if they do the right thing even when no one is watching.
9.2 IMPORTANCE OF ETHICS AND INTEGRITY
1. In Personal Life: Builds trust. A person with integrity is reliable, dependable, and
respected.
2. In Professional Life: A doctor with integrity won't recommend a fake drug. A teacher
with integrity won't take a bribe for marks. An engineer with integrity won't use
substandard materials for a building. It is the basis of professional trust.
3. In Governance (The Most Critical):
o Builds Public Trust: When citizens believe their leaders have integrity, they are
more likely to pay taxes and obey laws.
o Ensures Rule of Law: A judge with integrity cannot be bribed, ensuring fair
justice.
o Promotes Development: Leaders with integrity will use public funds for public
projects (schools, hospitals, roads), not embezzle them.
o Prevents Corruption: Corruption is, by definition, a failure of ethics and a lack
of integrity.
9.3 CHALLENGES TO ETHICS AND INTEGRITY IN NIGERIA
The lack of integrity (i.e., corruption) is arguably Nigeria's single greatest challenge.
1. Corruption: The abuse of entrusted power for private gain. This includes:
o Bribery: Offering money to influence a decision.
o Embezzlement: Stealing public funds.
o Nepotism/Favouritism: Giving jobs and contracts to family/friends, not the most
qualified person.
2. Electoral Malpractice: Rigging elections, snatching ballot boxes, and buying votes are
all massive breaches of integrity in the democratic process.
22
3. "419" & Scams (Advance-Fee Fraud): This has severely damaged Nigeria's
international reputation, which is a failure of ethics on a national scale.
4. "Get Rich Quick" Mentality: A societal pressure that sometimes celebrates wealth
without questioning its source, putting pressure on individuals to compromise their
integrity.
9.4 THE ROLE OF ETHICS & INTEGRITY IN GOVERNANCE
 Good Governance: This is a system of governing that is transparent (clear), accountable
(responsible), and ethical.
 The Civil Service: An ethical civil service implements government policy fairly and
efficiently, based on rules, not bribes.
 The Legislature: Ethical lawmakers will pass laws for the good of the nation, not just for
their region or their pockets.
 Institutions Fighting for Integrity:
o ICPC (Independent Corrupt Practices Commission): Tackles corruption
within the public service.
o EFCC (Economic and Financial Crimes Commission): Tackles money
laundering, scams, and financial crimes.

WEEK 10: NATIONAL REBIRTH & TRANSFORMATION AGENDA
10.1 INTRODUCTION: NATIONAL REBIRTH
"National Rebirth" is a recurring idea in Nigerian politics. It is a call for a fundamental renewal
of the nation's values, spirit, and sense of direction. It is often launched by governments to
mobilize citizens, move away from past failures (like corruption and disunity), and set a new,
positive course.
10.2 HISTORICAL GOVERNMENT INITIATIVES
Almost every Nigerian government (military and civilian) has launched its own "agenda" for
national rebirth.
1. War Against Indiscipline (WAI) (Buhari/Idiagbon Regime, 1984): A military-led
campaign to force social change. It was famous for its harsh enforcement of rules like
queuing at bus stops, sanitation, and patriotism.
2. Mass Mobilisation for Social Justice and Economic Recovery (MAMSER)
(Babangida Regime, 1987): A broad-based "social mobilization" program aimed at
building a new political culture of self-reliance, social justice, and patriotism in
preparation for a return to civilian rule.
3. "Heart of Africa" Project (Obasanjo Regime, 2000s): A rebranding campaign aimed
at improving Nigeria's negative international image and attracting foreign investment.
4. National Orientation Agency (NOA): This is the permanent government agency (still
active today) that is tasked with communicating government policies and promoting
patriotism and positive national values.
10.3 IN-DEPTH: THE TRANSFORMATION AGENDA (2011-2015)
The scheme specifically names this agenda from the Goodluck Jonathan administration.
 Context: It was a medium-term plan to accelerate development and achieve Nigeria's
long-term "Vision 20:2020" (which was a goal to be one of the top 20 economies by
2020).
25
 Core Pillars (Goals): The Transformation Agenda was built on several key pillars:
1. Economic Growth: Prioritizing sectors with high growth potential, like
Agriculture (Agric Transformation Agenda - ATA), manufacturing, and oil/gas
(e.g., the Petroleum Industry Bill).
2. Infrastructure: Major focus on power (electricity) sector privatization, road
construction, and aviation reform.
3. Human Capital Development: Improving education (e.g., creating new federal
universities) and health.
4. Governance: Tackling corruption (though this was seen as its weakest point) and
improving the efficiency of government.
 Subsequent Agendas: The idea continues. The Buhari administration had the "Change"
agenda and the "Economic Recovery and Growth Plan (ERGP)." The current Tinubu
administration has the "Renewed Hope" agenda.
10.4 THE ROLE OF CITIZEN PARTICIPATION
This is the most important link. Government agendas always fail without citizen participation.
A "Transformation Agenda" cannot just be a government document; it must be a national project.
 How Citizens Participate in National Transformation:
1. Economic Participation: Paying taxes (which fund the agenda), starting
businesses (civic entrepreneurship), working hard and with integrity.
2. Political Participation: Voting for leaders based on their ability to implement a
good agenda (not on ethnicity).
3. Accountability & Transparency: This is the most critical role. Citizens (and
groups like BudgIT) must read the agenda, track the projects, and hold the
government accountable for its promises.
4. Personal Transformation: The "War Against Indiscipline" was based on the
idea that national rebirth starts with the individual. A citizen who throws trash in
the gutter, jumps the queue, or offers a bribe is undermining any national agenda.

SS3 CITIZENSHIP AND HERITAGE STUDIES LESSON NOTES

WEEK 1: GLOBAL ISSUES IN CITIZENSHIP
1.1 INTRODUCTION
In the modern, globalized world, the concept of "citizenship" is no longer limited to our
relationship with our own nation-state (Nigeria). We are also Global Citizens, meaning our lives
are interconnected with people and events across the world. Global issues are problems that
transcend national borders; they cannot be solved by one country alone and require international
cooperation. These issues profoundly affect our rights, responsibilities, and very identity as
Nigerian citizens.
1.2 TERRORISM
 Definition: Terrorism is the unlawful use of violence, fear, and intimidation, especially
against civilians, in the pursuit of political, religious, or ideological aims. It is a tool used
by non-state actors (groups) to challenge the state and sow panic.
 The Nigerian Context:
1. Boko Haram: An insurgent group in Northeast Nigeria (Borno, Yobe, Adamawa)
seeking to establish an Islamic caliphate and opposing Western education.
2. ISWAP (Islamic State West Africa Province): A splinter group from Boko
Haram, now affiliated with the global ISIS network.
3. Banditry/Insecurity: While often seen as criminal, large-scale, organized
banditry and kidnapping-for-ransom in the Northwest and North-Central regions
share many characteristics with terrorism (use of fear, challenging the state's
monopoly on violence).
 Impact on Citizenship:
1. Mass Displacement: Terrorism has created millions of Internally Displaced
Persons (IDPs), who have been forced to flee their homes. This makes them
citizens without a community, unable to vote, farm, or access education.
29
2. Erosion of State Trust: When the state fails to protect citizens from terror (its
primary duty), citizens' loyalty (allegiance) to the state weakens.
3. Human Rights Abuses: Terrorism leads to a state of conflict, resulting in abuses
from both the terrorists (killings, abductions) and sometimes from security forces
in their counter-terrorism operations.
4. Restriction of Freedoms: To fight terror, the state often increases security
measures, such as checkpoints, curfews, and surveillance, which can limit
citizens' freedom of movement and association.
1.3 MIGRATION
 Definition: The movement of people from one place to another with the intention of
settling, permanently or temporarily, at a new location.
o Internal Migration: Movement within Nigeria (e.g., rural-to-urban, North-to-
South).
o International Migration: Movement across national borders (e.g., from Nigeria
to Canada, the UK, or Ghana).
 Causes (Push and Pull Factors):
o Push Factors (Forcing people to leave Nigeria): Insecurity, high unemployment,
poor infrastructure (especially power), lack of quality education and healthcare.
o Pull Factors (Attracting people to other countries): Perceived better job
opportunities, higher wages, quality of life, political stability, and educational
opportunities.
 Impact on Citizenship:
1. Brain Drain: This is the emigration (exit) of highly trained or intelligent people (e.g.,
doctors, nurses, engineers, tech experts) from Nigeria. This weakens the Nigerian workforce and
economy, as the state (which may have subsidized their education) loses its best minds.
2. Irregular Migration & "Japa" Syndrome: The desperate, often illegal, migration of
youth (the "Japa" phenomenon) through dangerous routes (e.g., the Sahara Desert and
Mediterranean Sea) leads to exploitation, slavery (e.g., in Libya), and death.
30
3. The Diaspora: Nigerians living abroad form a powerful "Diaspora." They contribute to
the nation by sending remittances (money) back home, which is a major source of foreign
exchange for Nigeria. They also influence global opinions about Nigeria.
1.4 CLIMATE CHANGE
 Definition: A long-term, large-scale shift in the planet's weather patterns or average
temperatures, primarily caused by the burning of fossil fuels (oil, gas, coal) which
releases "greenhouse gases" that trap heat.
 The Nigerian Context: Nigeria is extremely vulnerable to climate change.
1. In the North (Sahel): Desertification and Drought. The Sahara Desert is
expanding southwards, drying up water sources like Lake Chad.
2. In the South (Coastal): Coastal Erosion and Flooding. Rising sea levels
threaten coastal cities like Lagos, Port Harcourt, and Bayelsa.
3. Middle Belt & General: Erratic Rainfall and Extreme Heat. This disrupts the
farming calendar and reduces crop yields.
 Impact on Citizenship:
1. Threat to Livelihoods: It directly threatens the two main pillars of the traditional
economy: farming (affected by drought) and fishing (affected by dying lakes and
flooded deltas).
2. Resource-Based Conflict: Climate change is a major driver of the farmerherder
conflict. As desertification pushes herders south from the dry North in
search of pasture and water, they come into conflict with farmers in the Middle
Belt, leading to violence, displacement, and food insecurity.
3. Environmental Refugees: People forced to migrate not because of war, but
because their land is no longer habitable (e.g., flooded or turned to desert).

WEEK 2: NIGERIA’S ROLE IN INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS
2.1 INTRODUCTION
Nigeria’s foreign policy since independence has been guided by Afrocentrism – the belief that
Nigeria's own destiny is tied to the dignity, liberation, and prosperity of the African continent.
This policy is often visualized as a series of "Concentric Circles," with Nigeria's highest
priority being its immediate neighbours (West Africa), followed by the continent (Africa), and
then the rest of the world. Nigeria plays its role through key international organizations.
2.2 NIGERIA IN ECOWAS (ECONOMIC COMMUNITY OF WEST AFRICAN STATES)
 Role: Nigeria is the "big brother" and most powerful member of ECOWAS, a regional
body for West Africa.
 Contributions:
1. Founding Member: Nigeria, under General Yakubu Gowon, was a primary
architect and founding member in 1975.
2. Economic Hub: Nigeria accounts for over 60% of ECOWAS's population and
GDP. Its economy is the "engine" of the region.
3. Peacekeeping (ECOMOG): Nigeria created, led, and funded ECOMOG
(ECOWAS Cease-fire Monitoring Group) to end the brutal civil wars in Liberia
and Sierra Leone in the 1990s.
4. Promoting Democracy: Nigeria consistently uses its political and economic
weight to pressure countries to return to democracy after military coups (e.g., in
The Gambia 2017, and more recently, in Mali, Guinea, and Niger, though with
less success).
5. Financial Support: Nigeria is the largest financial contributor to the ECOWAS
budget and hosts its headquarters (Abuja).
2.3 NIGERIA IN THE AU (AFRICAN UNION)
 Role: A continental leader and one of the "big five" (along with South Africa, Egypt,
Algeria, and Ethiopia) that shape AU policy.
33
 Contributions:
1. Anti-Apartheid Struggle: Nigeria was a "Frontline State" in the fight against
apartheid in South Africa. It gave financial, diplomatic, and military support to
liberation movements.
2. Founding of OAU/AU: A key founding member of the Organisation of African
Unity (OAU) in 1963, which later became the African Union (AU) in 2002.
3. Peace and Security: Nigeria is a major troop contributor to AU peacekeeping
missions (e.g., in Darfur, Sudan) and a key member of the AU's Peace and
Security Council.
4. NEPAD (New Partnership for Africa's Development): Nigeria's President
Obasanjo was a chief architect of this economic framework for African
development.
2.4 NIGERIA IN THE UN (UNITED NATIONS)
 Role: A prominent "Global South" voice, advocating for the interests of Africa and the
developing world.
 Contributions:
1. Global Peacekeeping: Nigeria has a long and celebrated history of contributing
troops and police to UN peacekeeping missions all over the world (Congo,
Lebanon, Yugoslavia, etc.).
2. High-Level Leadership: Many Nigerians have held top UN posts, including:
 Amina J. Mohammed (current Deputy Secretary-General of the UN).
 Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala (current Director-General of the World Trade
Organization - WTO).
 Tijjani Muhammad-Bande (President of the 74th UN General
Assembly).
3. Advocacy: Nigeria has been a consistent voice at the UN General Assembly,
calling for debt relief, fair trade, and (most notably) a permanent seat for Africa
on the UN Security Council, arguing that the council's current structure is
outdated and does not reflect modern global realities.

WEEK 3: HUMAN RIGHTS PROTECTION IN NIGERIA
3.1 INTRODUCTION
Human Rights are universal, inalienable rights that every person is entitled to simply by virtue of
being human. In Nigeria, these rights are formally guaranteed in Chapter IV of the 1999
Constitution (as amended). These include the Right to Life, Right to Dignity, Right to Fair
Hearing, Freedom of Expression, etc. However, guaranteeing rights on paper is different from
protecting them in practice. Several bodies are tasked with this.
3.2 THE JUDICIARY
 Role: The Judiciary (the court system) is the primary and ultimate protector of human
rights in Nigeria.
 Functions:
1. Interpretation: It is the only body with the power to interpret the Constitution
and say what a "right" means.
2. Enforcement: A citizen whose rights have been violated (e.g., by the police) can
sue the government in court (using the "Fundamental Rights Enforcement
Procedure") to seek redress.
3. Redress: If the court agrees, it can declare the government's action illegal and
order a remedy, such as:
 Release from unlawful detention.
 Payment of damages (compensation).
 A public apology.
4. Judicial Review: The judiciary has the power to strike down any law passed by
the legislature or any action taken by the executive that violates the Constitution.
 Challenges: Judicial independence is often threatened by executive influence, corruption,
slow court processes (justice delayed is justice denied), and high costs of legal fees.
3.3 THE NATIONAL HUMAN RIGHTS COMMISSION (NHRC)
36
 Role: This is the statutory government body (created by an Act) specifically established
to "promote, protect, and enforce" human rights in Nigeria.
 Functions:
1. Investigation: It receives and investigates complaints of human rights violations
from citizens.
2. Monitoring: It monitors the human rights situation in the country, including visits
to prisons, detention centers, and IDP camps.
3. Advisory: It advises the government on policies and laws that relate to human
rights.
4. Enlightenment: It educates the public (like this class) about their fundamental
rights.
5. Redress: The NHRC can (and does) sit as a panel to hear cases and award
compensation to victims (e.g., the panel on police brutality after #EndSARS).
3.4 NON-GOVERNMENTAL ORGANIZATIONS (NGOs) & CIVIL SOCIETY
 Role: These are the "watchdogs" of society. They are private, non-profit groups that
advocate for specific causes, including human rights.
 Functions:
1. Advocacy & "Naming and Shaming": They raise public awareness about
abuses, issue press releases, and "name and shame" government agencies or
corporations that violate rights.
2. Documentation: They independently investigate and document cases of abuse,
creating reports that are used by lawyers, researchers, and international bodies
(like the UN).
o Legal Aid: They provide "pro bono" (free) legal services to victims of human
rights abuses who cannot afford a lawyer.
4. Protests & Mobilization: They organize peaceful protests and campaigns to
demand change (e.g., the #EndSARS movement was driven by civil society).
 Examples in Nigeria:
37
o Socio-Economic Rights and Accountability Project (SERAP): Famous for
suing the government to demand accountability and transparency.
o Federation of International Women Lawyers (FIDA): Focuses on protecting
the rights of women and children.
o Amnesty International, Nigeria: The local chapter of the global human rights
organization.

WEEK 4: SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT GOALS (SDGs)
4.1 CONCEPT
 Definition: The SDGs are a set of 17 global goals adopted by all United Nations member
states in 2015 as a "universal call to action to end poverty, protect the planet, and ensure
that all people enjoy peace and prosperity by the year 2030."
 Background: They replaced the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs), which ran
from 2000-2015.
 Core Principle: The "three pillars" of sustainable development:
1. Economic Sustainability: Building a strong economy that provides jobs.
2. Social Sustainability: Ensuring health, education, and equality for all.
3. Environmental Sustainability: Protecting the planet (climate, water, forests).
 Motto: The central, guiding principle of the SDGs is to "Leave No One Behind."
4.2 RELEVANCE OF THE SDGs TO NIGERIA
The 17 SDGs are not abstract; they are a direct roadmap for solving Nigeria's most pressing
national challenges.
 GOAL 1: No Poverty:
o Relevance: Extremely relevant. Nigeria has one of the world's highest numbers of
people living in extreme poverty. Achieving this goal means lifting millions out
of poverty through jobs, social safety nets, and economic growth.
 GOAL 2: Zero Hunger:
o Relevance: Aims to end hunger and malnutrition. This is vital in a country with
high food inflation, farmer-herder conflicts disrupting food production, and
stunting in children.
 GOAL 3: Good Health and Well-being:
o Relevance: Aims to reduce maternal and child mortality, and fight diseases.
Nigeria's high maternal mortality rate and the "brain drain" of doctors make this
goal a top priority.
 GOAL 4: Quality Education:
39
o Relevance: Aims for inclusive and equitable quality education. This directly
addresses Nigeria's massive "out-of-school children" crisis, the poor state of
public universities, and the need for skills-based education.
 GOAL 5: Gender Equality:
o Relevance: Aims to empower women and girls. This tackles issues like child
marriage, Female Genital Mutilation (FGM), low political representation of
women, and the "gender pay gap."
 GOAL 7: Affordable and Clean Energy:
o Relevance: This is central to Nigeria's economic survival. It means solving the
"generator" problem by providing stable electricity, ideally from a mix of sources
including solar.
 GOAL 8: Decent Work and Economic Growth:
o Relevance: Addresses the massive youth unemployment problem.
 GOAL 10: Reduced Inequalities:
o Relevance: Aims to reduce the massive gap between the rich and the poor, which
is a source of social instability.
 GOAL 16: Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions:
o Relevance: This is arguably the most important goal for Nigeria. It is about
ending violence (insecurity), fighting corruption, and building a government,
police, and judiciary that are transparent, accountable, and trusted by the people.
Without Goal 16, it is impossible to achieve the other 16.

WEEK 6: YOUTH & CITIZENSHIP
6.1 INTRODUCTION
According to the Nigerian National Youth Policy, a "youth" is a person between the ages of 15
and 29. By this definition, Nigeria has one of the largest youth populations in the world. This
"youth bulge" can be a "demographic dividend" (a source of energy, innovation, and growth)
or a "demographic time bomb" (a source of instability and unrest) depending on how youth are
engaged as citizens.
6.2 ROLE OF YOUTH IN DEMOCRACY
1. As Voters: Youths (18-35) consistently make up the largest demographic on the voter
register. Their collective decision can (and does) decide the outcome of elections.
2. As Advocates (Voice): Youths are masters of modern advocacy.
o Social Media: Using platforms like X (Twitter), Instagram, and TikTok to raise
awareness, critique government, and mobilize for a cause.
o Protest: The #EndSARS protest of October 2020 was a powerful, youth-led
movement against police brutality that captured global attention, demonstrating
the power of youth mobilization.
3. As Political Candidates: The "Not Too Young to Run" Act (passed in 2018) was a
major victory for youth advocacy. It lowered the age requirements to run for office (e.g.,
President from 40 to 35, House of Reps from 30 to 25), opening the door for more young
people to participate directly as candidates.
4. Challenges: Youths are also vulnerable to being manipulated.
o Political Thuggery: Being hired by politicians to snatch ballot boxes or
intimidate opponents.
o Voter Apathy: A feeling of cynicism ("my vote won't count") that leads to low
youth voter turnout.
o Disinformation: Being targets of "fake news" designed to incite anger or
confusion.
6.3 ROLE OF YOUTH IN INNOVATION
43
1. The Tech Ecosystem ("Silicon Lagoon"): Nigeria's tech boom is almost entirely youthled.
o Fintech: Young Nigerians founded "Unicorns" (companies valued over $1 billion)
like Paystack and Flutterwave, which have revolutionized digital payments in
Africa.
o Civic Tech: Using tech to solve social problems. e.g., BudgIT (founded by Seun
Onigbinde) uses tech to simplify government budgets so citizens can demand
accountability.
2. The Creative Industry: This is Nigeria's greatest "soft power" export.
o Afrobeats: Youthful artists like Wizkid, Burna Boy, Davido, and Rema have
turned Nigerian music into a globally dominant genre.
o Nollywood: The film industry is driven by young writers, directors, and actors.
o Fashion & Arts: Young designers and artists are setting global trends.
6.4 ROLE OF YOUTH IN VOLUNTEERING
 Definition: Volunteering is the act of giving one's time and skills freely (without pay) to
help the community or a cause.
 Impact on Citizenship:
1. National Service (NYSC): This is Nigeria's mandatory volunteer program. Its
primary goal is to foster national unity by sending young graduates to different
parts of the country, promoting inter-ethnic understanding.
2. Community Building: Youth-led NGOs and community groups often organize
"clean-up" days, visit orphanages, or run skills training, filling gaps that the
government cannot.
3. Skill Development: Volunteering is a way for youths to build leadership,
teamwork, and empathy – all key skills for effective citizenship.

WEEK 8: CHALLENGES OF NIGERIAN CITIZENSHIP
8.1 INTRODUCTION
While it is a duty to be a "good citizen," there are significant obstacles in the Nigerian
environment that make this difficult. These challenges often lead to apathy, cynicism, and a
weak "social contract" between the citizen and the state.
8.2 CORRUPTION
 Definition: The abuse of entrusted power for private gain.
 Impact on Citizenship:
1. Erosion of Trust: When citizens see leaders embezzling funds meant for
hospitals and roads, they lose all faith in the government and the "Nigerian
project."
2. Creates Inequality: Corruption creates a small, super-rich elite and a massive
poor population, destroying social justice.
3. Perverts Values: It creates a culture where "who you know" (nepotism) is more
important than "what you know" (merit). It makes honest work seem foolish.
4. Discourages Duties: A citizen who is forced to pay a bribe for a passport, a
driver's license, or to get their child into school feels exploited by the state, not
protected by it. This makes them less willing to perform their duties (like paying
taxes).
8.3 INSECURITY
 Definition: A state of fear or danger; the absence of protection from harm.
 Context: This is now a nationwide problem, including terrorism, banditry, kidnappingfor-
ransom, farmer-herder clashes, and urban crime.
 Impact on Citizenship:
1. Failure of the Social Contract: According to political philosophy, the primary
reason citizens give up their freedoms to a state is for protection. When the state
fails to provide security, the social contract is broken.
47
2. Restricts Rights: It destroys the fundamental Right to Life and the Right to
Freedom of Movement. Citizens can no longer travel freely on major highways or
live safely in their own homes.
3. Economic Ruin: Insecurity has crippled the agricultural sector, as farmers are
unable to go to their farms, leading to food scarcity and inflation.
8.4 POOR LEADERSHIP
 Definition: A lack of vision, integrity, competence, and empathy from those in positions
of authority.
 Impact on Citizenship:
1. The "Root" Problem: Poor leadership is the source of corruption and insecurity.
It is the failure of leaders to make tough, good, and just decisions for the entire
nation.
2. Divisiveness: Leaders who use ethnicity, religion, or regionalism to gain power
("divide and rule") actively destroy national unity.
3. Kills Patriotism: Patriotism (love for one's country) is difficult to maintain when
leaders show no love for the country themselves, only for their own bank accounts.
4. Promotes Apathy: Citizens become so "used to" bad governance that they give
up hope and disengage from the political process, believing "nothing will ever
change."

WEEK 9: STRATEGIES FOR STRENGTHENING CITIZENSHIP
9.1 INTRODUCTION
Knowing the challenges from Week 8 is not enough. An effective SS3 citizen must be able to
propose and analyze solutions. Strengthening citizenship means empowering citizens with
knowledge and creating an environment where their participation is meaningful.
9.2 EDUCATION (THE FOUNDATIONAL STRATEGY)
 Role: This is the most important long-term strategy.
1. Civic Knowledge: Education (specifically, this class) is where citizens learn
about their rights, their duties, the constitution, and how government should work.
An ignorant citizenry cannot demand accountability.
2. Critical Thinking: Education teaches citizens how to analyze problems, spot
"fake news," and evaluate the promises of politicians.
3. National Unity: Shared education in public schools can (in theory) help build a
common Nigerian identity that transcends ethnic and religious lines.
4. Empowerment: Education is the primary tool for economic empowerment,
lifting people out of poverty and making them less susceptible to being bought by

 Key Examples:

9.4 ROLE OF THE FAMILY
 Role: The family is the "first school" of citizenship. It is the basic unit of society where
values are transferred.
 Functions:
1. Instilling Core Values: The family is where a child first learns the difference

for others are (or should be) taught at home.
2. Countering Corruption: A family that teaches its children that "get-rich-quick"
is wrong, and that a good name is better than ill-gotten wealth, is building a
foundation for an incorruptible citizen.
3. Teaching Responsibility: Giving children chores and holding them accountable
for their actions teaches personal responsibility, which is the bedrock of civic
responsibility.

WEEK 10: CIVIC COMPETENCE FOR NATION BUILDING
10.1 INTRODUCTION
"Civic Competence" is the complete set of tools an individual needs to be an effective, active,
and responsible citizen. It's the "capacity" to participate in and improve one's community and
nation. Nation-building is not a job for the government alone; it is a result of millions of
competent citizens. This competence is a mix of three things: Knowledge, Skills, and Values.
10.2 CIVIC KNOWLEDGE (THE "WHAT")
This is the information and understanding a citizen must have.
 Constitutional Knowledge: Knowing your rights (Chapter IV) and your duties (Section
24).
 Political Knowledge: Understanding the structure of government (3 branches: Executive,
Legislature, Judiciary) and the levels of government (3 tiers: Federal, State, Local).
 Historical Knowledge: Understanding Nigeria's history (e.g., amalgamation, civil war,
military rule) to know why we face our current challenges.
 Issue Knowledge: Being informed about current events and the major problems (e.g., the
basics of the economy, climate change, insecurity).
10.3 CIVIC SKILLS (THE "HOW")
This is the practical ability to use knowledge to get things done.
 Critical Thinking & Analysis: The ability to analyze problems, separate fact from
opinion, and identify "fake news."
 Communication: The ability to speak and write clearly, persuasively, and respectfully
(e.g., writing a letter to your LGA Chairman, a newspaper, or your representative).
 Advocacy & Mobilization: The skill of organizing people around a common goal (e.g.,
starting a petition, organizing a peaceful protest, running a social media campaign).
 Collaboration: The ability to work with people from different ethnic or religious
backgrounds to achieve a common good.
53
 Financial Literacy: The ability to understand a budget (personal or public) and demand
accountability.
10.4 CIVIC VALUES (THE "WHY")
This is the moral compass or disposition that motivates a citizen to act for the common good, not
just for themselves.
 Integrity & Honesty: The foundation.
 Justice & Fairness: A deep-seated belief in the Rule of Law and in "equity" for all.
 Tolerance & Respect for Diversity: A fundamental requirement for a multi-ethnic
nation. It means respecting the rights of others to be different (in religion, tribe, opinion)
without resorting to hatred or violence.
 Patriotism: This is not blind loyalty ("my country, right or wrong"). It is a constructive
patriotism – a deep love for one's country that includes the courage to criticize it and the
desire to improve it.
 Responsibility: An "ownership" mindset. A competent citizen sees a problem (e.g., litter
on the street) and feels a personal responsibility to act (e.g., organize a clean-up), rather
than just saying "it's the government's job."
Conclusion: A citizen with Knowledge but no Skills or Values is a passive critic. A citizen with
Skills but no Values can be a corrupt politician. A citizen with Values but no Knowledge can be
easily manipulated. True Civic Competence is the combination of all three.

WEEK 2: NATIONAL CONSCIOUSNESS
2.1 INTRODUCTION
This week, we are moving from "National Identity" to a deeper, more profound concept:
"National Consciousness."
 National Identity: This is the passive feeling of belonging to a nation. It is the answer to
"Who are you?" ("I am a Nigerian"). It is based on symbols (flag, anthem) and a passport.
 National Consciousness: This is the active, internalized awareness and commitment to
the nation's collective interest. It is the answer to "What must we do?" ("We, as Nigerians,
must solve this problem"). It is the mindset that places the nation's success above
personal, ethnic, or religious gain.
In short: Identity is a status; Consciousness is a mindset.
2.2 IMPORTANCE OF NATIONAL CONSCIOUSNESS
National consciousness is the "social glue" that allows a diverse, multi-ethnic state like Nigeria
to function.
1. Antidote to Disunity: It is the only force capable of overcoming the "big three" divisions:
Ethnicity (Tribalism), Religious Intolerance, and Regionalism.
2. Curbing Corruption: A leader with national consciousness sees public funds as "our"
money for "our" development, not "my" money to be stolen. A citizen with national
consciousness will refuse to bribe or be bribed, seeing it as an act of theft from the nation.
3. Building Public Good: It is the mindset that makes citizens pay taxes willingly, knowing
the money will build schools for all. It's the mindset that makes a citizen in Lagos care
about the security of a citizen in Borno, seeing them as "one of us."
4. Promoting Merit: National consciousness leads to Meritocracy (rule by the best) over
Nepotism (favouring family/tribe). You hire the best engineer for the job, regardless of
their state of origin, because you want the nation's bridge to be strong.
2.3 HOW TO DEVELOP NATIONAL CONSCIOUSNESS
61
This mindset is not automatic; it must be built deliberately.
1. Top-Down (Government-led) Strategies:
o Good Governance: This is the most important. The state must be seen to be fair,
just, and equitable. When citizens see justice (Week 4, Term 1), they believe in
the nation.
o National Institutions: Using institutions to force integration. The NYSC is the
prime example, where youth are posted across the country.
o National Symbols: The flag, anthem, and pledge, when backed by action, can
inspire unity.
o Federal Character Principle: (As enshrined in the Constitution) The idea is to
ensure no single ethnic group dominates government, thereby giving all groups a
"sense of belonging." However, it is often criticized when it sacrifices merit.
2. Bottom-Up (Citizen-led) Strategies:
o Education: This class (Citizenship Education) is a primary tool. Teaching history
(a shared story) and civic values.
o Inter-Marriage & Interaction: Social mixing between ethnic and religious
groups naturally breaks down stereotypes.
o The Media: Nollywood, Afrobeats, and national newspapers have a role in
creating a shared "Nigerian culture" and telling stories that unite us.
o Common Experiences: Shared moments of national pride (like winning the
AFCON) or national tragedy (which can bring people together in shared grief)
forge consciousness.
2.4 CHALLENGES TO NATIONAL CONSCIOUSNESS
 Poor Leadership: When leaders are openly corrupt or ethnically biased, they destroy
national consciousness and signal that "it's a game of 'us' vs. 'them'."
 Injustice & Inequality: A massive gap between the rich and poor, or the perception that
the justice system only punishes the poor, makes it hard to feel "all in this together."
 Ethno-Religious Politics: Politicians who "play the ethnic card" to win votes are actively
sabotaging national consciousness for personal gain.

WEEK 6: LEGACY OF NIGERIAN HERITAGE
6.1 INTRODUCTION
This week, we synthesize the "Heritage" aspect of our subject. Our heritage is our "inheritance" –
the sum of all the tangible and intangible traditions, arts, and beliefs passed down to us. As an
SS3 student, you are not just a receiver of this heritage; you are now a custodian of it. You have
to decide what to preserve, what to modernize, and what to abandon.
6.2 CONTINUITY: WHAT HAS SURVIVED?
Despite colonization, globalization, and modernization, core parts of our heritage remain strong
and continue to define us:
 Intangible Heritage:
o Languages: Hundreds of local languages are still the "mother tongue" for
millions.
o Social Values: A deep respect for elders, the importance of family (immediate
and extended), and a strong sense of community.
o Festivals & Ceremonies: Naming ceremonies, traditional marriages, and major
festivals (Eyo, New Yam, Argungu) are still widely practiced.
o Cuisine: Our national and regional foods (Jollof, Amala, Fufu, Miyan Kuka) are a
powerful, unifying part of our daily heritage.
 Tangible Heritage:
o Traditional Rulership: The Obas, Emirs, Obis, and other traditional rulers still
hold significant (though non-political) influence and are custodians of tradition.
o Art Forms: Crafts like weaving (Aso-Ofi, Akwete), pottery, and carving continue,
even if in new forms.
6.3 PRESERVATION: SAVING OUR PAST FOR THE FUTURE
 Why Preserve?
71
1. Identity: "A people without a past are a people without a future." Our heritage is
our unique identity in a globalized world.
2. Economic Value: Heritage is a product. Tourism (festivals, museums, sites) and
Creative Industries (Afrobeats, Fashion) are multi-billion dollar industries built
on our heritage.
3. Knowledge: Traditional medicine, farming practices, and proverbs contain deep
wisdom.
 How to Preserve?
o Government: Through institutions like the National Commission for Museums
and Monuments (NCMM) and by protecting UNESCO World Heritage Sites
(Osun-Osogbo Grove, Sukur Landscape).
o Citizens (Your Role):
1. Speak Your Language: The most effective way to preserve it.
2. Documentation: You can preserve heritage. Interview your grandparents.
Record their stories, your family history, and local folktales.
3. Participate: Attend festivals, learn a traditional craft.
6.4 MODERNIZATION: ADAPTING HERITAGE (THE CRITICAL BALANCE)
Heritage is not static; it must adapt or it dies. Modernization is the process of updating heritage
to remain relevant.
 Successful Modernization (Integration):
o Music (Afrobeats): Fela Kuti, Burna Boy, etc., modernized traditional rhythms,
languages, and social commentary into a global product.
o Fashion: Modern designers using Ankara, Adire, and Aso-Ofi to create worldclass
fashion.
o Nollywood: Using modern film technology to tell traditional (and modern)
Nigerian stories.
 The Challenge (Harmful Practices vs. Heritage):
72
o This is the most difficult part. As a modern, educated citizen, you must
differentiate between culture and a human rights violation.
o Heritage: New Yam Festival, respect for elders, traditional dances.
o Harmful Practices (To be abandoned): Female Genital Mutilation (FGM), child
marriage, certain widowhood rites, tribal-mark scarification (that harms).
o Your Role: You must be the agent of change within your culture, using your
education and respect to advocate for the end of harmful practices while
promoting the positive aspects.

9.1 INTRODUCTION
This week is 100% focused on deconstructing the SSCE (WASSCE/NECO) examination. You

9.2 UNDERSTANDING THE EXAM STRUCTURE
 Paper 1: Objective (Multiple Choice Questions - MCQ)
o Format: 50 questions, 50 marks, ~50 minutes.
o Tests: Breadth of knowledge. It covers the entire 3-year syllabus. A small detail
from an SS1 topic can appear.
o Strategy:
1. Read Carefully: Watch for "NOT," "EXCEPT," "ALL."
2. Eliminate: Instantly cross out the 2-3 obviously wrong answers.
3. Don't Get Stuck: If a question is too hard, circle it and come back.
4. No Gaps: Answer every question, even if you have to guess (no negative
marking).
 Paper 2: Essay (Theory / Structured Questions)
o Format: Usually 5 or 6 questions. You must answer 3 or 4 (e.g., "Answer 4
questions in all. Answer 1 from Section A and 3 from Section B").
o Tests: Depth of knowledge. Your ability to explain, analyze, and apply.
o Strategy: This is where you win or lose.
9.3 HOW TO ANSWER ESSAY QUESTIONS (THE 10-MARK FORMULA)
Most essay questions are worth 8-12 marks.
 "List 4..." or "State 5...": This is a list question. Do not write an essay. Just list them.
 "Explain 4..." or "Discuss 3...": This is an explanation question. This is the main type.
DO NOT just list.
 The Winning Formula:
77
1. Introduction (2 marks): Start by defining the key concept in the question.
 Q: "Explain 4 challenges to the Rule of Law in Nigeria."
 Intro: "The Rule of Law is a principle that all citizens, including the
government, are subject to the law... However, in Nigeria, its practice is
hindered by several challenges."
2. Body (4-5 points) (6-7 marks):
 State your point clearly. (e.g., "1. Corruption and Bribery:")
 Explain the point. (e.g., "This is when public officials, like judges or
police, take bribes. This undermines the Rule of Law...").
 Give a Nigerian Example! (This is essential). (e.g., "...for instance, a
judge who takes a bribe to free a guilty politician is destroying 'equality
before the law'.")
 Repeat this 4-5 times.
3. Conclusion (1 mark): A simple summary. ("In conclusion, these challenges...
must be addressed...")

WEEK 10: LIFE AFTER SCHOOL: THE LIFELONG CITIZEN
10.1 INTRODUCTION: THIS IS NOT A FAREWELL
This is your final lesson. The key takeaway of this entire subject is that "Citizenship" is not a
class you take; it is a life you live. Everything you have learned now becomes your personal
responsibility to practice.
10.2 YOUR CIVIC LIFE: THE NEXT STAGES
1. As a Tertiary Student (University, Poly, College):
o Student Union Politics: This is a micro-democracy. You will have a chance to
practice voting, debating, and even running for office (e.g., SUG President). You
will see how it mirrors national politics (propaganda, debates, and sadly,
sometimes, corruption).
o Advocacy: You will encounter real-world issues (e.g., ASUU strikes, poor hostel
conditions). This is your chance to practice advocacy and peaceful protest.
o Integrity: You will face immense pressure. The choice to reject exam malpractice
and cultism is a civic choice that builds your character (integrity).
2. As a Worker, Professional, or Business Owner:
o Your Workplace: You will face ethical dilemmas. Will you be the one person in
the office who insists on integrity? Will you be the entrepreneur who pays fair
wages and quality products?
o Paying Taxes: Your responsibility to pay taxes becomes real. This is your
investment in the nation.
o Accountability: Because you pay taxes, you now have the power and the right to
demand accountability from your Local Government Chairman to the President.
3. As a Parent and Family Member:
o The "First School": You will become the primary teacher of citizenship. Your
children will learn their first values (honesty, tolerance, hard work) from you.
o The Cycle: You will be the one passing on your "heritage," and you will have to
choose what values to pass on.

10.3 FINAL CHARGE: PROBLEMS AS OPPORTUNITIES
You are graduating in a challenging time. It is easy to look at Nigeria's problems (Weeks 8 & 9,
Term 2) and feel cynical or apathetic.
 Apathy: The belief that "nothing I do matters."
 Cynicism: The belief that "everyone is corrupt, so I must be too."
The mindset of a "Competent Citizen" (Week 10, Term 2) is the opposite. This mindset sees
a problem as an opportunity for action.
 Insecurity is an opportunity for better community policing and justice.
 Corruption is an opportunity for transparency and accountability (like BudgIT).
 Poverty (SDG 1) is an opportunity for social entrepreneurship.
 Disunity is an opportunity for you to be a bridge-builder, to be the one who makes a
friend from another tribe, and to hire based on merit.
Your country needs nation-builders. This subject was your invitation. Your civic life, and your
final exam, begins now. Good luck.
