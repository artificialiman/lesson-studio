Chapter One

Why Glucose, Not Hexose

There are two completely different naming systems running in parallel. One describes structure. The other describes identity. Hexose is a category. Glucose is a name.

This confusion costs marks every year. Hexose means "6-carbon sugar." It is a structural category, like saying "6-door vehicle." Glucose, fructose, and galactose are all hexoses — but they are completely different compounds with different properties, different reactions, and different roles in the body.

The names glucose, fructose, and sucrose come from Greek and Latin roots describing taste, source, or discovery — not structure. They predate IUPAC. They stuck because biochemistry adopted them before systematic naming existed.

The core distinction
-ose tells you the class. The prefix (gluc-, fruc-, galact-) tells you the specific molecule. One is a category. The other is an identity.

The structural classification system runs separately: triose (3C), tetrose (4C), pentose (5C), hexose (6C), heptose (7C). And within each: aldose (aldehyde group) vs ketose (ketone group). Glucose = aldohexose. Fructose = ketohexose. JAMB occasionally tests this distinction directly.

  • Monosaccharide — single sugar unit, cannot be hydrolysed further
  • Disaccharide — two monosaccharides joined by condensation (−H₂O)
  • Polysaccharide — many units: starch, glycogen, cellulose (all glucose, different bonds)
  • Starch vs Cellulose — same monomer (glucose), different glycosidic bond. One digestible, one not.

Chapter Three

Suffixes — The Personality Tag

The suffix names the functional group. The functional group is the only part of the molecule that reacts. Master the suffix, master the reaction.

SuffixGroupStructurePersonality / Exam fact
−aneAlkaneC−C single bonds onlySaturated. Substitution only (UV light). Combustion.
−eneAlkeneC=C double bondUnsaturated. Addition reactions. Decolourises bromine water.
−yneAlkyneC≡C triple bondHighly unsaturated. Addition × 2. Ethyne = acetylene.
−olAlcohol−OHPolar. Oxidised → aldehyde → carboxylic acid. Dehydrated → alkene.
−alAldehyde−CHO (end carbon)Reducing agent. Positive Fehling's/Tollens'. Oxidised → acid.
−oneKetone−C=O (middle carbon)Not a reducing agent. Negative Fehling's. This is the JAMB trap.
−anoic acidCarboxylic acid−COOHAcidic. Forms esters with alcohols. Neutralises bases → salt + water.
−anoateEster−COO−Oil / fragrant. Condensation product. Hydrolysed back to acid + alcohol.
−amine / −amineAmine−NH₂Basic. Fishy smell. Reacts with acids to form ammonium salts.
−oate (as ester name)Esteralkyl + anoateNamed: alcohol part first (alkyl), then acid part (−anoate).
Aldehyde vs Ketone — the single most tested distinction
Both have C=O. Aldehyde: the carbonyl is at the END of the chain (−CHO). Ketone: the carbonyl is in the MIDDLE. Only aldehyde reduces Fehling's solution to brick-red Cu₂O. Ketone does nothing.

The ether suffix is an exception — it has no standard IUPAC suffix. Ethers are named as alkoxy + alkane: CH₃OCH₃ = methoxymethane, CH₃OC₂H₅ = methoxyethane. Common name is just "ether" (meaning diethyl ether specifically). Ethers are isomers of alcohols with the same molecular formula — JAMB exploits this.

Chapter Five

Functional Group Priority

When a molecule has more than one functional group, priority determines the suffix (main name) and what gets a prefix. The highest priority group wins the suffix. Everything else becomes a prefix.

This is why amino acids are called amino acids and not carboxylic amines — the acid group has higher priority than the amine, so it wins the suffix (−oic acid). The amine becomes a prefix (amino−).

1
Carboxylic Acid −COOH
Suffix: −anoic acid  |  Highest priority always
Example: CH₃COOH = ethanoic acid (not hydroxyethanal)
2
Ester −COO−
Suffix: −anoate  |  Named before naming the alkyl group
Example: CH₃COOC₂H₅ = ethyl ethanoate
3
Aldehyde −CHO
Suffix: −al  |  Always at carbon-1 (end of chain)
Example: HCHO = methanal (formaldehyde)
4
Ketone C=O
Suffix: −one  |  Position number required (propan-2-one)
Example: CH₃COCH₃ = propanone (no number needed — only one possible position)
5
Alcohol −OH
Suffix: −ol  |  Position number required for 3+ carbon chains
Example: CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ = propan-2-ol
6
Amine −NH₂
Suffix: −amine  |  Prefix: amino− when outranked
Example: NH₂CH₂COOH = aminoethanoic acid (glycine) — acid wins
7
Alkene / Alkyne C=C / C≡C
Suffix: −ene / −yne  |  Lowest among common groups
Example: CH₂=CHOH = ethenol (not vinyl alcohol — though both used)
The one rule
Higher priority group = suffix. Lower priority groups = prefixes. The carbon carrying the highest priority group gets the lowest possible number.

Why common names survived IUPAC

IUPAC systematised organic naming in the 1900s, long after biochemistry, industry, and commerce had already named everything. Glucose, acetic acid, acetone, glycerol — these were named centuries earlier by observation (taste, smell, source). Industry kept them because they're shorter and already embedded in labels, regulations, and supply chains. JAMB tests both systems because both appear in real Nigerian lab and industrial contexts. Know the IUPAC name to understand structure. Know the common name to recognise it in context.

The prefix tells you the size. The suffix tells you the personality. The priority tells you what wins when both compete.
