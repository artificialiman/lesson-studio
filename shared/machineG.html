1.1 What a Machine Is

A machine is any device that makes work easier by changing the magnitude or direction of a force. A machine does not create energy — it only redirects or reshapes it. This is the single governing fact of the entire topic.

FunctionMeaningExample
Force multiplierIncreases the applied effort so a smaller force moves a larger load.Crowbar, lever, hydraulic jack.
Speed multiplierIncreases the distance or speed of the output.Bicycle gears, fishing rod.
Direction changerRedirects force to a more convenient direction.Single fixed pulley.

Force multiplier vs speed multiplier — the one rule that separates them: a machine that multiplies force must move the effort a greater distance than the load. A machine that multiplies speed moves the load a greater distance than the effort. No machine multiplies both at the same time; that would break conservation of energy.

1.3 The Master Relationship

Work output is always a fraction of work input in any real machine. Dividing each side by the effort and distance that produced it gives the relation that governs every machine problem:

MA = η × VR
η = (MA / VR) × 100%
VR = MA / η

When the machine is ideal (η = 1), MA = VR. When the machine is real (η < 1), MA < VR. In practice, MA is always less than VR, because friction and the weight of the machine's own parts consume part of the input.

1.4 Efficiency and Its Losses

Efficiency is always below 100% in real machines, for three reasons:

  1. Friction between moving parts converts some input work into heat.
  2. The machine's own weight — pulleys, levers and blocks must themselves be lifted, consuming work.
  3. Deformation or vibration absorbs energy that never reaches the load.
Boundary fact: no machine can have efficiency above 100%. A claimed figure above 100% indicates a measurement error or a violation of the conservation of energy. Efficiency = 100% is only achievable in the ideal case where friction and internal weight are zero.

1.5 The Lever

A lever is a rigid bar that rotates about a fixed point called the fulcrum (or pivot). The three orders differ by what lies between what.

OrderArrangementExamples
First orderFulcrum between effort and load.Crowbar, seesaw, scissors, pliers.
Second orderLoad between fulcrum and effort.Wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener.
Third orderEffort between fulcrum and load.Fishing rod, forearm, tweezers, broom.
Law of moments: E × a = L × b
Velocity Ratio: VR = a / b

Where a is the distance from fulcrum to effort (effort arm) and b is the distance from fulcrum to load (load arm).

1.6 Exceptions and Traps

Exception 1 — the lever's own weight. A lever is not a pure MA = VR device when its own weight matters. The lever's weight acts as an extra load at its centre of gravity. In long crowbars or beams, this can substantially reduce the effective mechanical advantage and must be accounted for in accurate work.
Exception 2 — classification is structural, not visual. The order of a lever is determined by the arrangement of L, E and F — not by the shape of the object. A pair of scissors is a first-order lever (fulcrum between effort and load) even though it looks like a cutting tool. A broom is a third-order lever (effort between fulcrum and load) even though it looks like a rigid bar.
Structural fact: a second-order lever always has MA > 1. A third-order lever always has MA < 1. These are not design choices — they follow necessarily from the geometry.
Common trap: confusing MA with efficiency. MA can be greater than, equal to, or less than 1. Efficiency is always ≤ 100%. A machine can have MA = 0.5 (third-order lever) and still be efficient — these measure different things.

Key Vocabulary

  • Machine — device that changes the magnitude or direction of a force.
  • Load (L) — the force the machine overcomes.
  • Effort (E) — the force applied to the machine.
  • Fulcrum — the fixed pivot of a lever.
  • Mechanical Advantage (MA) — load divided by effort.
  • Velocity Ratio (VR) — effort distance divided by load distance.
  • Efficiency (η) — work output as a percentage of work input.
  • Force multiplier — machine that increases the applied force.
  • Speed multiplier — machine that increases the output speed.
  • Ideal machine — a machine with no friction or internal weight (η = 100%).
