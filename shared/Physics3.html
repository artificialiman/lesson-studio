WEEK 1Conversion of Energy

1.1 Definitions and the Law

TermDefinition / statement
EnergyThe capacity to do work. Scalar quantity. Unit: joule (J).
WorkForce × distance moved in the direction of the force. 1 J = 1 N × 1 m.
PowerRate of doing work or converting energy. Unit: watt (W) = 1 J s⁻¹.
Energy conversionThe change of energy from one form to another by a device (converter / transducer).
Conservation of energyEnergy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be changed from one form to another. The total energy of an isolated system is constant.
EfficiencyThe fraction of the energy input that appears as useful output. Never 100%, because part of the input is dissipated as heat (friction, resistance) and sound.
Energy input = Useful energy output + Wasted energy (heat + sound)

1.3 Derivations

  • Work–energy theorem: F = ma and v² = u² + 2as, so as = (v² − u²)/2. Then Fs = mas = ½mv² − ½mu². Work done = change in KE.
  • Conservation in free fall: at height h and at rest, E = mgh. After falling h, v² = 2gh, so KE = ½m(2gh) = mgh. PE lost = KE gained. v = √(2gh), independent of mass.
  • Power = force × velocity: P = W/t = Fd/t = Fv.
  • Overall efficiency of two stages: E₁ = η₁E₀ leaves the first stage; E₂ = η₂E₁ = η₁η₂E₀ leaves the second.

1.5 Energy Chains

Thermal station: Chemical (fuel) → Heat (boiler) → KE (steam) → Mechanical (turbine) → Electrical (generator)

WEEK 2Transmission Systems

2.1 Concept and Definitions

TermMeaning
TransmissionThe transfer of power, motion or energy from the source (prime mover) to the point where it is used.
Driver / drivenThe driver is the part connected to the source (effort side). The driven part delivers the output (load side).
Velocity ratio of a driveVR = speed of driver ÷ speed of driven = Ndriver / Ndriven. VR < 1: speed increased, torque reduced. VR > 1: speed reduced, torque increased.
Transmission efficiencyPower delivered by the driven part ÷ power supplied to the driver.
TorqueTurning effect of a force: τ = F × r (N m).
Prime mover (engine / motor) → Transmission (shaft, gears, belt, chain) → Working part (wheels, blades, machine)

2.2 Mechanical Transmission

ElementHow it transmitsDirectionKey points
GearsMeshing teeth push each other; no slip. Spur, helical, bevel (shafts at an angle), worm (large reduction, self-locking), rack and pinion (rotation ↔ straight line).Meshing gears turn in opposite directions; an idler gear reverses the direction without changing the ratio.Most efficient, fixed ratio, needs lubrication, noisy if worn.
Belt driveFriction between belt and pulley. Open belt: same direction. Crossed belt: opposite direction.As described.Can slip, so ratio is not exact. Quiet, cheap, protects machine by slipping on overload. V-belt grips better.
Chain driveChain links engage sprocket teeth; no slip.Same direction.Keeps timing (bicycle, motorcycle, engine cam chain). Needs oiling and correct tension.
Shaft / couplingRotating rod carries torque between machines in line (gearbox → propeller shaft → differential).Same.Misalignment gives vibration.
Lever linkageRigid bar transfers force and motion about a pivot (brake pedal, gear stick).Depends on pivot position.See Week 3.

WEEK 3Uses of Machines

3.1 Definition and Functions

A machine is a device that makes work easier by changing the size, direction or speed of an applied force. A machine does not create energy; it only transfers it.

FunctionExampleEffect
Multiplies forceCrowbar, hydraulic jack, screw jackSmall effort moves a large load (effort moves farther)
Multiplies speed / distanceTongs, fishing rod, bicycle gearsLarge effort, small load, but load moves farther or faster
Changes directionFixed pulley (flagpole)Pull down to lift up
ConvenienceWheelbarrow, can openerSafer or easier position of effort

3.2 Quantities and Formulae

QuantityDefinitionFormula
Load, LForce the machine overcomes (N)weight = mg
Effort, EForce applied to the machine (N)—
Mechanical advantage (MA)How many times the load exceeds the effort. No unit.MA = L / E
Velocity ratio (VR)Distance moved by effort ÷ distance moved by load, in the same time. Depends on geometry only. No unit.VR = dE / dL
Efficiency (η)Work output ÷ work inputη = (L·dL)/(E·dE) × 100% = (MA/VR) × 100%
Energy wastedWork against friction and in moving the machine's own partsE·dE − L·dL
MA = η × VR   |   E = L / MA   |   Ideal machine: η = 100%, MA = VR
  • Derivation of η = MA/VR: η = (L·dL)/(E·dE) = (L/E) ÷ (dE/dL) = MA ÷ VR.
  • Limits: MA < VR in every real machine (friction), so η < 100%. MA > 1 means force gain; MA < 1 means speed gain.
  • Law of the machine: a graph of E against L is a straight line, E = mL + C. Slope m = increase in effort per unit load; intercept C = effort that only overcomes friction at no load. Limiting MA = 1/m. Limiting efficiency = 1/(m × VR).
  • Efficiency rises with load (fixed friction becomes a smaller share), so MA and η increase as L increases, up to the limit above.

3.3 Simple Machines: VR Formulae and Derivations

MachineVR / MA relationDerivationNotes and examples
LeverE × effort arm = L × load arm
VR = effort arm / load arm		Principle of moments: at balance, clockwise moment = anticlockwise moment about the fulcrum. Effort end sweeps an arc longer than the load end in the ratio of the arms.Class 1 (F between): crowbar, seesaw, scissors, pliers. Class 2 (L between): wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener; MA always > 1. Class 3 (E between): forceps, tongs, forearm, fishing rod; MA always < 1.

Garage lift, car brakes, excavator arm, baling press.

ItemFact
Self-locking ruleA machine that holds its load when the effort is removed has η < 50%. A screw jack and a worm gear are examples.

UTME trap list(1) VR and MA have no units. (2) VR is geometric, MA depends on the load. (3) η = MA/VR, and VR is effort distance over load distance. (4) Convert cm to m and minutes to seconds first. (5) Hydraulic VR uses areas (or radius squared), not radii. (6) Class 3 levers have MA < 1.

WEEK 4Repair and Maintenance of Machines

4.1 Definitions

TermMeaning
MaintenanceAll actions taken to keep a machine in good working condition so that it works safely, efficiently and for its full design life.
RepairRestoring a machine that has failed or worn out to working order by fixing or replacing parts.
FaultAny condition that stops a machine working properly (wear, breakage, leakage, blockage, electrical failure).
DowntimeTime a machine is out of service. Availability = uptime ÷ (uptime + downtime).
Friction force f = μR  |  Wasted work = f × d  |  Lubrication lowers μ, so η rises

4.3 Lubrication, Cleaning and Routine Checks

PracticePurpose and detail
LubricationReduces friction and wear; carries away heat; seals gaps; prevents rust; flushes out dirt. Types: oil (engines, gearboxes), grease (bearings, chains), graphite (dry, locks and hinges), air or water films (special bearings). Viscosity is graded (e.g. SAE 40).
CleaningRemoves dust, mud, metal particles and old grease that act as abrasives; lets leaks and cracks be seen.
Routine checksOil and coolant level, belt and chain tension, tyre pressure, battery terminals, air and fuel filters, loose bolts, unusual noise, vibration or smell.

4.4 Preventive and Corrective Maintenance

BasisPreventive maintenanceCorrective maintenance
When doneOn a schedule, before failureAfter a fault or breakdown
ActivitiesLubrication, cleaning, inspection, adjustment, planned part replacementDiagnosis, repair or replacement of the failed part
DowntimeShort and plannedLong and unplanned
CostSmall, regularOften large; a failed part can damage others
ExampleChanging engine oil every service intervalReplacing a burnt-out motor winding

4.5 Figures and Practice

ItemFact
Predictive (condition-based) maintenanceA third type: parts are replaced when a measurement (vibration, temperature, oil analysis) shows wear, not on a calendar.
Petrol generator servicingManufacturers commonly ask for the first oil change after about 20 running hours (run-in metal particles), then roughly every 100 hours or 6 months. The manual sets the interval.
Vehicle oil changeCommonly about 5 000 km on mineral oil and about 10 000 km on synthetic oil; always follow the service manual.
Cost patternA breakdown carries repair cost plus lost working time; planned servicing costs less per hour of machine life.

WEEK 6Dams and Energy Production

6.1 Definitions and Types

A dam is a barrier built across a river to hold back water. The stored water forms a reservoir (lake). Head (h) = vertical height between the reservoir surface and the turbine.

Type (by purpose)Main purposeFeatures
HydroelectricGenerate electricityIntake, penstock, turbine hall, generator, spillway
IrrigationStore and release water to farmlandCanals and gates; reservoir sized for the dry season
MultipurposePower + irrigation + flood control + water supply + fisheries + navigationMost large dams; purposes compete for the same water
Type (by structure)Description
GravityIts own weight resists the water pressure (concrete or masonry). Kainji is a gravity dam.
ArchCurved wall transfers the load into the valley sides; narrow rocky gorges.
EmbankmentEarth or rockfill with a watertight core; wide valleys with soft ground.

6.2 Hydroelectric Power Generation

Reservoir (Gravitational PE) → Intake gate → Penstock (water gains KE) → Turbine (Mechanical) → Generator (Electrical) → Step-up transformer → Transmission line
ComponentFunction
PenstockLarge pipe taking water from the intake down to the turbine; the fall converts PE to KE and pressure.
TurbineWater strikes the blades and spins the shaft. Pelton (high head, low flow), Francis (medium head), Kaplan (low head, high flow).
GeneratorThe shaft turns magnets inside coils; induced e.m.f. gives a.c. (Faraday's law).
SpillwayReleases surplus water safely in flood.
Step-up transformerRaises generator voltage to transmission voltage (e.g. 330 kV) to cut line loss.

6.3 Formulae and Derivation

QuantityFormulaNote
Mass flow rateṁ = ρQρ = 1 000 kg m⁻³ (water), Q in m³ s⁻¹
Volume flow rateQ = AvA = pipe cross-section, v = water speed
Power availableP = ρQghwatts; g = 10 m s⁻²

  • Dry season effect: low lake level lowers h and low river flow lowers Q, so P falls on both counts.

WEEK 8Rockets and Satellites

8.1 Rocket Propulsion: Principles and Formulae

Newton's third law: to every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. The rocket pushes hot gas backward; the gas pushes the rocket forward with equal force (thrust). No air is needed, so rockets work in a vacuum.

QuantityFormulaDerivation / note
Conservation of momentumM·Δv = m·veMomentum of gas expelled backward = momentum gained by rocket forward (starting from rest).
ThrustF = (dm/dt) × veNewton's 2nd law: force = rate of change of momentum of the exhaust. ve = exhaust speed relative to rocket.
Net accelerationa = (F − Mg)/M = F/M − gLift-off needs F > Mg. As fuel burns, M falls, so a rises.
Rocket equationΔv = ve ln(M₀/Mf)M₀ = full mass, Mf = empty mass. Basis of staging.
Orbital speedv = √(GM/r) = √(gR²/r)Gravity supplies the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r.
Escape speedv = √(2gR) ≈ 11.2 km s⁻¹KE at launch = PE needed to reach infinity.

8.4 Quiz Facts and Launch Logistics

ItemFact
First satelliteSputnik 1 (USSR), 4 October 1957, 83.6 kg, orbit ≈ 96 min. First human in space: Yuri Gagarin, 12 April 1961.
Geostationary milestonesIdea published by Arthur C. Clarke, 1945. Syncom 3 (1964) was the first geostationary satellite. Early Bird (1965) was the first commercial communications satellite in that orbit.

SuccessorsNigeriaSat-2 and NigeriaSat-X, built by SSTL, launched together on a Dnepr rocket from Yasny, Russia, 17 August 2011. NigeriaSat-2 images at 2.5 m (panchromatic), 5 m (multispectral) and 32 m (wide swath).
StatusNo longer operational.

9.2 Launch Logistics and Operating Facts

ItemFact
Shared rocketThe Kosmos-3M carried seven satellites in one launch: NigeriaSat-1, UK-DMC, BILSAT-1 (Turkey), STSAT-1 (South Korea), Rubin-4-DSI (Germany), Mozhayets-4 and Larets (Russia). Sharing split the launch cost.
Launch slipThe launch was planned for July 2003 and moved to September because the Russian launch agency had to fly a military satellite first.
Why sun-synchronousThe orbit crosses each latitude at the same local solar time every pass, so images from different dates have the same lighting and can be compared for change (floods, farmland, desert edge).
Ground contactA low-orbit satellite is above a given ground station for only about ten minutes per pass, so images are stored on board and sent down during passes over the station.
Resolution meaning32 m resolution: each pixel covers 32 m × 32 m of ground. Good for land cover and large floods; too coarse to count buildings.

Why C-band for NigeriaHeavy tropical rain absorbs Ku and Ka signals (rain fade). C-band is the most rain-tolerant of the three, at the cost of larger dishes. Ku and Ka give small dishes and more capacity.
