Question 01Structural Isomers

Which pair of compounds are structural isomers?

Compound X
Compound Y
AX and Y are the same compound drawn differently
BX and Y are structural isomers — same molecular formula C₂H₆O, different functional groups
CX and Y are geometric isomers
DX and Y cannot be isomers because they have different boiling points
Correct Answer
B
Both have molecular formula C₂H₆O but X is ethanol (−OH group, alcohol) and Y is methoxymethane (−O− group, ether). Different functional groups = functional group isomers, a type of structural isomer. Different boiling points (78°C vs −24°C) confirm different properties — isomers are not the same compound.
Question 02Positional Isomers

The two structures below are examples of which type of isomerism?

Propan-1-ol
Propan-2-ol
AChain isomerism — different carbon skeleton arrangements
BFunctional group isomerism — different functional groups
CPositional isomerism — same functional group, different position on chain
DGeometric isomerism — different spatial arrangement around a double bond
Correct Answer
C
Both are propanols with formula C₃H₈O and both carry an −OH group. The only difference is position: C1 vs C2. This is positional isomerism. Chain isomerism would require a different carbon skeleton. FG isomerism requires a different type of functional group entirely.
Question 03Geometric Isomers

Which condition is necessary for geometric (cis/trans) isomerism to exist?

cis-but-2-ene
trans-but-2-ene
AThe molecule must contain a carbon–carbon single bond
BEach double-bond carbon must carry two identical groups
CEach double-bond carbon must carry two different groups, and rotation around C=C is restricted
DThe molecule must have at least six carbons
Correct Answer
C
Geometric isomerism requires: (1) a C=C double bond (rotation is locked), and (2) each carbon of the double bond must carry two different groups. If either carbon has two identical groups, cis and trans become the same compound. But-1-ene has no geometric isomers because one carbon of the double bond carries two H atoms.
Question 04Chain Isomers

How many chain isomers does butane (C₄H₁₀) have, including itself?

n-butane
isobutane
A1
B2
C3
D4
Correct Answer
B
C₄H₁₀ has exactly two chain isomers: n-butane (straight chain) and 2-methylpropane/isobutane (branched). Both have formula C₄H₁₀. The carbon skeleton differs — one is linear, one is branched at the centre. There is no third arrangement possible for four carbons.
Question 05FG Isomers

A compound has molecular formula C₃H₆O. Which pair of functional group isomers is possible?

APropanal and propan-1-ol
BPropanal and propanone
CPropanone and propan-2-ol
DPropanal and propanoic acid
Correct Answer
B
Propanal (CH₃CH₂CHO) and propanone (CH₃COCH₃) both have formula C₃H₆O. Propanal has an aldehyde group (−CHO); propanone has a ketone group (C=O in the middle). Different functional groups, same formula = functional group isomers. Propan-1-ol is C₃H₈O (one extra H₂ — not an isomer). Propanoic acid is C₃H₆O₂ (extra oxygen).
Question 06Isomer Count

Which of the following is NOT a structural isomer of C₂H₆O?

div
AEthanol (CH₃CH₂OH)
BMethoxymethane (CH₃OCH₃)
CEthanal (CH₃CHO)
DBoth A and B only
Correct Answer
C
Ethanal (CH₃CHO) has formula C₂H₄O — only 4 hydrogens, not 6. It cannot be an isomer of C₂H₆O. Ethanol and methoxymethane are both C₂H₆O and are genuine structural isomers of each other. Count atoms carefully before assuming isomerism.
Question 07Isomer Properties

Ethanol (bp 78°C) and methoxymethane (bp −24°C) are structural isomers. The large boiling point difference is best explained by:

AEthanol has a higher molecular mass
BEthanol forms hydrogen bonds via its −OH group; methoxymethane cannot form H-bonds with itself
CMethoxymethane has a longer carbon chain
DEthanol is more volatile than methoxymethane
Correct Answer
B
Both have identical molecular mass (46 g/mol). The difference is intermolecular forces. Ethanol's −OH group allows hydrogen bonding between molecules — strong, requiring more energy to overcome. Methoxymethane has no −OH: only weak van der Waals forces. This is why functional group isomers have completely different physical properties despite identical formulas.
Reaction Transformations
Question 08Oxidation Chain

Ethanol is oxidised in two successive steps. What are the products, in order?

AEthanoic acid → Ethanal
BEthanal → Ethanoic acid
CEthene → Ethanal
DEthanal → Ethanol
Correct Answer
B
Oxidation of primary alcohols follows: Alcohol → Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid. Ethanol (−OH) → Ethanal (−CHO) → Ethanoic acid (−COOH). Each step adds one oxygen. The oxidising agent is acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ (turns green) or KMnO₄. Controlled oxidation stops at aldehyde; excess oxidant goes to acid.
Question 09Addition Reaction

Ethene reacts with bromine water. What is the structural change and what does this reaction confirm?

Ethene + Br₂
→
1,2-dibromoethane
ASubstitution occurs; confirms the compound is saturated
BAddition occurs across C=C; bromine water decolourises, confirming unsaturation
CAddition occurs; the product is ethanol
DElimination occurs; confirms the compound is saturated
Correct Answer
B
Addition reaction: Br₂ adds across the C=C double bond, converting it to a single bond and producing 1,2-dibromoethane. The orange-brown bromine water is decolourised — this is the standard test for unsaturation (C=C present). Alkanes do NOT decolourise bromine water in the dark — they require UV light for substitution.
Question 10Esterification

Ethanol reacts with ethanoic acid in the presence of concentrated H₂SO₄. What type of reaction is this and what are the products?

AHydrolysis — produces ethane and water
BCondensation — produces ethyl ethanoate and water
CAddition — produces ethyl ethanoate only
DSubstitution — produces methyl ethanoate and water
Correct Answer
B
Condensation (esterification): two molecules join and water is eliminated. Ethanol + Ethanoic acid → Ethyl ethanoate + H₂O. The −OH from the acid and −H from the alcohol combine to form water. H₂SO₄ is a catalyst (not consumed). The ester name: alcohol part first (ethyl) + acid part (ethanoate). The reaction is reversible — hydrolysis is the reverse.
Question 11Elimination / Dehydration

When ethanol is heated with excess concentrated H₂SO₄ at 170°C, the major product is:

AEthane
BDiethyl ether
CEthene
DEthanal
Correct Answer
C
Elimination (dehydration): H₂O is removed from ethanol, forming ethene (C=C double bond). At 170°C with excess acid — alkene is the product. At 140°C with less acid — ether is the product instead. Temperature is the control. This is the industrial route to ethene. Ethanal is an oxidation product, not a dehydration product.
Question 12Saponification

A triglyceride (fat) is heated with concentrated NaOH solution. What type of reaction occurs and what are the products?

ACondensation — produces fatty acids and glycerol
BHydrolysis — produces soap (sodium salt of fatty acid) and glycerol
COxidation — produces fatty acids and water
DHydrolysis — produces ethanol and glycerol
Correct Answer
B
Saponification is alkaline hydrolysis of an ester. The ester bonds in the triglyceride are broken by NaOH. Products: soap (sodium salt of the long-chain fatty acid — RCOONa) + glycerol (propan-1,2,3-triol). NaOH → hard (solid) soap. KOH → soft (liquid) soap. This is the reverse of condensation — hydrolysis always breaks ester bonds.
Question 13Substitution — Alkanes

Methane reacts with chlorine gas. Which conditions are required and what type of reaction occurs?

AAddition reaction; requires heat only
BSubstitution reaction; requires UV light (free radical mechanism)
CElimination reaction; requires a catalyst
DSubstitution reaction; requires concentrated H₂SO₄
Correct Answer
B
Free radical substitution: one H atom is replaced by Cl. Requires UV light to initiate — UV breaks Cl₂ into Cl• radicals (homolytic fission). Alkanes cannot undergo addition (no double bond). This reaction is progressive — CH₃Cl can react further to give CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃, CCl₄. The HCl produced is the other product.
Tests & Distinguishing Reactions
Question 14Fehling's Test

A student adds Fehling's solution to two unknown compounds and heats gently. Compound A gives a brick-red precipitate. Compound B gives no change. Which conclusion is correct?

AA is a ketone; B is an aldehyde
BA is an aldehyde (reducing sugar or aldehyde); B is a ketone or non-reducing compound
CA is an alcohol; B is a carboxylic acid
DBoth A and B are aldehydes — Compound B just needs more time
Correct Answer
B
Fehling's solution contains Cu²⁺ (blue). Aldehydes reduce Cu²⁺ to Cu⁺, forming a brick-red Cu₂O precipitate. Ketones cannot reduce Fehling's — no reaction, no colour change. Compound A = aldehyde (or reducing sugar like glucose). Compound B = ketone (or any non-reducing compound). This is the primary chemical distinction between aldehydes and ketones.
Question 15Tollens' / Silver Mirror

Tollens' reagent (ammoniacal silver nitrate) is used to distinguish aldehydes from ketones. What is the positive result and what does it confirm?

ABlue precipitate forms — confirms a ketone
BSilver mirror forms on inside of tube — confirms an aldehyde
CYellow precipitate forms — confirms an alkene
DSilver mirror forms — confirms a ketone
Correct Answer
B
Tollens' reagent contains Ag⁺ ions. Aldehydes reduce Ag⁺ to Ag⁰ (metallic silver), which deposits as a silver mirror on the glass. Ketones cannot do this. The aldehyde itself is oxidised to a carboxylic acid in the process. Both Fehling's and Tollens' test for reducing power — Fehling's gives brick-red precipitate, Tollens' gives silver mirror.
Question 16Bromine Water Test

Which of the following will NOT decolourise bromine water in the absence of UV light?

AEthene
BEthyne
CPropene
DPropane
Correct Answer
D
Propane is a saturated alkane — no double or triple bonds. It cannot undergo addition with bromine. UV light is needed for free radical substitution with alkanes, but that still doesn't count as "decolourising bromine water" in the exam context. Ethene, ethyne, and propene are all unsaturated — they add Br₂ across their multiple bonds instantly, decolourising the orange-brown bromine water.
Question 17Distinguishing Tests

A chemist has four unlabelled bottles containing: ethanol, ethanoic acid, ethanal, and propanone. Which single test would distinguish ethanal from propanone?

AAdd bromine water — ethanal decolourises it, propanone does not
BAdd Fehling's solution and heat — ethanal gives brick-red precipitate, propanone gives no change
CAdd sodium carbonate — ethanal fizzes, propanone does not
DAdd universal indicator — ethanal turns red, propanone turns blue
Correct Answer
B
Fehling's test is the cleanest single test here. Ethanal (aldehyde) reduces Cu²⁺ → brick-red Cu₂O. Propanone (ketone) gives no reaction. Bromine water would not work cleanly here — neither ethanal nor propanone reliably decolourises it under standard conditions. Sodium carbonate tests for acids. Universal indicator doesn't distinguish these two carbonyl compounds.
Functional Group Identification
Question 18FG from Structure

Identify the functional group present in the structure below and name the compound.

AKetone group (C=O); propanone
BAldehyde group (−CHO); propanal
CCarboxyl group (−COOH); propanoic acid
DEster group (−COO−); propyl methanoate
Correct Answer
C
The structure shows a carboxyl group (−COOH): a C=O (carbonyl) and −OH on the same carbon, at the end of the chain. This is a carboxylic acid. Three-carbon chain (prop−) + acid suffix (−anoic acid) = propanoic acid. Common name: propionic acid. Distinguish from ester: ester has −COO− with an alkyl group on the oxygen, not H.
Question 19FG Priority Application

A molecule contains both an −NH₂ group and a −COOH group on a two-carbon chain. What is its correct IUPAC name?

A2-aminoethanoic acid (glycine)
BEthanoic amine
CCarboxyaminoethane
D2-carboxylamine
Correct Answer
A
Priority rule in action: −COOH outranks −NH₂. The acid group wins the suffix (−anoic acid). The amine becomes a prefix (amino−). Two-carbon chain = eth−. Position of NH₂ on C2 = 2-amino. Full name: 2-aminoethanoic acid. Common name: glycine — the simplest amino acid. This is the direct application of functional group priority in naming.

Question 20IUPAC ↔ Common Name

Match each common name to its correct IUPAC name and functional group class.

I. Acetone    II. Formic acid    III. Acetylene    IV. Acetic acid
AI=Propanone (ketone), II=Methanoic acid (carboxylic acid), III=Ethyne (alkyne), IV=Ethanoic acid (carboxylic acid)
BI=Propanal (aldehyde), II=Ethanoic acid, III=Ethene (alkene), IV=Methanoic acid
CI=Propanone (ketone), II=Ethanoic acid, III=Ethene, IV=Methanoic acid
DI=Ethanal (aldehyde), II=Methanoic acid, III=Ethyne, IV=Ethanoic acid
Correct Answer
A
I. Acetone = Propanone (3C ketone — C=O in the middle). II. Formic acid = Methanoic acid (1C, from Latin formica = ant). III. Acetylene = Ethyne (2C, triple bond, welding gas). IV. Acetic acid = Ethanoic acid (2C, from Latin acetum = vinegar). All four are in the acet−/form− family — trace them to their carbon count and you'll never confuse them.
