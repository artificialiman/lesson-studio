Chapter One

Equilibrium — The Stalemate

Not a finish line. Not completion. A stalemate where both reactions are still running — at the same rate — so nothing appears to change.

The compression
Equilibrium is a tug-of-war where both teams pull with equal force. The rope isn't moving — but nobody has stopped pulling.

The most common misconception: "equilibrium means the reaction is complete." It means the opposite. Complete reactions are one-way streets — no reverse, no equilibrium possible. Equilibrium only exists because the reaction is reversible (shown by ⇌). At equilibrium, the forward rate equals the reverse rate. Concentrations are constant — not zero.

This is why ΔG = 0 at equilibrium. Nature is exactly undecided. Neither side has enough advantage to push further. The system is locked in its stalemate until something disturbs it.

Chapter Two

ΔH, ΔS, ΔG — Nature's Verdict

Nature is lazy (prefers low energy) and chaotic (prefers high disorder). ΔG is the final vote — it combines both tendencies and decides whether a reaction happens freely.

The one compression
ΔH is the energy bill. ΔS is the chaos score. ΔG is nature's final verdict. When ΔG is negative, nature pays willingly. When positive, you have to force it.
Gibbs Free Energy — the master equation
ΔG = ΔH − TΔS
ΔG −ve
Spontaneous. Free.
ΔG = 0
Equilibrium. Undecided.
ΔG +ve
Non-spontaneous. Forced.

Units trap: ΔH is almost always given in kJ/mol. ΔS is always in J/mol/K. Before substituting into ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, convert ΔH to J (multiply by 1000) or ΔS to kJ (divide by 1000). Mixing units is the most common calculation error on this topic.

To find the temperature at which a reaction becomes spontaneous (when ΔG changes sign), set ΔG = 0 and solve: T = ΔH / ΔS. This is the crossover temperature.

The difference between Le Chatelier's Principle and Hess's Law is examinable. A law is mathematically provable with no exceptions — Hess's Law holds always because energy is conserved. A principle is a powerful predictive rule with known edge cases. Le Chatelier predicts direction reliably but cannot predict magnitude, and breaks down in the four exception cases below.

1
Catalyst — no shift at all
Adding a catalyst speeds up both forward and reverse reactions equally. The equilibrium position does not change. The system reaches the same stalemate, just faster.
JAMB trap: "A catalyst increases the yield of product." FALSE. It increases the rate only.
2
Inert gas at constant volume
Adding an inert gas (e.g. argon) to a fixed-volume container increases total pressure but does not change the partial pressures of the reacting gases. No shift occurs.
Contrast with: inert gas at constant pressure — this dilutes partial pressures and does cause a shift.
3
Inert gas at constant pressure
Here the volume must expand to maintain pressure, which dilutes all partial pressures. The system shifts toward the side with more moles of gas to compensate.
Opposite result to constant-volume case. Both use inert gas — context determines outcome.
4
Temperature — changes K itself
All other disturbances shift position without changing K. Temperature alone changes the value of K. Le Chatelier predicts the direction of shift, but the equilibrium constant is now fundamentally different.
This is why temperature is uniquely powerful — it does not just disturb the stalemate, it redefine it.
Chapter Four

Hess's Law

A law — no exceptions, no conditions. The total enthalpy change of a reaction depends only on the start and end states, never on the route taken.

The compression
The height difference between Lagos and Abuja is fixed — whether you drive through Ibadan or Lokoja. ΔH is that height difference. The route is irrelevant.

Hess's Law is a direct consequence of conservation of energy. Because energy cannot be created or destroyed, the total enthalpy change must be the same regardless of pathway. This means you can calculate ΔH for reactions that cannot be measured directly — by combining reactions whose ΔH values are known.

Worked example — C + 2H₂ → CH₄
Given three reactions. CH₄ is a product in target but reactant in reaction 3 → flip reaction 3, sign becomes +890. Keep reaction 1 (C on left ✅). Multiply reaction 2 by 2 (need 2H₂). Add: −394 + (−572) + 890 = −76 kJ/mol. CO₂, 2H₂O, and 2O₂ cancel.
  • ΔH of formation — always has elements in standard state as reactants, 1 mole of compound as product
  • ΔH of combustion — compound + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Always exothermic. Never flip the sign instinctively.
  • ΔH reaction = Σ ΔHf° products − Σ ΔHf° reactants — alternative method when formation enthalpies are given
  • ΔH reaction = Σ ΔHc° reactants − Σ ΔHc° products — when combustion enthalpies are given (note: reversed order)
  • Bond energy method — ΔH = Σ bonds broken − Σ bonds formed. Breaking bonds costs energy (+). Forming bonds releases energy (−).

Why these four topics are one idea

Equilibrium, enthalpy, entropy, and Hess's Law are not four separate topics. They are four views of the same question: what does nature do with energy? Hess's Law measures the energy cost (ΔH) of any pathway. Entropy measures disorder (ΔS). Gibbs combines them into a final verdict (ΔG). And equilibrium is the physical state where that verdict reaches exactly zero — nature perfectly undecided, both reactions running, nothing changing. Every JAMB question on this block is asking one of these four things.

ΔG = 0 is where everything meets — equilibrium, thermodynamics, and nature's final answer all converge at that single point.
