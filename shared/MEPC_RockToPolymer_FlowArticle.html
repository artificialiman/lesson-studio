Act Zero

The Beach — Before the Lesson Begins

You do not need a laboratory to see chemistry. You need a shoreline, bare feet, and the willingness to look at ordinary things as if you have never seen them before.

A natural beach — every element of the periodic table is already here

Stand at the shoreline at night. The sand under your feet is silicon dioxide — SiO₂, an acidic oxide, the same compound that makes glass and computer chips. The water around your ankles is sodium chloride dissolved in water — NaCl(aq), an ionic compound that formed when hydrochloric acid reacted with sodium hydroxide in geological time, the salt of a neutralisation nobody witnessed. The shells scattered along the tideline are calcium carbonate — CaCO₃, a base, slowly dissolving in the slightly acidic seawater. The rock face is streaked orange — iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃, rust, the spontaneous oxidation of iron that begins the moment the metal meets oxygen and water. The seaweed is a tangle of organic polymers — cellulose, carrageenan, proteins — condensation products built from simple monomers by the same reaction that makes nylon in a factory.

Above all of this, invisible and indifferent, the atmosphere is 78% nitrogen gas — more nitrogen than anything else, and almost entirely unavailable. The bacteria in the wet sand are among the few things on earth that can break that N≡N triple bond and fix it into a usable form. The Haber process is humanity's industrial attempt to do what those bacteria do chemically — at scale, by force, using a catalyst and 200 atmospheres of pressure.

Now look at the dead jellyfish at the waterline. It is beginning to decay. Bacteria are breaking its proteins back into amino acids, its lipids back into fatty acids and glycerol, its carbohydrates back into sugars. Carbon dioxide returns to the air. Mineral ions leach back into the sand. The organic matter that remains will be buried. Over millions of years, heat and pressure will compress it. The hydrogen and carbon will reorganise into long hydrocarbon chains. What was a jellyfish will, eventually, become crude oil.

The Act 0 compression
The beach is not a place outside of chemistry. It is chemistry paused — every element, every compound class, every reaction type present simultaneously, waiting for the conditions to change.
  • Sand (SiO₂) — acidic oxide. Reacts with strong alkalis. The source of silicon for semiconductors after purification.
  • Seawater (NaCl aq) — ionic compound in solution. Electrolysis of brine → Cl₂ + NaOH + H₂. The chlor-alkali process starts here.
  • Shells (CaCO₃) — basic salt. Dissolves in acid: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. The same reaction happens in acid rain.
  • Rust (Fe₂O₃) — spontaneous oxidation. ΔG negative. The reverse of the blast furnace — iron returning to its ore form.
  • Seaweed (organic polymer) — condensation product. Cellulose is a natural polyester of glucose. Every synthetic polymer mimics what biology already does.
  • Dead organism — decay → organic matter → burial → compression → crude oil. The origin of every fossil fuel is a living thing that died and was not fully decomposed.
  • Atmosphere (N₂, O₂, CO₂) — the inorganic reservoir. N₂ feeds the Haber process. O₂ enables combustion. CO₂ is the carbon source for all photosynthesis — and all organic chemistry began here.
The loop that closes everything
Living things are made from atmospheric CO₂ and water. They die. They decay. Over millions of years they become crude oil. We extract and burn it. It returns to CO₂ and water. The carbon cycle is the chemistry cycle — and it runs whether we understand it or not.
Act One

The Cast — Elements as Personalities

The periodic table is not a filing cabinet. It is a personality spectrum — from elements desperate to lose electrons to elements so satisfied they have never reacted with anything in recorded history.

The opening compression
Every element is either trying to give electrons away, trying to take them, or has decided the whole business is beneath it. That single instinct — give, take, or ignore — determines everything that follows.

The first great divide is metals vs nonmetals. Metals sit left and centre of the periodic table — they give electrons. Nonmetals sit right — they take them. The line between them is blurry by design: a diagonal strip of metalloids runs from boron to astatine, neither fully committed to either side, which is precisely why silicon runs every computer on earth.

But within each kingdom, personality varies enormously. Not all metals react the same way. Not all nonmetals want the same thing. The story gets interesting in the details.

The Act 1 principle
Metals want to lose electrons. Nonmetals want to gain them. Noble gases want neither. Everything in chemistry is just variations on this one exchange — who gives, who takes, and at what cost.
Act Two

The Compounds — What Happens When Elements Meet

Elements don't stay alone for long. The moment a metal meets a nonmetal, electrons transfer. The moment two nonmetals meet, electrons are shared. The resulting compounds divide neatly into two kingdoms — those trying to make salts, and those trying to avoid the whole conversation.

The Act 2 compression
Every compound is either a salt producer, a salt itself, or something that has found a way to remain neutral in a world that wants to force a reaction.

The salt-producer kingdom contains three characters: acids, bases, and their offspring — salts. Acids donate protons. Bases accept them. When they meet, they neutralise each other and produce a salt plus water. It's the most fundamental transaction in chemistry, and it happens in your stomach every time you eat.

But some compounds refuse to pick a side. Amphoteric substances react with both acids and bases — they're the diplomats of chemistry. And then there are compounds like CO₂ that don't form salts under ordinary conditions but lean acidic in water. The organic world — esters, alcohols, hydrocarbons — largely stays neutral, reacting through its own mechanisms that have nothing to do with proton transfer.

Amphoteric behaviour — ZnO playing both sides
  • Acid — proton donor (H⁺ source). Strong acids fully dissociate: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃. Weak acids partially dissociate: CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃.
  • Base — proton acceptor. Soluble bases are alkalis: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂. All alkalis are bases; not all bases are alkalis.
  • Neutralisation — Acid + Base → Salt + Water. The salt name = metal from base + anion from acid.
  • Salt — ionic compound, product of neutralisation. NaCl, CuSO₄, Ca(NO₃)₂. Properties determined by constituent ions.
  • Complex / Coordination compound — transition metal centre + ligands (electron donors). [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ is deep blue. Formation is a ligand substitution, not a simple acid-base reaction.

    The neutral kingdom is larger than it seems. Organic compounds — hydrocarbons, alcohols, esters, sugars — constitute the majority of all known compounds, and most of them sit outside the acid-base framework entirely. They react through their functional groups — addition, substitution, elimination, condensation, hydrolysis — not through proton transfer. This is why organic chemistry feels like a separate subject. It isn't. It's just the part of chemistry where carbon decided to do things its own way.

    The neutral kingdom — compounds that refused to pick a side
    Amphoteric oxides (Al₂O₃, ZnO) — react with both acid and base, produce a salt either way, sit at the border of both kingdoms. Acidic-leaning oxides in water (CO₂, SO₂) — not salts themselves, but dissolve in water to form weak acids; technically acidic oxides, not neutral. True neutral oxides (CO, H₂O, NO) — react with neither acid nor base under standard conditions. Organic compounds — the largest neutral class; react through functional groups, not proton exchange; the entire carbon world lives here.
    The Act 2 principle
    Acids and bases are not opposites — they are partners. One cannot exist without the other to react with. And amphoteric compounds are not confused — they are simply more versatile than the binary framework allows.
    Act Three

    The Transformations — From Rock to Everything

    Chemistry is not a collection of facts. It is a series of transformations — each compound the raw material for the next reaction, each reaction a step in a longer journey that began in the ground and ends in a fuel tank, a fertiliser bag, a hospital ward, or a plastic bottle.

    The Act 3 compression
    Every useful material humanity has ever made started as a mineral, an ore, or a gas. Chemistry is the art of transformation — from what the earth provides to what civilisation requires.
    The Grand Flow — mineral ore to polymer, one unbroken chain

    The first chain begins underground. Mineral ores are compounds — oxides, sulphides, carbonates — in which metals are locked by ionic bonds. To extract the metal, those bonds must be broken. How you break them depends entirely on where the metal sits in the reactivity series.

    • Very reactive metals (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) — cannot be reduced by carbon or hydrogen. Require electrolysis of the molten ore. Al is extracted from molten Al₂O₃ (bauxite) in the Hall-Héroult process. Enormous energy cost — hence aluminium's historical expense.
    • Moderately reactive metals (Zn, Fe, Ni, Pb, Sn) — reduced by carbon (coke) or carbon monoxide in a blast furnace. Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. Carbon is the reducing agent — it takes oxygen away from the metal oxide.
    • Low reactivity metals (Cu) — partially by reduction, partially by electrolytic refining. Raw copper is cast as anode; pure copper deposits on cathode.
    • Noble metals (Au, Ag, Pt) — occur native. No extraction chemistry needed. Physical separation (panning, smelting) suffices.

    The second transformation is perhaps the most consequential in human history. Nitrogen gas makes up 78% of the atmosphere — and yet for most of history, it was completely inaccessible to plants and therefore to agriculture. The Haber process, developed in 1909, changed this. It is not an exaggeration to say it feeds half the world's population today.

    The Haber compression
    Nitrogen is everywhere and unavailable. Haber made it available. The atoms in half of all human beings alive today passed through a Haber plant.
    • Haber Process — N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. Conditions: 450°C, 200 atm, iron catalyst. Reversible — equilibrium position is the challenge.
    • Le Chatelier applied — high pressure favours the forward reaction (4 moles gas → 2 moles gas). High temperature favours reverse (exothermic forward). 450°C is the industrial compromise: fast enough rate, acceptable yield.
    • Ostwald Process — converts ammonia to nitric acid. NH₃ → NO → NO₂ → HNO₃. Platinum-rhodium catalyst. Produces the acid used in fertilisers and explosives.
    • Fertiliser chain — NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃ (ammonium nitrate, high-nitrogen fertiliser). Or NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄ (ammonium sulphate).
    • Hydrogen source — H₂ for Haber comes from the steam reforming of natural gas: CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂. So the fertiliser chain begins with fossil fuels — a carbon footprint buried in every crop.

    The third transformation begins with crude oil — a mixture of thousands of hydrocarbons formed over millions of years from compressed organic matter. Crude oil is useless as it comes out of the ground. The refinery is where chemistry begins.

    Fractional distillation separates crude oil by boiling point, which is a proxy for carbon chain length. Short chains (C₁–C₄) vaporise first and exit at the top of the column. Long chains (C₃₀+) never fully vaporise and are collected as bitumen from the bottom. Each fraction has a specific industrial destiny.

    • Fractional distillation — crude oil heated to ~350°C; vapours condense at different heights in the column. Shorter chain = lower boiling point = higher exit point.
    • Cracking — long-chain alkanes (low demand) are broken into short-chain alkenes (high demand). C₁₀H₂₂ → C₄H₈ + C₃H₆ + CH₄. Thermal cracking (high temp/pressure) or catalytic cracking (zeolite catalyst, lower temp).
    • Why cracking matters — alkenes are the feedstock for almost all organic synthesis. Without cracking, there is no ethene, no polyethylene, no PVC, no polypropylene. The plastic world depends on it.
    • Addition polymerisation — alkene monomers open their C=C double bonds and link end-to-end. nCH₂=CH₂ → (−CH₂−CH₂−)ₙ. No by-product. Polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), PVC, polystyrene all made this way.
    • Condensation polymerisation — monomers with two functional groups join and eliminate small molecules (H₂O or HCl). Nylon: diamine + diacid → polyamide + H₂O. Polyester (Terylene): diol + diacid → polyester + H₂O.
    • End of the chain — polymer → plastic goods → (disposal problem: non-biodegradable) OR pharmaceutical synthesis → drug → patient → metabolism → excretion.

    Three chains — one connected chemistry

    These three chains do not operate independently. The chlor-alkali process electrolyses brine to produce Cl₂ and NaOH simultaneously — and that Cl₂ becomes the chlorine in PVC, connecting the inorganic extraction chain directly to the polymer chain. Sulphuric acid, produced industrially by the Contact process (SO₂ → SO₃ → H₂SO₄), is used in fertiliser production, metal refining, and petroleum refining — it touches all three chains at once. It is the single most produced industrial chemical in the world for this reason.

    And at the very end of the organic chain, where polymers meet biology, lies the most elegant transformation of all: proteins are natural polyamides, DNA is a natural polymer, and the enzymes that drive every metabolic reaction are themselves the products of condensation polymerisation — the same fundamental bond-forming mechanism that makes nylon. The chemistry does not change. Only the monomer does.

    The closing compression
    There is no boundary between inorganic and organic chemistry. There is no wall between the laboratory and the living cell. There is only the same small set of reactions — transfer, share, oxidise, reduce, condense, hydrolyse — applied across an infinite range of materials, at every scale from atom to atmosphere.

    The Bridge

    The Big Three — Every Reaction is One of These

    Strip away every reaction in every chapter of this article and you find the same three types underneath. Not five, not twenty — three. The entire machinery of chemistry runs on spontaneous reactions, thermodynamic reactions, and electrolytic reactions. They are defined by a single number: ΔG.

    The bridge compression
    ΔG negative: nature pays. ΔG positive: you must pay. ΔG forced by electricity: you override nature entirely. Every reaction ever written fits one of these three sentences.
    The Big 3 — positioned by ΔG and energy source

    The relationship between the three is precise. Spontaneous reactions release free energy — ΔG is negative, the reaction proceeds without external input. Combustion, neutralisation, rusting, the discharge of a battery: all spontaneous. The free energy released can be captured as heat (a furnace) or as electricity (a galvanic cell). This is what a battery is — a spontaneous redox reaction arranged so that electrons travel through a wire rather than jumping directly between reactants.

    Thermodynamic reactions are the managed middle ground. They are often reversible — sitting at or near equilibrium — and their direction is controlled by adjusting temperature, pressure, and concentration. The Haber process is endothermic in reverse and exothermic in the forward direction. By setting the temperature to 450°C and the pressure to 200 atm, engineers shift the equilibrium toward products while maintaining a rate fast enough to be industrial. ΔG is being managed, not eliminated. Le Chatelier's principle is the rule that describes how equilibrium responds to every adjustment.

    Electrolytic reactions are forced reactions — non-spontaneous processes made to run by applying external electrical energy. To extract aluminium from Al₂O₃, you are forcing the reverse of the spontaneous oxidation of aluminium — you are using electricity to undo what nature did. The minimum voltage required is set by the ΔG of the spontaneous reaction you are reversing. The higher the ΔG of the natural process, the more energy electrolysis demands. This is why sodium and aluminium are so energy-expensive to produce, and why gold costs almost nothing to refine — gold's oxidation is barely spontaneous to begin with.

    The connection that closes the loop
    Iron rusts spontaneously — ΔG negative, returning to Fe₂O₃. The blast furnace reverses this thermodynamically — heat-driven, equilibrium-managed, requiring constant fuel. Aluminium also oxidises spontaneously — but its oxide layer is protective, not destructive. To extract Al from Al₂O₃, electrolysis must force the reversal electrically. Three metals, three different relationships with ΔG, three different extraction methods. The periodic table is a map of how much energy nature spent making compounds — and how much energy we must spend to undo them.
    The decay loop — from living thing to crude oil and back

    The decay loop is the oldest chemistry on earth. It predates the periodic table, predates the laboratory, predates the human species. Every carbon atom in your body has been through this cycle — in a star, in a rock, in an ocean, in a plant, in an animal, in the ground, in the air — before arriving in you. It will continue after you. The Haber process and the blast furnace and the cracking tower are all small interruptions in loops that have been running for four billion years.

    This is why chemistry is not just a school subject. It is the description of how matter moves through time.

    The final compression
    Every reaction in this article — from the sand on a beach to the polymer in a plastic bottle — is nature managing electron flow. Give electrons, take electrons, store electrons, force electrons. The whole story, in four words.
    The Big 3 mapped to UTME topics

    Spontaneous reactions map to: electrochemical cells (EMF, standard electrode potentials), thermodynamics (ΔG, ΔH, ΔS, the 4-case table), corrosion, combustion, and any reaction described as "exothermic and feasible." Thermodynamic reactions map to: equilibrium (Kc, Kp), Le Chatelier's principle, Haber process, Contact process, and any question about industrial conditions. Electrolytic reactions map to: electrolysis calculations (Faraday's laws, Q = It), extraction of reactive metals, electroplating, the chlor-alkali process, and the Downs cell. JAMB frequently crosses these categories in a single question — a question about the Haber process that then asks about the electrode reactions in the electrolytic production of the hydrogen feedstock is asking about all three types in one breath.

    From the first electron transfer in a mineral ore to the last peptide bond in a protein — it was always the same chemistry, asking the same question: who gives, and who takes?
