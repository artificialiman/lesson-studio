Chapter One

What Electrolysis Actually Is

You push electricity into a liquid. The electricity forces dissolved substances to break apart and deposit as solid metal — or escape as gas. Physics goes in. Chemistry comes out.

That's the whole story. Everything else in this document is just the maths that describes how much chemistry comes out per unit of electricity you push in.

The liquid must be able to conduct electricity. That means it contains ions — charged particles free to move. Pure water barely conducts. Dissolved salt water? Very well. Molten metal salts? Even better.

Two metal rods dip into the liquid. The battery pushes electrons out of one rod (the cathode — negative) and pulls electrons into the other (the anode — positive). Ions in the liquid migrate toward whichever electrode has the opposite charge. When they arrive, they either gain or lose electrons — and that exchange turns them from floating ions into actual physical substance.

Central Insight
"Electricity in → electrons move → ions collect electrons → solid metal (or gas) appears → weigh it."

Every electrolysis question is asking: how much solid appeared? Or how much electricity did it take?

The cathode is where metal deposits. Positive ions (like Cu²⁺) swim toward the negative electrode, collect the electrons waiting there, and become neutral copper atoms that stick to the rod.

The anode is where things are destroyed or oxidised. Negative ions arrive, give up electrons, and either dissolve the electrode or release gas (O₂, Cl₂).

You do not need to understand why ions move toward opposite charges right now. Just know: they do, and that movement is what the entire calculation depends on.

Chapter Two

The Chain — Every Question Is This

Electrolysis converts electrical energy to chemical mass through a fixed sequence of steps. Learn the chain. Every question just tells you where to enter and where to stop.

Here is what the chain looks like written as a sentence:

Current × time = total charge → ÷ Faraday's constant = moles of electrons → ÷ electrons-per-ion = moles of substance → × molar mass = grams.

That sentence is the entire subject. The rest of this document exists to make sure every symbol in that sentence means something concrete to you.

Chapter Three

The n Problem

The letter n appears four times in the chain. Each time it means something completely different. Most marks are lost here — not to wrong maths, but to losing track of which n you're currently counting.

This is not a chemistry problem. It is a tracking problem. Know which population you're counting — electrons, substance, or gas — and the arithmetic is just multiplication and division.

SymbolWhat it countsFormulaPlain words
n(e⁻) Moles of electrons Q ÷ F How many electrons actually flowed. Pure charge carriers — not atoms, not ions. Just electrons.
z Electrons per ion from half-equation Not a count — a ratio. The bridge between electron-world and substance-world. Cu²⁺ needs 2. Al³⁺ needs 3.
n(sub) Moles of substance n(e⁻) ÷ z How many atoms (or molecules) actually appeared. What you weigh at the end.
n(gas) Moles of gas produced same as n(sub) For H₂, O₂, Cl₂. Same moles — but instead of multiplying by Mr for mass, multiply by 22.4 for volume.
Why z matters so much
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu means: for every 2 electrons, you get 1 copper atom. z = 2.

Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al means: for every 3 electrons, you get 1 aluminium atom. z = 3.

Same current, same time → same electrons — but aluminium produces fewer atoms per electron, so less mass deposits. z is the exchange rate between electron-world and substance-world.

The half-equation is not an abstract chemistry concept. It's the one line that tells you z — the exchange rate you need. Read it once. Extract z. Move on.

Chapter Four

The Metals Table — Your Exchange Rates

Every metal has three numbers you need: Mr (its atomic mass), z (electrons per ion), and Mr÷z (how many grams one mole of electrons deposits). Memorise the common ones. Read the rest off the question.

Mr÷z is not a formula to calculate every time. It's a shortcut: if you know how many moles of electrons flowed, multiplying by Mr÷z gives you the mass directly in one step, skipping n(sub). Both routes give the same answer — use whichever is faster.

Series cells — the key insight
In a series circuit, the same current flows everywhere. Which means the same number of electrons pass through every cell. Find n(e⁻) from whatever data cell 1 gives you — then use that exact n(e⁻) in cell 2 as if you'd been given it directly.

The identity question (find Mr or z) is the hardest type because it feels backwards. But it's the same chain — you just stop at a different node. Run the chain as far as you can with what you're given, then solve for the unknown where the chain breaks.

  • Read the question — what's given? what's asked?
  • Identify direction — left-to-right (I,t given) or right-to-left (mass given)
  • Write the half-equation — extract z from it
  • Walk the chain — one step at a time, don't skip nodes
  • Gas check — if product is H₂, O₂, or Cl₂, end with ×22.4 not ×Mr
  • Units check — time must be in seconds, current in amps, before you compute Q
For the curious — why voltage connects to all of this

Voltage, concentration, pressure, and osmotic pressure are all the same thing underneath: a gradient. A measure of how unequal two sides are. In electrochemistry, voltage (E°) is just "electron pressure" — how urgently electrons want to flow from one electrode to the other. The Nernst equation (E = E° − RT/nF × lnQ) is how that pressure changes as the reaction proceeds and concentrations shift. When E = 0, the cell is dead — equilibrium. This connects electrolysis to thermodynamics, equilibrium, and every other unit in AP Chemistry. But you do not need any of that to solve electrolysis calculations. The chain above is complete on its own.

Every calculation is a walk along one chain. The only variable is which metal — because that changes z and Mr. Everything else is always the same four steps.
