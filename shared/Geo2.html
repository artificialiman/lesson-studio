WEEK 1Industry and Geography I: Meaning, Importance, Location Factors

Content: Meaning and importance of industry, and how geography decides where industries are sited.

1.1 Meaning of Industry

Industry is any economic activity that extracts raw materials, processes them into finished goods, or provides a service, for sale or profit. Where an industry stands depends on geography: relief, climate, water, minerals, soil, and the people and roads around it.

1.2 Importance of Industries

  1. Employment: Industries give jobs to skilled and unskilled workers.
  2. Foreign exchange: Exported products (crude oil, cocoa, processed goods) earn foreign currency.
  3. Import reduction: Local factories make goods that would otherwise be bought abroad.
  4. Raw material use: Industries add value to farm and mineral products.
  5. Government revenue: Factories pay taxes and royalties.
  6. Growth of towns and infrastructure: Roads, power and housing develop around industrial centres.
  7. Technology and skills: Workers learn new methods that spread to other sectors.

1.3 Factors that Decide Industrial Location

FactorHow it influences locationNigerian example
Raw materialsBulky or perishable raw materials are processed near their source to cut transport cost.Cement at limestone sites: Ewekoro, Nkalagu, Obajana, Gboko. Sugar at cane fields: Bacita, Numan.
Power and energyFactories need steady electricity, gas or coal near or connected to them.Hydro-power from Kainji, Jebba and Shiroro; gas in the Niger Delta.
WaterNeeded for cooling, washing, and as an input (drinks, textiles, paper).Breweries and bottling plants near rivers or boreholes.
Land (site)Flat, firm, well-drained and cheap land with room to expand.Planned estates at Ikeja, Ilupeju, Agbara.
ClimateAffects the crop supply and some processes (drying, storage, humidity).Cocoa mills in the humid south; groundnut and cotton mills in the drier north (Kano, Kaduna).
LabourSkilled and cheap workers are drawn to dense populations.Lagos, Kano, Aba, Onitsha.
MarketProducts that are heavy, fragile or perishable are made near buyers.Bread, soft drinks, and printing in Lagos, Abuja, Ibadan.
TransportRoads, rail, ports and airports move inputs in and goods out.Apapa and Port Harcourt ports; Lagos–Ibadan corridor.
CapitalLarge plants need money for land, machines and running costs.Banks in Lagos and Abuja attract investment.
Government policyTax breaks, industrial estates and bans on imports shape choices.Free trade zones at Calabar and Onne.

1.4 Types of Location Pull

  • Raw-material-oriented: The raw material loses weight in processing, so the factory sits at the source (cement, sugar, tin smelting, palm-oil milling).
  • Market-oriented: The product is bulky, fragile or perishable, so it is made near buyers (bread, soft drinks).
  • Power-oriented: Energy is the biggest cost, so the plant sits by cheap power (aluminium smelting).
  • Transport-oriented: Heavy imported inputs make ports the best sites (oil refining, flour milling).
  • Footloose: Light, high-value items depend little on any one factor (electronics assembly, printing).

2.4 Problems of Industrialisation in Nigeria

  1. Unstable power supply: Frequent outages raise costs and force firms to run generators.
  2. Poor transport: Bad roads and weak rail links slow delivery and raise prices.
  3. Shortage of capital: Loans are costly and hard to get, especially for small firms.
  4. Shortage of skilled labour and technology: Many machines and experts are imported.
  5. Dependence on imported inputs: Falling naira value raises production costs.
  6. Cheap imports and smuggling: Foreign goods undercut local products.
  7. Inconsistent policies: Changing rules discourage long-term investment.
  8. Insecurity: Attacks and theft raise costs and drive investors away.

Possible Solutions

  • Improve power generation and distribution, including local and renewable power.
  • Build and repair roads, rail lines and ports.
  • Give low-interest loans and tax relief to manufacturers.
  • Train workers in technical schools and support research.
  • Add value to local raw materials and enforce border controls.
  • Keep industrial policies stable and strengthen security.

WEEK 3Geographic Information System (GIS) I: Meaning, Components, Data

Content: What a GIS is, what it is made of, and the kinds of data it handles.

3.1 Meaning of GIS

A Geographic Information System (GIS) is a computer-based system for capturing, storing, managing, analysing and displaying geographic data. It links a place on the map to facts about that place, so a map can answer questions.

3.2 Functions of a GIS

  1. Capture: Collect data from surveys, maps, GPS and images.
  2. Store: Keep data in an organised database.
  3. Manage: Edit, update and combine data sets.
  4. Analyse: Measure distance and area, overlay layers, find the best site or route.
  5. Display: Produce maps, charts and reports.

3.3 Components of a GIS

SoftwarePrograms that store and process the data (for example QGIS, ArcGIS).
DataThe spatial and attribute information: the most important and costly part.
PeopleTrained users who design, run and read the system.
MethodsThe rules and procedures used to collect and analyse data.

3.4 Types of GIS Data

Layers

A GIS keeps each theme (roads, rivers, settlements, vegetation) in its own layer. Layers stack on top of each other, and overlay shows how they relate, for example where a proposed road crosses a flood zone.

4.2 Remote Sensing

Remote sensing is collecting information about the earth's surface from a distance, using sensors on satellites, aircraft or drones. Sensors record light or heat reflected or given off by the ground, and computers turn it into images.

  • Platforms: satellites, aircraft, drones.
  • Uses: mapping land use, tracking floods and desert spread, watching crop health, finding oil-spill areas.
  • Nigerian link: NigeriaSat satellites are run by the National Space Research and Development Agency (NASRDA), Abuja.

4.3 Global Positioning System (GPS)

GPS is a satellite navigation system. A receiver picks signals from several satellites and works out its latitude, longitude and height. It is used in phones, vehicles, ships, aircraft and survey work, and it feeds location data straight into a GIS.

4.4 Applications of GIS

AreaHow GIS is used
Town planningChoosing sites for roads, schools, markets and housing; mapping land use.

Disaster managementMapping flood zones, tracking storms, guiding rescue and relief.
EnvironmentWatching deforestation, erosion, desert spread and pollution.
Transport and navigationFinding the shortest route; managing traffic; tracking vehicles.
HealthMapping disease outbreaks and clinics; planning vaccination.
Resource managementLocating minerals, water and forests.
Security and boundariesMapping borders, land ownership and crime areas.

Advantages of GIS

  • Stores huge data in a small space.
  • Updates maps quickly and cheaply.
  • Combines many kinds of data in one view.
  • Supports faster, better-informed decisions.
  • Makes clear maps and charts.

Limitations of GIS

  • Equipment and software can be costly.
  • Needs trained personnel.
  • Depends on steady electricity and internet.
  • Poor or out-of-date data gives wrong results.
  • Data security and privacy risks.

6.3 Plate Tectonics

The lithosphere is split into large plates that move a few centimetres a year. Movement is driven by heat currents in the mantle. Most folding, faulting, volcanoes and earthquakes happen at plate edges.

BoundaryMovementResult
DivergentPlates move apart.Rift valleys, mid-ocean ridges, volcanoes.
ConvergentPlates move together.Fold mountains, ocean trenches, volcanoes, earthquakes.
TransformPlates slide past each other.Earthquakes.

6.4 Internal (Endogenic) Processes and Landforms

ProcessWhat happensLandforms and examples
FoldingCompression bends rock layers into upfolds (anticlines) and downfolds (synclines).Fold mountains: Himalayas, Alps, Rockies, Andes, Atlas.
FaultingTension or compression cracks rock and blocks move along the crack.Rift valley (East African Rift, Red Sea); block mountain or horst (Black Forest, Vosges); fault scarp.
VolcanismMagma reaches or nears the surface.Extrusive: volcanic cone, crater, caldera, lava plateau. Intrusive: batholith, sill, dyke.
EarthquakeSudden release of stress in the crust sends out shock waves.Ground cracks, scarps, tsunamis at sea.

Volcanoes

  • Active: erupts now or has erupted recently (Mount Etna, Mount Cameroon).
  • Dormant: quiet for a long time but may erupt again.
  • Extinct: not expected to erupt again.

Earthquakes

  • Measured by: seismograph. The Richter scale gives magnitude; the Mercalli scale gives intensity.
  • Causes: plate movement, faulting, volcanic eruption, human activity such as mining and dam filling.
  • Effects: collapsed buildings, fires, landslides, tsunamis, loss of life, breakdown of services.
  • Reducing damage: earthquake-resistant buildings, early warning, land-use control, drills.

Instruments are housed in a Stevenson screen, a white, louvred wooden box on legs that shields them from sun and rain while letting air move through.

9.3 Factors that Influence Climate

  1. Latitude: Places near the equator receive more direct sunlight and are hotter than places near the poles.
  2. Altitude: Temperature falls about 6.5°C for every 1,000 m of height.
  3. Distance from the sea: Coasts have milder temperatures and more rain; interiors are drier with larger temperature ranges.
  4. Ocean currents: Warm currents warm the coast they pass; cold currents cool it and make it dry.
  5. Prevailing winds: Winds from the sea bring moisture; winds from the land are dry.
  6. Relief and aspect: Mountains cause rain on windward slopes and dry rain-shadow on leeward slopes.
  7. Vegetation cover: Forests raise humidity and moderate temperature.

9.4 Types of Rainfall

TypeHow it formsWhere common
ConvectionalStrong heating makes moist air rise, cool and condense, usually with thunder in the afternoon.Equatorial and tropical lands.
Relief (orographic)Moist air is forced up a mountain, cools and drops rain on the windward side.Mountain regions such as the Jos Plateau and Cameroon Mountain.
Frontal (cyclonic)Warm air rises over colder air at a front.Temperate regions.

9.5 Climate of Nigeria

Nigeria lies in the tropics and its weather is controlled by two air masses that meet along the Inter-Tropical Discontinuity (ITD).

Air massSourceCharacter
Tropical Maritime (south-west winds)Atlantic OceanWarm, moist; brings the rainy season.
Tropical Continental (north-east winds)Sahara DesertHot, dry, dusty; brings the dry season and the Harmattan.
  • Rainy season: Long in the south, short in the north.
  • Dry season and Harmattan: Longer in the north; the Harmattan is strongest from December to February.
  • Rainfall pattern: Highest on the coast (over 2,000 mm in the Niger Delta) and falls steadily northward to under 600 mm in the far north.
  • Temperature: High all year; annual range is small in the south and larger in the north.

9.7 Climate Change

  • Causes: burning fossil fuels, deforestation, gas flaring, farming and waste that release greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane).
  • Effects: rising temperatures, changing rainfall, droughts, floods, melting ice, rising sea level, loss of crops.
  • Reducing it: use clean energy, plant trees, end gas flaring, save energy, adapt farming.

10.4 Conservation of Resources

  1. Afforestation and reforestation: plant trees; control logging.
  2. Soil conservation: terracing, contour ploughing, crop rotation, cover crops.
  3. Control of pollution: end gas flaring, treat waste, clean oil spills, fine polluters.
  4. Use of renewable energy: solar, wind and hydro-power.
  5. Recycling and reuse: cut waste and save raw materials.
  6. Protected areas: game reserves and national parks (for example Yankari, Cross River).
  7. Laws and education: enforce environmental law through agencies such as NESREA; teach the public.
  8. Sustainable development: use resources so that future generations still have them.
