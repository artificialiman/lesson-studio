WEEK 1Fundamental and Derived Quantities and Units

Content: Meaning of physical quantities; fundamental quantities and units; derived quantities and units; SI units; dimensional analysis.

1.1 Theory of Quantities and Units

Physics is a quantitative science. Every statement in physics is a statement about measurable quantities. To measure a quantity, we compare it with a standard of the same kind. The result is a number followed by a unit.

A physical quantity is a property that can be measured and expressed as a number with a unit. Examples: length, mass, time, force, energy. Non-examples: beauty, intelligence — they cannot be measured with a standard.

Some quantities are more basic than others. Length, mass and time cannot be expressed in terms of anything simpler. They are fundamental. Speed, force and energy can be expressed in terms of length, mass and time. They are derived.

The choice of which quantities are fundamental is a convention, not a discovery. The SI system fixes seven fundamental quantities. All other quantities in physics are derived from them.

1.2 Laws of Quantities and Units

Law of Homogeneity of Dimensions

In any valid physical equation, the dimensions of all terms must be the same. Quantities of different dimensions cannot be added or equated.

This is not an experimental law. It is a consistency requirement. Any equation that violates it is wrong.

1.3 Principles of Quantities and Units

Principle of Dimensional Consistency

A physical equation is dimensionally consistent only if every term has the same dimensions. This principle is used to check equations and to derive the form of unknown relations.

Principle of Unit Consistency

In any calculation, all quantities must be expressed in units of the same system before they are combined. Mixing systems (e.g. cm with m) produces numerical errors even when the formula is correct.

WEEK 2Measurements of Mass, Weight, Length and Time

Content: Instruments for measurement; accuracy and precision; errors in measurement; significant figures.

2.1 Theory of Measurement

Every measurement is a comparison with a standard. The standard is fixed by international agreement. The act of measurement inevitably introduces some uncertainty, because no instrument is perfect and no observer is perfect.

Mass and weight are different quantities. Mass is the quantity of matter, measured with a beam balance, and is the same everywhere. Weight is the gravitational force on the body, measured with a spring balance, and varies with the local value of g.

Length is measured with rules, calipers and micrometers. The choice of instrument depends on the size of the object and the precision required. A metre rule reads to 1 mm. A vernier caliper reads to 0.1 mm. A micrometer screw gauge reads to 0.01 mm.

Time is measured with clocks and stopwatches. A mechanical stopwatch reads to 0.1 s. A digital stopwatch reads to 0.01 s. The period of a pendulum can be timed over many oscillations to reduce the effect of reaction-time error.

Accuracy and precision are different qualities. A measurement can be precise but not accurate (repeated readings cluster but miss the true value). It can be accurate but not precise (readings scatter around the true value). The best measurements are both.

2.2 Laws of Measurement

Measurement is not governed by laws in the way that motion is. It is governed by principles of error and by the definitions of the quantities. The only regularity that has the status of a law is the rule for combining errors, stated below as a principle.

2.3 Principles of Measurement

Principle of Least Count

The uncertainty of a single reading is half the least count of the instrument, or the least count itself, depending on the convention used. The least count is the smallest change in the quantity that the instrument can detect.

Principle of Repeated Measurement

Repeating a measurement and taking the average reduces the effect of random errors. The average of many readings is more reliable than a single reading.

Principle of Error Combination

When quantities are combined, their errors combine according to fixed rules. For sums and differences, absolute errors add. For products and quotients, fractional errors add.

2.5 Worked Examples

Example 1 — Average of repeated readings

Five readings of a length are 2.34 cm, 2.35 cm, 2.33 cm, 2.36 cm and 2.32 cm. Find the average and state the precision.

Average = (2.34 + 2.35 + 2.33 + 2.36 + 2.32) / 5
Average = 11.70 / 5 = 2.34 cm

Example 2 — Mass and weight

A body has a mass of 5 kg. Find its weight on Earth (g = 9.8 m/s²) and on the Moon (g = 1.6 m/s²).

On Earth: W = mg = 5 × 9.8 = 49 N
On Moon:  W = mg = 5 × 1.6 = 8 N
The mass is 5 kg in both places.

Example 3 — Combining errors

The length of a rectangle is (10.0 ± 0.1) cm and the width is (5.0 ± 0.1) cm. Find the area and its uncertainty.

Area = 10.0 × 5.0 = 50.0 cm²
Fractional error = 0.1/10.0 + 0.1/5.0 = 0.01 + 0.02 = 0.03
Absolute error = 0.03 × 50.0 = 1.5 cm²
Area = (50.0 ± 1.5) cm²

Example 4 — Timing many oscillations

A student times 20 oscillations of a pendulum and obtains 40.0 s. The stopwatch least count is 0.1 s. Find the period and its uncertainty.

Period = 40.0 / 20 = 2.00 s
Uncertainty per oscillation = 0.1 / 20 = 0.005 s
Period = (2.00 ± 0.005) s

Example 5 — Significant figures

A length is measured as 0.00340 m. How many significant figures are present?

Leading zeros are not significant.
The digits 3, 4 and 0 are significant.
The measurement has 3 significant figures.

WEEK 3Position, Distance and Displacement

Content: Concept of position; distance and displacement; applications; distance–time and displacement–time graphs.

3.1 Theory of Position and Displacement

Position requires a reference point. A body has no absolute position. When we say a body is at x = 5 m, we mean 5 m from a chosen origin, in a chosen positive direction. Change the origin and the position changes.

Distance and displacement differ in kind. Distance is the total length of the path travelled, regardless of direction. Displacement is the straight-line change in position, with direction. Distance is a scalar; displacement is a vector.

Displacement does not depend on the path taken. If a body starts at A and ends at B, its displacement is the same whatever route it followed. Distance, in contrast, depends entirely on the route.

A round trip has zero displacement but non-zero distance. This is the clearest way to see that the two quantities are distinct.

3.2 Laws of Position and Displacement

Law of Vector Addition for Displacement

If a body undergoes successive displacements, the resultant displacement is the vector sum of the individual displacements.

This law is the basis for all calculations involving displacement in two or three dimensions.

3.3 Principles of Position and Displacement

Principle of Relativity of Position

Position is always measured relative to a chosen reference point. No position is absolute. Changing the reference point changes all positions by the same vector.

Principle of Path Independence of Displacement

Displacement depends only on the initial and final positions, not on the path taken.

Example 1 — Distance and displacement on a straight line

A boy walks 8 m east, then 3 m west. Find the distance travelled and the displacement.

Distance = 8 + 3 = 11 m
Displacement = 8 − 3 = 5 m east

A runner completes one lap of a circular track of radius 50 m. Find the distance and the displacement.

Displacement = 0 (start and finish are the same point)

Example 3 — Displacement in two dimensions

A girl walks 4 m east, then 3 m north. Find her displacement.

Magnitude = √(4² + 3²) = √25 = 5 m
Direction: tan θ = 3/4 = 0.75, θ = 36.9° north of east

Example 4 — Average speed and average velocity

A cyclist travels 30 km east in 2 h, then 30 km west in 1 h. Find the average speed and the average velocity for the whole journey.

Total distance = 30 + 30 = 60 km
Total time = 2 + 1 = 3 h
Average speed = 60 / 3 = 20 km/h
Total displacement = 30 − 30 = 0 km
Average velocity = 0 / 3 = 0 km/h

Example 5 — Position on a line

A particle is at x = 2 m and moves to x = −5 m. Find its displacement.

Δx = x₂ − x₁ = −5 − 2 = −7 m
The displacement is 7 m in the negative direction.

WEEK 4Scalars and Vectors

Content: Definitions; differences; addition of vectors; resolution of vectors; equilibrium.

4.1 Theory of Scalars and Vectors

Some quantities are fully described by a number and a unit. Mass, time and temperature are of this kind. They are scalars.

Other quantities require a direction as well. Displacement, velocity and force are of this kind. They are vectors. A vector is fully specified only when both its magnitude and its direction are given.

Vectors do not add like ordinary numbers. Adding 3 N and 4 N gives 7 N only if the forces act along the same line. If they act at right angles, the resultant is 5 N. If they act in opposite directions, the resultant is 1 N. The direction matters.

Any vector can be split into two perpendicular components. This is called resolution. It allows vector problems to be reduced to two independent scalar problems along the chosen axes.

4.2 Laws of Scalars and Vectors

Triangle Law of Vector Addition

If two vectors are represented in magnitude and direction by two sides of a triangle taken in order, the resultant is represented by the third side taken in the opposite order.

Parallelogram Law of Vector Addition

If two vectors are represented by two adjacent sides of a parallelogram, the resultant is represented by the diagonal through their common point.

Law of Commutative Addition

Vector addition is commutative: A + B = B + A.

4.3 Principles of Scalars and Vectors

Principle of Vector Resolution

Any vector can be replaced by two perpendicular components whose vector sum equals the original vector. The components are A cos θ and A sin θ when θ is measured from one of the axes.

Principle of Equilibrium

A body is in equilibrium when the vector sum of all forces acting on it is zero. In component form, the sum of the x-components is zero and the sum of the y-components is zero.

Two forces of 10 N and 4 N act on a body. Find the resultant when they act in the same direction and when they act in opposite directions.

A force of 50 N acts at 30° to the horizontal. Find its horizontal and vertical components.

WEEK 6Speed, Velocity and Acceleration

Content: Definitions; equations; graphical representation; uniform and non-uniform motion.

6.1 Theory of Speed, Velocity and Acceleration

Speed measures how fast a body moves, without regard to direction. It is the rate of change of distance. A body can have high speed and zero velocity, as in circular motion at constant speed.

Velocity measures how fast and in what direction. It is the rate of change of displacement. A change in direction at constant speed is a change in velocity, and therefore requires acceleration.

Acceleration measures how quickly velocity changes. It is the rate of change of velocity. A body accelerates if its speed changes, if its direction changes, or if both change.

Graphs make the relationships visible. On a distance–time graph, the slope is speed. On a velocity–time graph, the slope is acceleration and the area under the graph is displacement.

6.2 Laws of Motion (Introductory)

Definition of Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a = (v − u) / t, for uniform acceleration.

6.3 Principles of Speed, Velocity and Acceleration

Principle of Graph Interpretation

On a distance–time graph, the slope at any point gives the speed. On a velocity–time graph, the slope gives the acceleration and the area under the graph gives the displacement.

Principle of Relative Motion

The velocity of a body depends on the frame of reference from which it is measured. A passenger walking forward in a moving train has a different velocity relative to the train and relative to the ground.

6.5 Worked Examples

Example 1 — Average speed

A car travels 180 km in 2.5 h. Find its average speed.

v̄ = d / t = 180 / 2.5 = 72 km/h

Example 2 — Acceleration

A car accelerates from 10 m/s to 30 m/s in 5 s. Find its acceleration.

a = (v − u) / t = (30 − 10) / 5 = 4 m/s²

Example 3 — Retardation

A train moving at 20 m/s brakes uniformly and stops in 8 s. Find its retardation.

a = (v − u) / t = (0 − 20) / 8 = −2.5 m/s²

A body moves with uniform acceleration from 4 m/s to 12 m/s in 6 s. Find the distance travelled.

Average velocity = (4 + 12) / 2 = 8 m/s
Distance = average velocity × time = 8 × 6 = 48 m

Example 5 — Relative velocity

Two cars move in the same direction at 20 m/s and 15 m/s. Find their relative velocity. Then find it when they move in opposite directions.

Same direction: 20 − 15 = 5 m/s
Opposite direction: 20 + 15 = 35 m/s

WEEK 8Motion

Content: Concept of motion; types of motion; equations of uniformly accelerated motion.

8.1 Theory of Motion

Motion is the change in position with time relative to a reference point. A body is in motion if its position changes; it is at rest if its position does not change, relative to the chosen reference.

Motion is relative. A passenger sitting in a moving train is at rest relative to the train and in motion relative to the ground. There is no absolute rest or absolute motion.

Motion can be classified by its path and by its velocity. By path: rectilinear (straight line), circular, oscillatory. By velocity: uniform (constant velocity) or non-uniform (changing velocity).

The equations of uniformly accelerated motion describe the simplest non-uniform case. They apply only when the acceleration is constant in magnitude and direction. They connect five quantities: initial velocity u, final velocity v, acceleration a, time t, and displacement s.

8.2 Laws of Motion

First Equation of Motion

v = u + at

Follows directly from the definition of acceleration.

Second Equation of Motion

s = ut + ½at²

Follows from the average velocity and the definition of acceleration.

Third Equation of Motion

v² = u² + 2as

Eliminates time from the first two equations.

8.3 Principles of Motion

Principle of Independence of Motion Components

Horizontal and vertical motions are independent. A projectile's horizontal motion is unaffected by its vertical motion.

Principle of Relativity of Motion

Whether a body is at rest or in motion depends on the frame of reference of the observer.

A car starts from rest and accelerates at 3 m/s² for 8 s. Find its final velocity.

v = u + at = 0 + 3 × 8 = 24 m/s

A body starts from rest and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 5 s. Find the distance travelled.

A stone is dropped from a height of 80 m. Find the time to reach the ground and the velocity just before impact. Use g = 10 m/s².

A train accelerates from rest at 2 m/s² for 10 s, then travels at constant velocity for 20 s. Find the total distance travelled.

         s₁ = 0 + ½ × 2 × 100 = 100 m
Phase 2: s₂ = 20 × 20 = 400 m
Total distance = 100 + 400 = 500 m

WEEK 9Motion (Continued)

Content: Applications of the equations of motion in real life; projectiles; circular motion (introduction).

9.1 Theory of Applications of Motion

The equations of motion apply to any body moving with constant acceleration. The most important real applications are: falling bodies, vehicles under uniform acceleration or braking, and projectiles near the Earth's surface.

A projectile is a body moving under gravity alone after being given an initial velocity. It has both horizontal and vertical motion. The horizontal motion is at constant velocity; the vertical motion is uniformly accelerated by gravity. The two are independent.

Circular motion at constant speed still involves acceleration. The speed is constant, but the direction of velocity changes, so the body accelerates towards the centre of the circle. This acceleration is called centripetal acceleration.

Real problems often involve more than one phase. The total distance is the sum of the distances in each phase. Each phase is treated with the equations of motion separately.

9.2 Laws of Motion (Continued)

Equations of Motion for Free Fall

For a body falling from rest under gravity:

v = gt
h = ½gt²
v² = 2gh

Equations for a Projectile (Introduction)

Horizontal motion: x = uₓ t. Vertical motion: y = u_y t − ½gt².

9.3 Principles of Motion (Continued)

Principle of Independence of Components

The horizontal and vertical motions of a projectile are independent. Changing the horizontal velocity does not change the time of flight.

Principle of Centripetal Acceleration

A body moving in a circle at constant speed has an acceleration directed towards the centre, of magnitude a = v² / r.

v = u − gt  →  0 = 20 − 10t  →  t = 2 s

Example 3 — Projectile range

A ball is projected at 30 m/s at 45° to the horizontal. Find its range. Use g = 10 m/s².

R = u² sin 2θ / g = 30² × sin 90° / 10
R = 900 × 1 / 10 = 90 m

Example 4 — Centripetal acceleration

A car moves round a circular track of radius 50 m at 20 m/s. Find its centripetal acceleration.

a = v² / r = 20² / 50 = 400 / 50 = 8 m/s²

Example 5 — Multi-phase problem

A car accelerates uniformly from rest to 30 m/s in 10 s, travels at 30 m/s for 20 s, then decelerates uniformly to rest in 5 s. Find the total distance travelled.

Phase 1: s₁ = ½(0 + 30) × 10 = 150 m
Phase 2: s₂ = 30 × 20 = 600 m
Phase 3: s₃ = ½(30 + 0) × 5 = 75 m
Total distance = 150 + 600 + 75 = 825 m

Content: Graphical analysis of motion; problem solving; interpretation of distance–time, velocity–time and acceleration–time graphs.

10.1 Theory of Graphical Analysis

A graph is a compact statement of the relation between two quantities. For motion, the most useful graphs are distance–time, velocity–time, and acceleration–time.

The slope of a graph has physical meaning. On a distance–time graph, the slope is speed. On a velocity–time graph, the slope is acceleration. On an acceleration–time graph, the slope has no simple everyday meaning in this course.

The area under a graph also has physical meaning. On a velocity–time graph, the area under the curve is displacement. On an acceleration–time graph, the area under the curve is change in velocity.

Different segments of a graph tell different parts of the story. A horizontal line on a velocity–time graph means constant velocity. A sloping straight line means uniform acceleration. A curved line means non-uniform acceleration.

10.2 Laws of Graphical Analysis

Slope Rule

The slope of a distance–time graph gives speed. The slope of a velocity–time graph gives acceleration.

Area Rule

The area under a velocity–time graph gives displacement. The area under an acceleration–time graph gives change in velocity.

10.3 Principles of Graphical Analysis

Principle of Graphical Addition

When a graph has several straight-line segments, the total displacement is the sum of the areas under each segment.

Principle of Instantaneous Slope

The instantaneous speed or acceleration at any point is given by the slope of the tangent to the graph at that point.

10.5 Worked Examples

Example 1 — Slope of distance–time graph

A distance–time graph is a straight line through the points (0, 0) and (5, 20). Find the speed.

Speed = slope = (20 − 0) / (5 − 0) = 4 m/s

Example 2 — Area under a velocity–time graph

A velocity–time graph rises uniformly from 0 to 10 m/s over 4 s, then stays at 10 m/s for 6 s. Find the total displacement.

Phase 1 (triangle): area = ½ × 4 × 10 = 20 m
Phase 2 (rectangle): area = 6 × 10 = 60 m
Total displacement = 20 + 60 = 80 m

Example 3 — Acceleration from a velocity–time graph

A velocity–time graph has a straight line from (2, 6) to (8, 24). Find the acceleration.

Acceleration = slope = (24 − 6) / (8 − 2) = 18 / 6 = 3 m/s²

Example 4 — Deceleration from a graph

A velocity–time graph falls uniformly from 15 m/s to 0 m/s over 5 s. Find the deceleration and the distance travelled.

Deceleration = −15 / 5 = −3 m/s²  (magnitude 3 m/s²)
Distance = area of triangle = ½ × 5 × 15 = 37.5 m

Example 5 — Multi-segment graph

A velocity–time graph has three segments: (0–4 s) from 0 to 12 m/s; (4–10 s) constant at 12 m/s; (10–14 s) from 12 m/s to 0. Find the total distance.

Segment 1: ½ × 4 × 12 = 24 m
Segment 2: 6 × 12 = 72 m
Segment 3: ½ × 4 × 12 = 24 m
Total distance = 24 + 72 + 24 = 120 m
