WEEK 1Introduction to Digital Technologies

Content: Meaning of digital technology, digital versus analogue, the information cycle, characteristics, uses, benefits, risks and safe use.

1.1 Meaning of Digital Technology

Digital technology is any tool, device, system or resource that creates, stores, processes or sends information as binary digits (0s and 1s). Computers, phones, the Internet, ATMs, digital cameras, smart TVs and AI assistants are all digital technologies. ICT (Information and Communication Technology) is the wider name for using these tools to handle information and to communicate.

1.3 The Information Cycle

Every digital system follows four steps: Input (data enters) → Processing (data becomes information) → Output (results appear) → Storage (data and results are kept). Communication then carries the results to other people or machines.

1.4 Characteristics of Digital Technology

  • Speed: Billions of operations each second.
  • Accuracy: Correct results if the data and program are correct.
  • Storage: A small chip holds millions of pages.
  • Diligence: No tiredness or loss of focus on repeated work.
  • Versatility: One machine does many kinds of task.
  • Automation: Tasks run without a person at each step.
  • Connectivity: Devices share data over networks.
  • Limits: No judgement of its own; "garbage in, garbage out".

Benefits

  • Faster work and communication.
  • Easy access to information and learning.
  • Lower cost of storing and copying records.
  • New jobs and businesses.
  • Services reach remote places.

Risks

  • Cybercrime, fraud and identity theft.
  • Loss of privacy and data leaks.
  • Job loss from automation.
  • Addiction, eye strain, poor posture.
  • Unequal access (digital divide).
  • Electronic waste.

1.6 Safe and Responsible Use

  • Use long, different passwords and switch on two-step verification.
  • Do not click unknown links or share OTPs, PINs or passwords; this is how phishing works.
  • Keep software and antivirus updated; back up important files.
  • Respect copyright; do not copy or share other people's work as your own.
  • Follow netiquette: be polite, do not bully or spread false information.
  • Know the law: the Cybercrimes Act (2015) and the Nigeria Data Protection Act (2023).

1.7 Careers in Digital Technology

Software developer, web designer, network engineer, cybersecurity analyst, data analyst, database administrator, AI and machine-learning engineer, computer technician, UI/UX designer, digital marketer, IT support officer.

WEEK 2History and Industry of Digital Technologies I: From Drums to Mechanical Calculators

Content: Signalling, counting, writing, printing, mechanical calculation, and the telegraph, the ideas that made the computer possible.

Every digital technology rests on one idea: a message can be turned into a pattern of simple signs, sent or stored, and turned back into the message. The story below follows that idea from the drum to AI. Each era handed a tool to the next.

2.1 Era 1: Signals and Tallies (Prehistory onward)

People first counted on bones and sent messages beyond the reach of a voice. The Ishango bone from Central Africa, about 20,000 years old, carries grouped notches, one of the oldest known counting records. Drums, horns, flutes, whistles, smoke and beacon fires carried alarms and news across valleys. In West Africa the talking drum (the Yoruba dùndún) copies the rising and falling tones of a tonal language, so a drummer can "speak" and villages can relay the message onward. This is telecommunication in its first form: encode, send, relay, decode. Other cultures stored meaning in marks and knots: Ifá divination builds 256 signs (odù) from marks of two kinds, a binary-like code, and the Inca used knotted cords (quipu) as a number memory.

2.2 Era 2: Writing, Numbers and Logic (3400 BCE to 1500 CE)

Writing turned speech into something that could wait. Sumerian clay tablets (cuneiform) and Egyptian hieroglyphs on papyrus made records permanent and carriable. The Phoenician alphabet showed that a few symbols could code every word, which is the same trick a computer uses with bits. Number systems grew alongside: the Sumerians counted in sixty (the source of our 60-minute hour), the abacus sped up sums, and in India the zero and place-value notation came together. When scholars in Baghdad such as Al-Khwarizmi spread this decimal notation and wrote procedures for solving problems, the word algorithm was born from his name. Greek thinkers added logic and the first famous algorithm, while paper from China made records cheap.

2.3 Era 3: Print and Mechanical Calculation (1040 to 1800)

Movable type appeared in China about 1040, and Gutenberg's press (c. 1450) made books cheap in Europe. Cheap text spread literacy, science and mathematics, and produced the scholars who then built calculators. Napier's logarithms (1614) turned multiplication into addition and led to the slide rule. Pascal's gear-driven adding machine (1642) and Leibniz's stepped reckoner (1673) proved that arithmetic can be done by a mechanism. Leibniz also published the binary number system (1703), which is exactly what computers use, though it had no machine to use it yet.

2.4 Era 4: Programs, Logic and the Wire (1804 to 1936)

The Jacquard loom (1804) read patterns from punched cards, the first instructions stored outside a person's head. Charles Babbage designed the Analytical Engine (1837) with a store (memory), a mill (processor) and card input, and Ada Lovelace published the first algorithm meant for a machine (1843), noting that such machines could work on symbols as well as numbers. George Boole turned logic into algebra with 1 and 0 (1854). At the same time the telegraph (Morse, 1837–44) sent dots and dashes, a two-state code, along wires, and the telephone (1876) and radio (1895–1901) followed. Hollerith's punched-card tabulator (1890) ran the US census and grew into the company that became IBM. Turing's 1936 paper described a machine able to compute anything computable, the theory of the computer.

WEEK 3History and Industry of Digital Technologies II: From Vacuum Tubes to AI

Content: Electronic computers, transistors, the personal computer, the Internet and Web, mobile, and the rise of AI and smart systems, with the industry and Nigerian milestones.

3.1 Era 5: Electronic Computers (1937 to 1956)

Claude Shannon showed in 1937 that Boolean logic could be built from electric switches, which joined Boole's algebra to electronics. War then paid for speed: Zuse's Z3 (1941), the Atanasoff–Berry computer (1942), Colossus for codebreaking at Bletchley Park (1943–44) and ENIAC (1945). ENIAC used about 18,000 vacuum tubes and weighed some 30 tonnes. Von Neumann's 1945 design put program and data in the same memory. The Manchester "Baby" ran the first stored program in 1948 and Shannon published information theory the same year, defining the bit. UNIVAC I (1951) was the first computer sold to business and government, and Grace Hopper's compiler, FORTRAN and COBOL let people program in words instead of binary.

3.2 Era 6: Transistors and Integrated Circuits (1947 to 1970)

The transistor (Bell Labs, 1947) replaced the hot, fragile vacuum tube with a small, cool, cheap switch. The integrated circuit (Kilby and Noyce, 1958–59) put many transistors on one chip, and Moore's 1965 observation that chip density doubles about every two years set the pace of the whole industry. Computers moved into banks, universities and governments: the first electronic computer in Nigeria arrived in 1963 to process the 1962/63 census. IBM's System/360 (1964) gave customers a compatible family of machines and BASIC made programming easy to learn. In 1969 ARPANET made its first link, Unix began, and computers helped guide Apollo to the Moon.

3.3 Era 7: The Microprocessor and the Personal Computer (1971 to 1990)

Intel's 4004 (1971) put a whole CPU on one chip, and the same year the first email was sent. Xerox's Alto (1973) showed windows and icons, Ethernet linked machines in a building, and TCP/IP (1974) gave networks a common language. Hobbyists built the Altair (1975), met at the Homebrew Computer Club, and started Microsoft and Apple; the Apple II (1977), VisiCalc (1979) and the IBM PC (1981) gave ordinary offices a reason to buy a computer. On 1 January 1983 ARPANET switched to TCP/IP, and the Internet as we know it began. Macintosh and Windows brought the graphical interface to everyone. In Nigeria, the 1977 Indigenisation Decree led IBM to leave the market and opened it to local computer vendors.

3.4 Era 8: The Web and Mobile (1991 to 2010)

The Web opened to the public in 1991, Linus Torvalds released the Linux kernel the same year, and the Mosaic and Netscape browsers (1993–94) made the Web popular. Amazon (1994) and Google (1998) turned it into commerce and search, and Wikipedia (2001) turned it into a shared library. In Nigeria the NCC was set up in 1992, and the Nigerian-assembled Obodex PC appeared the same year. The launch of GSM mobile service in 2001 was the big change: phones reached millions who had never had a landline. 9.1 Meaning

An operating system (OS) is system software that manages the computer's hardware and software and provides a platform on which other programs run. It sits between the user, the applications and the hardware.

9.2 Functions of an Operating System

  1. Process management: decides which program uses the CPU and when.
  2. Memory management: gives each program RAM and frees it afterwards.
  3. File management: creates, names, stores, finds and protects files and folders.
  4. Device management: controls input, output and storage devices through drivers.
  5. User interface: lets the user communicate with the computer.
  6. Security: logins, passwords and permissions.
  7. Networking: connects the machine to other machines and the Internet.

9.5 The Boot Process

  1. Power is switched on; the CPU starts running firmware (BIOS or UEFI) stored in ROM.
  2. POST (Power-On Self-Test) checks the CPU, RAM and devices.
  3. The firmware finds the bootstrap loader on the storage device.
  4. The loader loads the kernel of the OS into RAM.
  5. The OS starts its services and drivers and shows the login or desktop screen.

Cold boot: starting from power off. Warm boot: restarting without cutting power.

WEEK 10Data Representation

Content: How numbers, text, images, sound and video are stored as bits; file-size formulas; compression; error checking.

10.1 Why Everything Becomes Bits

Computer circuits have two states, so all data (numbers, letters, pictures, sound, video) must be turned into patterns of 0s and 1s. Digitisation is the conversion of analogue data to digital; the reverse is done when a speaker plays sound or a screen shows a picture.

10.2 Numbers

  • Unsigned integers: plain binary; 8 bits hold 0 to 255.
  • Signed integers: two's complement; the leftmost bit shows the sign (0 positive, 1 negative); 8 bits hold −128 to +127.
  • Real numbers: stored in floating-point form (a sign, a fraction part and an exponent).

10.3 Text

Each character has a numeric code from a character set. ASCII uses 7 bits (128 characters); extended ASCII uses 8 bits (256). Unicode covers almost all writing systems and emoji; its UTF-8 form uses 1 to 4 bytes per character.

CharacterDecimal codeBinary (8 bits)
Space3200100000

A6501000001

Capital and small letters differ by 32, so 'a' = 'A' + 32. A text file of n characters in ASCII takes about n bytes.

10.4 Images

A bitmap image is a grid of pixels; each pixel's colour is stored in a set number of bits (colour depth). More pixels and greater depth mean more detail and a bigger file.

Colour depthColours possible
1 bit2 (black and white)
8 bits256
24 bits (RGB, 8 each)16,777,216

Image file size

size (bytes) = width × height × colour depth (bits) ÷ 8
Example: 800 × 600 pixels, 24-bit colour
= 800 × 600 × 24 ÷ 8 = 1,440,000 bytes ≈ 1.4 MB (before compression)

A vector graphic stores shapes as formulas, so it stays sharp when enlarged and is best for logos and diagrams (SVG).

10.5 Sound

Sound is an analogue wave. The computer measures it many times a second (sampling) and stores each measure in a set number of bits (bit depth). A higher sampling rate and bit depth give better quality and bigger files.

Sound file size

size (bytes) = sampling rate (Hz) × bit depth × channels × seconds ÷ 8
Example: CD quality 44,100 Hz, 16-bit, stereo (2 channels), 60 seconds
= 44,100 × 16 × 2 × 60 ÷ 8 = 10,584,000 bytes ≈ 10.6 MB

10.6 Video

Video is a sequence of still images (frames) shown at a frame rate (for example 25 or 30 frames per second) together with a sound track. Uncompressed video is huge, so it is always compressed.

10.7 Compression

TypeHow it worksExamples
LosslessRewrites data more compactly; original restored exactlyZIP, PNG, FLAC
LossyRemoves detail people are unlikely to notice; cannot be undoneJPEG, MP3, MP4

Uses: save storage space and send files faster.

10.8 Detecting Errors: Parity

A parity bit is added to each group of bits so the total number of 1s is always even (even parity) or always odd (odd parity). If the count is wrong on arrival, an error occurred.

Data 1011001 has four 1s.
Even parity: add 0 → 10110010     Odd parity: add 1 → 10110011

Parity detects a single-bit error but cannot say which bit is wrong.

10.9 Analogue to Digital and Back

  1. A sensor or microphone produces an analogue signal.
  2. An analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) samples it and turns the samples into binary.
  3. The computer stores and processes the binary data.
  4. A digital-to-analogue converter (DAC) turns it back into a signal for a speaker or screen.
