1.1 Introduction

Horticultural crop improvement (plant breeding) is the art and science of changing the genetic traits of plants to develop new varieties with more desirable characteristics. It is a deliberate, human-driven evolution of crops to better suit human needs.

1.2 Aims of Crop Improvement

AimExplanation and examples
1. Increased yieldCrops that produce more food, fibre or ornamental value per unit area — tomatoes with more fruits per plant, maize with larger cobs.
2. Improved quality
  • Nutritional: higher vitamin, protein or mineral content (e.g. “Golden Rice” enriched with Vitamin A).
  • Eating: better taste, flavour, tenderness or texture.
  • Aesthetic: brighter colours, larger flowers, more attractive shapes.
  • Keeping: longer shelf-life for fruits and vegetables, reducing post-harvest losses.
3. Pest and disease resistancePlants that can naturally defend themselves against specific insects, fungi, bacteria or viruses — e.g. cassava resistant to Cassava Mosaic Disease.
4. Abiotic stress tolerance “Hardy” crops that withstand difficult conditions: drought (maize that survives with less water), salinity (coastal or irrigated areas), heat or cold.
5. Desirable plant architectureModifying plant shape or structure — dwarf rice that doesn’t fall over; climbing bean varieties for vertical gardens.
6. Ease of harvestingCrops that mature uniformly for mechanical harvesting or have fewer thorns.

1.3 Methods of Crop Improvement

1. Selection

The oldest and most basic method. Identify plants with desirable traits and use them to create the next generation.

TypeProcessUse
Mass selection A large number of plants (a “mass”) are evaluated in the field. The “best”-looking plants (phenotype) are selected and harvested. Their seeds are bulked together and planted to form the next generation. Common for cross-pollinated crops. Simple and cheap but slow and not very precise.
Pure-line selection Used for self-pollinating crops. A breeder selects a single superior plant. Its seeds are collected and grown out. Since the plant self-pollinates, all offspring are genetically very similar (a “pure line”). Good for creating highly uniform varieties (e.g. a new tomato or bean variety).
Clonal selection Used for crops propagated vegetatively (asexually) like cassava, yams, bananas and potatoes. A breeder identifies a single superior plant (e.g. a cassava plant with huge tubers). Cuttings (“clones”) are taken and multiplied. All offspring are genetically identical to the superior parent.

2. Hybridisation (Cross-Breeding)

The most powerful traditional breeding method. It involves crossing two parent plants with different desirable traits to produce offspring that combines those traits.

StepExplanation
1. Parent selectionChoose two parents. Example: Parent A (tomato) — high-yielding but disease-susceptible. Parent B (tomato) — low-yielding but disease-resistant. Goal: create a variety that is high-yielding AND disease-resistant.
2. EmasculationTo prevent self-pollination, carefully remove the male parts (stamens/anthers) from the flower of Parent A (the “female” parent) before it produces pollen.
3. PollinationCollect pollen from the flower of Parent B (the “male” parent); manually transfer it to the stigma of the emasculated flower of Parent A.
4. BaggingCover the pollinated flower with a small bag (paper or cloth) to prevent stray, unwanted pollen from landing on it.
5. Harvesting F1The flower develops into a fruit. The seeds inside are the First Filial (F1) generation — they contain genes from both parents.
6. Growing F1 and F2The F1 seeds are planted; the F1 plants are often self-pollinated to produce the Second Filial (F2) generation.
7. SelectionThe F2 generation is where all the gene combinations appear. The breeder must plant thousands of F2 seeds and select the very few individual plants that show the desired combination (high yield + disease resistance).
8. StabilisationThese selected plants are grown and re-selected over many (6–10) generations until their desirable traits are “fixed” (i.e. they breed true).

Types of Hybridisation

  • Intraspecific: crossing two different varieties within the same species (Zea mays “A” × Zea mays “B”). The most common type.
  • Interspecific: crossing two different (but related) species — e.g. to transfer a wild, disease-resistant gene into a cultivated species.

2.1 Introduction

Genetics is the science of heredity. It explains how traits are passed from parents to offspring. This is the “science” behind the “art” of plant breeding. The foundation of this science was laid by Gregor Mendel.

2.2 Key Genetic Terminology

TermDefinitionExample
GeneThe basic unit of heredity — a segment of DNA that codes for a specific trait.The gene for flower colour.
AlleleDifferent forms of the same gene.Alleles “R” (red) and “r” (white) for the flower-colour gene.
PhenotypeThe physical appearance or observable trait.“The flower is red.”
GenotypeThe genetic makeup — the combination of alleles.“RR,” “Rr” or “rr.”
HomozygousHaving two identical alleles for a trait (true-breeding).RR or rr.
HeterozygousHaving two different alleles for a trait.Rr.
Dominant alleleMasks the effect of a recessive allele when present. Capital letter.RR and Rr both give red flowers.
Recessive alleleMasked by a dominant allele; only expressed when homozygous. Small letter.rr gives white flowers.

2.3 Mendel’s Laws of Heredity

Mendel’s First Law — The Law of Segregation

  • Concept: for any trait, an organism inherits two alleles, one from each parent. These two alleles segregate (separate) during gamete formation, so each gamete contains only one allele for that trait.
  • Example (monohybrid cross): a homozygous dominant plant (RR — red) is crossed with a homozygous recessive plant (rr — white).
    • P generation: RR × rr
    • Gametes: the RR parent only makes R; the rr parent only makes r.
    • F1 generation: all offspring receive one R and one r → all are Rr (genotype) and all have red flowers (phenotype).
    • F1 self-cross: Rr × Rr.
    • Gametes: each F1 parent produces 50% R and 50% r.

  • Phenotypic ratio: 3 red : 1 white (75% red, 25% white).
  • Genotypic ratio: 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr (25% homozygous dominant, 50% heterozygous, 25% homozygous recessive).
  • Significance: explains why traits can “disappear” in F1 (white) and “reappear” in F2.

Mendel’s Second Law — The Law of Independent Assortment

  • Concept: alleles for different traits are inherited independently of one another. The inheritance of one trait (like flower colour) does not affect the inheritance of another trait (like plant height).
  • Example (dihybrid cross): a parent that is heterozygous for two traits — Round, Yellow seeds (RrYy).
    • The gene for shape (R/r) and the gene for colour (Y/y) are on different chromosomes.
    • When this plant makes gametes, the alleles “assort independently”. It can make four types of gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry.
    • Two such plants crossed (RrYy × RrYy) give the classic 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 phenotypic ratio — 9 round-yellow, 3 round-green, 3 wrinkled-yellow, 1 wrinkled-green.
  • Significance: the scientific reason why hybridisation works — new combinations of traits can be created, e.g. “round seeds” from one parent and “yellow colour” from another.

2.4 Applications in Crop Production

  1. Systematic breeding: Mendel’s laws turned plant breeding from guesswork into a predictive science. Breeders use Punnett squares to predict the outcomes of their crosses.
  2. Hybrid vigour (heterosis): the F1 hybrid (e.g. Rr) is superior to both homozygous parents (RR and rr) — bigger, more disease-resistant, higher-yielding. The entire hybrid-seed industry (e.g. hybrid maize) is built on this. Farmers buy F1 seeds, get the hybrid vigour, but cannot save the seeds, as F2 segregates and loses the superior traits.
  3. Understanding recessive traits: breeders can now understand why an undesirable trait (like disease susceptibility) might be “hidden” as a recessive allele in a heterozygous plant.

3.1 Introduction

Biotechnology involves using living organisms or their products to make or modify products for a specific use. In horticulture it allows for rapid and precise plant improvement, far beyond the speed of traditional breeding.

3.2 Tissue Culture (Micropropagation)

A. Definition

An in-vitro (in glass) technique of growing new plants from tiny pieces of plant tissue (leaf, stem, root tip) on a sterile, artificial nutrient medium (a jelly). The small piece of tissue used is called an explant. The key principle is totipotency — the ability of a single plant cell to grow into a complete, new plant.

B. The Process

StepExplanation
1. Explant selectionA small, healthy piece of the desired “mother plant” is selected (meristem tip, leaf section, bud).
2. SterilisationThe most critical step. The explant is surface-sterilised (bleach or alcohol) to kill fungi and bacteria. The nutrient medium and tools are also sterilised in an autoclave. Work is done in a laminar flow hood which provides sterile air.
3. InoculationThe sterile explant is placed onto the sterile nutrient medium (a gel containing water, sugar, macro/micro-nutrients and vitamins) in a test tube or jar.
4. IncubationThe jar is placed in a growth room with controlled light and temperature.
5. Callus & shoot formationThe explant cells divide and form an undifferentiated mass called a callus. By adding specific plant hormones (auxins and cytokinins) the breeder encourages the callus to form shoots.
6. RootingThe new, small shoots are transferred to a different medium containing hormones that promote root growth.
7. Acclimatisation (hardening off)The small “plantlets” are removed from the sterile, high-humidity jars. Their roots are washed and they are planted in sterile soil in a greenhouse. They are slowly adapted to the “real world” — lower humidity and natural soil.

C. Applications of Tissue Culture

  1. Mass propagation (cloning): one explant can produce thousands or millions of genetically identical (clonal) plantlets in a short time — vital for commercial production of ornamentals (orchids) and crops like bananas, plantains and yams.
  2. Production of disease-free plants: the meristem tip is often free of viruses, even if the mother plant is infected. Culturing the meristem produces “clean” planting material.
  3. Germplasm conservation: varieties can be stored as tiny, slow-growing tissue cultures in a lab (“gene bank”), saving space and protecting against field losses.
  4. Speeding up breeding: allows breeders to grow new hybrids or conduct genetic experiments much faster than in the field.

3.3 Genetic Modification (GM Crops)

A. Definition

Genetic Modification (GM), or genetic engineering, directly inserts a specific gene from one organism (a bacterium, virus or another plant) into the DNA of a crop. The resulting plant is a Genetically Modified Organism (GMO) or a “transgenic” plant. This is different from hybridisation, which can only cross related species.

B. The Process (Simplified)

  1. Gene identification: identify a useful gene — e.g. the Bt gene from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis, which is toxic to insects.
  2. Gene isolation: the gene is “cut out” using special restriction enzymes.
  3. Gene insertion (transformation): the isolated gene is inserted into the plant’s cells. Two common methods:
    • Gene gun: the gene is coated onto tiny gold particles and “shot” into the plant cells.
    • Agrobacterium tumefaciens: a natural soil bacterium that transfers its own DNA (on a “Ti plasmid”) into a plant. Scientists replace the bacterium’s DNA with the desired gene.
  4. Selection & regeneration: the cells are grown using tissue culture. The medium contains an “antibiotic marker” so only cells that took up the new gene survive. These cells are regenerated into whole plants.

C. Applications of GM Crops

ApplicationExplanation and examples
Insect resistanceBt Cotton and Bt Maize produce a protein that kills specific insect pests (like the bollworm), reducing the need for chemical insecticides.
Herbicide tolerance“Roundup Ready” soybeans or maize are engineered to be resistant to the herbicide glyphosate. This allows the farmer to spray the entire field, killing weeds but not the crop.
Nutritional enhancement“Golden Rice” is engineered to produce beta-carotene, a precursor to Vitamin A, to help combat vitamin deficiency in developing countries.
Disease resistanceEngineering plants to be resistant to viruses (e.g. Papaya Ringspot Virus).

D. Benefits and Concerns

BenefitsConcerns (the controversy)
  • Increased yields.
  • Reduced pesticide use.
  • Improved nutrition.
  • Enhanced food security.
  • Environmental: risk of “superweeds” (herbicide resistance spreading) or harm to non-target insects.
  • Human health: concerns (largely unproven) about long-term safety or allergies.
  • Economic: control of seeds by a few large corporations; farmers cannot save GM seeds.

4.1 Introduction

Organic horticulture is a holistic production system that avoids the use of synthetic (man-made) fertilisers, pesticides and herbicides. It focuses on building healthy soil and working with natural ecological cycles to produce nutritious food.

4.2 Principles of Organic Horticulture

  1. Principle of health — aims to produce healthy soil, healthy plants, healthy animals and healthy people; avoids inputs that can harm health.
  2. Principle of ecology — works with living ecological systems, not against them; emphasises recycling, biodiversity and balance in nature.
  3. Principle of fairness — seeks fairness in relationships with all involved, from farmers and workers to consumers.
  4. Principle of care — emphasises caution and responsibility in managing the farm to protect the environment and the well-being of future generations.

4.3 Key Practices in Organic Horticulture

A. Soil Fertility Management — “Feed the soil, not the plant.”

PracticeExplanation
CompostingThe controlled decomposition of organic matter (kitchen scraps, leaves, grass clippings, small amounts of manure) by microorganisms. The result, “compost,” is a dark, nutrient-rich soil conditioner.
Animal manuresUsing waste from poultry, goats or cattle. It must be composted or “aged” first — raw manure can burn plants and contain pathogens.
Green manures / cover croppingCover crop: a crop (Mucuna, Centrosema, Pueraria) planted between cash crops or during the off-season to cover bare soil. Green manure: a cover crop specifically grown to be cut down and tilled into the soil to add organic matter. Leguminous cover crops (beans, peas) are special because they “fix” nitrogen from the air into the soil.
Crop rotationNot planting the same crop in the same spot year after year. e.g. plant maize (heavy feeder) → beans (nitrogen fixer) → yams (root crop). Breaks pest cycles and balances soil nutrient use.

B. Pest and Disease Control — Not to eliminate all pests, but to keep them at a manageable, non-damaging level.

CategoryMethods
Cultural controlCrop rotation — prevents soil-borne diseases from building up. Intercropping — planting two or more different crops together (e.g. maize and cassava) confuses pests. Sanitation — removing and destroying diseased plants and crop debris.
Biological controlEncouraging “beneficial” insects (ladybugs, which eat aphids) by planting flowers they like. Using microbial pesticides like Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) — the natural bacteria, not the GM crop.
Physical/mechanicalHand-picking large insects (caterpillars) off plants. Using insect traps or reflective mulches.
Natural pesticidesPlant-based sprays like Neem oil (Azadirachta indica), garlic spray or hot-pepper spray. These are biodegradable but must still be used with care.

C. Weed Control

  • Mulching: covering the soil surface with organic materials (straw, grass clippings, wood chips). Blocks sunlight, preventing weed seeds from germinating; also conserves soil moisture.
  • Cover cropping: a dense cover crop will “choke out” and smother weeds.
  • Hand weeding / hoeing: mechanical removal of weeds.

4.4 Benefits and Challenges

BenefitsChallenges
  • Environmental: no chemical runoff to pollute water; improves soil health and biodiversity.
  • Health: food is free of synthetic pesticide residues.
  • Sustainability: builds long-term soil fertility.
  • Economic: (potentially) higher “premium” price for certified organic food; lower cost of inputs (no chemicals).
  • Labour intensive: weeding and compost-making require more physical work.
  • Lower yields: (sometimes, especially in the transition period).
  • Certification: the process to become “Certified Organic” can be complex and expensive for small farmers.

6.1 Introduction

A greenhouse (or glasshouse) is a structure designed to provide a controlled, protected environment for growing crops. It is covered with a transparent or translucent material (glass or plastic) that allows sunlight to enter and heats the interior.

6.2 Structures and Types

TypeStructureProsCons
Glasshouse Traditional structure — glass panels in a metal (aluminium) frame. Very long-lasting (30+ years); best light transmission. Very expensive; heavy; fragile (can break); traps heat (a major problem in Nigeria).
Polytunnel (hoop house) Arched hoops (metal or PVC) covered with a single or double layer of UV-stabilised polyethylene plastic. Much cheaper; easy to build; good for extending seasons. Plastic needs replacing every 3–5 years; can be damaged by wind.
Net house (shade house) A simple frame (wood or metal) covered with shade net or insect-proof netting. Very suitable for Nigerian/tropical climates. Does not trap heat; provides shade (reducing heat stress); physically excludes insect pests (whiteflies). Does not protect from heavy rain.

6.3 Environmental Control (The “Technology”)

A greenhouse allows the grower to become a “weather man” and control key factors.

FactorControls and methods
Temperature Cooling (critical in Nigeria): ventilation is key — side-vents that roll up, roof vents that open, large “extraction” fans to pull hot air out. “Evaporative cooling” (using wet pads and fans) is a more advanced method. Heating (for cold climates/nights): electric or gas heaters.
Light Reducing: shade cloths (nets) over the roof to reduce intense sunlight. Adding: artificial “grow lights” (LEDs) to supplement light on cloudy days or extend the “day length”.
Water & humidity Irrigation: drip irrigation (delivering water directly to the plant’s roots) is the most common and efficient method. Humidity: misters or foggers can be used to raise humidity for seedlings or certain crops.
Pest & disease control The structure is a physical barrier. Double-door entry systems act as an “airlock” to stop insects from flying in. Fine insect netting on all vents and openings.

6.4 Benefits of Greenhouse Technology

  1. Year-round production — grow high-value vegetables (tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers) even in the off-season.
  2. Protection from elements — from heavy rain (which spreads disease), strong winds and hailstones.
  3. Pest and disease control — drastically reduces pressure, leading to less pesticide use and higher quality produce.
  4. Increased yield — optimised conditions (water, nutrients) give much higher yields per square metre.
  5. Water efficiency — drip irrigation in a greenhouse uses up to 90% less water than traditional field irrigation.

6.5 Challenges in Nigeria

  1. High initial cost — setup for glasshouses or well-equipped polytunnels is very high.
  2. Overheating — the number one challenge. A poorly ventilated greenhouse becomes an “oven” in the tropical sun and kills plants.
  3. Constant power supply — advanced systems (fans, pumps, coolers) require reliable electricity — a major problem (requiring generators or large solar setups).
  4. Technical know-how — managing a greenhouse environment requires training and skill.
  5. Availability of materials — sourcing high-quality UV-treated plastic, shade nets and parts can be difficult or expensive.

  2. Compare a “Glasshouse” and a “Polytunnel” in terms of cost and durability.

  4. List three environmental factors a grower can control in a greenhouse.
  5. What is the single biggest challenge of using greenhouse technology in Nigeria, and what is the primary method to combat it?

8.1 Definitions

  • Urban horticulture: growing horticultural crops (fruits, vegetables, flowers) inside city boundaries — backyards, balconies, rooftops and community gardens.
  • Peri-urban horticulture: growing crops on the immediate fringes or edges of a city, often on small-scale commercial farms, to supply the city market.

8.2 Importance of Urban/Peri-Urban Horticulture

  1. Food security & nutrition — direct access to fresh, nutritious food (especially vegetables) for city dwellers, improving family diets.
  2. Income generation — jobs and business opportunities (selling fresh produce, seedlings or compost).
  3. Environmental improvement
    • “Greens” the city, improving air quality and reducing the “urban heat island” effect.
    • Reduces “food miles” (the distance food travels from farm to plate), saving energy and reducing carbon emissions.
    • Waste recycling — can utilise composted urban organic waste.
  4. Social & educational — community gardens bring people together; school gardens are powerful teaching tools.

8.3 Practices and Systems for Urban Horticulture

Since space is the biggest limitation, these methods focus on growing in small, intensive spaces.

PracticeExplanation
Container gardeningGrowing plants in any available container — plastic pots, buckets, “grow bags,” old car tyres, wooden boxes.
Sack (bag) gardeningFilling large woven sacks (like those for rice or garri) with soil and compost. Plants (like leafy greens) are planted on the top and in holes cut in the sides. Very space-efficient.

8.4 Hydroponics — Soil-Less Horticulture

An advanced form of urban horticulture that does not use soil.

Definition: a method of growing plants in a “nutrient solution” (water mixed with all the essential plant nutrients) or in an inert growing medium (gravel, perlite, coconut coir).

Common Systems

  • Nutrient Film Technique (NFT): a thin “film” of nutrient water flows continuously in a channel over the bare plant roots. Common for lettuce.
  • Deep Water Culture (DWC): plant roots are suspended directly in a large, aerated (bubbling) tank of nutrient solution.

Pros and Cons

Pros (Advantages)Cons (Disadvantages)
  • No soil needed — can be done anywhere (a roof, a warehouse, a balcony).
  • Extremely fast growth — plants get exactly the nutrients they need, 24/7.
  • Very high yield — more food in less space.
  • Water efficient — the water is recycled; uses 70–90% less water than soil farming.
  • High initial cost — pumps, tanks, plumbing, nutrient salts.
  • Technical skill — requires knowledge of chemistry to mix the nutrient solution.
  • Reliant on electricity — pumps and aerators must run 24/7. A power cut can kill the entire crop.

9.1 Meaning and Definition

Agroforestry is a sustainable land-management system that deliberately combines woody perennials (trees, shrubs) with agricultural crops and/or livestock, either at the same time or in sequence, on the same piece of land. It is the opposite of “monoculture” (planting only one crop). A traditional Nigerian compound farm (with fruit trees, yams, vegetables and chickens) is a classic example of agroforestry.

9.2 Agroforestry Practices (Systems)

SystemExplanation
Alley cropping (hedgerow intercropping) Planting food crops (maize, cassava, vegetables) in the “alleys” or “lanes” between rows of fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing trees or shrubs (Leucaena, Gliricidia, Faidherbia albida). The trees are pruned regularly, and the leaves (rich in nitrogen) are used as mulch and “green manure” for the crops in the alleys.
Multi-storey cropping (home gardens) A system that copies a natural forest with different “layers”: Top layer — coconut, oil palm, mango, ogbono. Middle layer — pawpaw, banana, plantain, cocoa, coffee. Low layer — cassava, yams (with trellises), cocoyam, peppers. Ground cover — pumpkin, melon, sweet potato.
Silvopasture Combining trees with pasture (grass) and livestock (animals). Animals (goats, sheep, cattle) graze on the grass growing under the trees. The trees provide shade for the animals and can also be a source of fodder.
Windbreaks / shelterbelts One or more rows of trees and shrubs planted around the border of a farm or field to protect crops, soil and animals from strong, damaging winds. They also prevent wind erosion.

9.3 Importance and Benefits of Agroforestry

  1. Improved soil fertility — leguminous trees (like Gliricidia) “fix” nitrogen from the air; tree leaf litter (mulch) decomposes, adding rich organic matter to the soil; tree roots can “pump” nutrients from deep in the soil back up to the surface.
  2. Soil and water conservation — tree roots bind the soil, preventing water and wind erosion; the canopy slows down heavy rain so it “soaks in” rather than “runs off”; mulch on the soil surface reduces water evaporation.
  3. Diversified income (economic security) — the farmer is not reliant on one crop. They get short-term income (food crops), medium-term income (fruit) and long-term income (timber, firewood). This spreads the risk.
  4. Improved microclimate — the tree canopy provides shade, which lowers soil temperature and reduces heat stress on crops and animals.
  5. Environmental benefits — increases biodiversity (a habitat for birds and “good” insects); sequesters carbon (trees absorb CO2), helping to fight climate change.

10.1 Introduction

Sustainable horticulture means growing food in a way that is:

  1. Ecologically sound — does not harm the environment (soil, water, air).
  2. Economically viable — farmers can make a good living.
  3. Socially just — good for families and communities.

The goal is to meet our present need for food without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. This topic ties together everything we have learned this term.

10.2 Key Pillars of Environmental Sustainability

A. Soil Conservation and Health

The problem: soil erosion, loss of organic matter and “dead” soil. Sustainable practices:

PracticeExplanation
Conservation tillage (zero/minimum tillage)A method of planting without ploughing or tilling the soil. A new crop is planted directly into the “stubble” (residue) of the previous crop. This protects the soil from erosion, saves water and builds organic matter.
Cover cropping & mulchingThe number-one rule of soil conservation: never leave the soil bare. A cover crop (like Mucuna) or a layer of mulch (like straw) acts as “armour” for the soil, protecting it from sun, wind and rain.
Contour ploughing / terracingOn sloping land, all farming activities (ploughing, planting) are done across the slope (on the contour), not up and down it. This creates small ridges that trap water and prevent it from rushing downhill and causing erosion.

B. Water Management and Conservation

The problem: water is scarce, rainfall is unreliable, traditional irrigation is wasteful. Sustainable practices:

  • Drip irrigation: the most efficient method — delivers water in slow “drips” directly to the root zone of each plant. This eliminates evaporation and runoff, saving 70–90% of water.
  • Rainwater harvesting: collecting and storing rainwater. Small-scale: from roofs into plastic tanks. Large-scale: building small farm ponds or “earth dams” to capture and store runoff water for use in the dry season.
  • Mulching: a layer of mulch is also one of the best water-conservation techniques, as it dramatically reduces water loss from evaporation at the soil surface.

C. Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

The problem: over-reliance on chemical pesticides kills beneficial insects, pollutes water and creates “resistant” pests. IPM is a “middle-ground” approach — a pyramid strategy.

LevelWhat is done
1. Base — Prevention (cultural)Use cultural methods first — crop rotation, resistant varieties, farm sanitation.
2. Physical / mechanicalUse physical controls — traps, hand-picking, barriers.
3. BiologicalUse biological controls — beneficial insects.
4. Top — Chemicals (last resort)Use chemical pesticides only when the pest level is high enough to cause economic damage, and use the least-toxic, most-specific chemical available.

D. Enhancing Biodiversity

The problem: monoculture (planting a single crop) is an “invitation” to pests and diseases. Sustainable practices: polyculture/intercropping; agroforestry (integrating trees creates habitats); planting flower borders — attracts pollinators (bees) and beneficial insects (pest predators).
