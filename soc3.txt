1. Define "Citizenship" and list the three ways it can be acquired (Birth, Registration,
Naturalization).
2. Differentiate between a "Right" and a "Responsibility" and explain their interdependence.
2
3. Define "Constitution" and state its primary importance.
4. List the three arms of government and their basic functions.
5. Recall key civic virtues like honesty, tolerance, and cooperation.
Lesson Content:
1. Foundational Concepts Review This lesson revisits the core ideas of Social and Citizenship
Studies from JSS1 and JSS2, which form the foundation for JSS3.
 A. Citizenship:
o Meaning: Citizenship is the status of being a legal member of a specific country.
A citizen is a member of a political community (a country) who enjoys certain
rights and privileges and is expected to perform specific duties.
o Acquisition (Recap):
1. By Birth (Indigene): Being born in a country or having parents (one or
both) who are citizens of that country. This is the most common way.
2. By Registration: (e.g., A woman from Ghana who marries a Nigerian
man can register to become a citizen).
3. By Naturalization: A foreigner who has lived in a country for a long,
continuous period (e.g., 15 years in Nigeria), speaks the language, and
shows good character can apply to become a citizen.
 B. Rights and Responsibilities:
o Rights: The privileges, freedoms, and entitlements guaranteed to every citizen by
the constitution (e.g., Right to life, Right to freedom of speech, Right to vote,
Right to education).
o Responsibilities (Duties): The obligations and duties that a citizen must perform
for the good of the community and the state.
o Interdependence: Rights and responsibilities are linked. To enjoy your rights,
you must perform your duties.
 Example: You have the right to security (provided by the police), but you
have the responsibility to obey the law and report crimes.
3
 Example: You have the right to vote, but you have the responsibility to
vote wisely and not sell your vote.
 C. Constitution:
o Meaning: A constitution is the supreme lawbook of a country. It is a set of
fundamental rules, principles, and laws that defines the powers of the government
and the rights of the citizens.
o Importance: It is the "social contract" between the government and the people. It
guides the government, prevents any one person from becoming too powerful (a
dictator), and protects the citizens from abuse.
 D. Arms of Government:
1. The Legislature (National Assembly): Makes the laws. (e.g., The Senate and
House of Representatives).
2. The Executive (Presidency/Governors): Implements and enforces the laws. (e.g.,
The President, Ministers, Police).
3. The Judiciary (Courts): Interprets the laws and punishes lawbreakers. (e.g.,
Judges, Magistrates).

WEEK 2: DEMOCRACY AND HUMAN RIGHTS
Content: Relationship between democracy and human rights.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "Democracy."
2. Define "Human Rights."
3. Explain the direct relationship between democracy and the protection of human rights.
4. Identify features of a democratic society that promote human rights.
5. Differentiate between how democracies and dictatorships treat human rights.
Lesson Content:
6
1. Definition of Key Terms
 Democracy:
o Origin: From the Greek words 'Demos' (the people) and 'Kratos' (rule or power).
o Meaning: A system of government by the people.
o Abraham Lincoln's Definition: "Government of the people, by the people, and
for the people."
o In Practice: It is a system where citizens elect representatives to make decisions
on their behalf through free and fair elections. It is based on the will of the
majority while protecting the rights of the minority.
 Human Rights:
o Meaning: Human Rights are the basic, fundamental rights and freedoms that
belong to every single person in the world, from birth until death.
o Characteristics: They are inalienable (cannot be taken away), universal (apply
to everyone, everywhere), and indivisible (all rights are equally important).
o Examples (from the UNDHR): The right to life, freedom from slavery, freedom
from torture, freedom of opinion and expression, the right to education, the right
to a fair trial.
2. The Relationship Between Democracy and Human Rights Democracy and human rights
are interdependent and mutually reinforcing. You cannot have one without the other.
 Democracy is the soil in which human rights grow.
 Human rights are the pillars that hold up a true democracy.
How Democracy Protects and Promotes Human Rights:
1. Freedom of Speech and Press (A Human Right): This right allows citizens and
journalists to openly criticize the government, expose corruption, and demand
accountability. This is a core part of democracy.
2. Right to Vote and Participate (A Human Right): Democracy is the only system built
on this right. It gives people the power to choose their leaders and remove bad ones.
7
3. Rule of Law (A Human Right): In a democracy, everyone is equal before the law—
including the president. The law (constitution) protects citizens' rights from being
violated by the government.
4. Freedom of Assembly (A Human Right): This right allows people to form political
parties, trade unions, and civil society groups to protest or demand change. This is
essential for a functioning democracy.
5. Right to a Fair Trial (A Human Right): An independent judiciary (courts) in a
democracy ensures that the government cannot imprison its critics without a fair and
public trial.
Democracy vs. Dictatorship:
 In a Democracy: The government's main job is to protect the rights of the people. Power
flows from the people to the government.
 In a Dictatorship (or Military Rule): The ruler has absolute power. The first things to
be suspended are human rights—freedom of speech, freedom of assembly, and free
press—so the dictator cannot be challenged.

5. How is the treatment of human rights different in a democracy compared to a dictatorship?

WEEK 3: NIGERIAN CONSTITUTION (1999)
Content: (I) Basic provisions (II) Fundamental objectives.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "Constitution" (as the supreme law).
2. Identify the 1999 Constitution (as amended) as the current law of Nigeria.
3. List the three (3) basic provisions (e.g., citizenship, rights, arms of government).
4. Define "Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy."
5. List three (3) examples of these objectives (e.g., political, economic, social).
Lesson Content:
10
1. What is the 1999 Constitution?
 It is the supreme (highest) law of the Federal Republic of Nigeria.
 All other laws, actions of the government (President, Governors), and actions of citizens
are subject to it. If any law or action goes against the constitution, it is considered "null
and void" (illegal).
 It is the document that created the Fourth Republic (our current democratic system).
2. Basic Provisions of the 1999 Constitution The constitution is a long document, but its basic
provisions (contents) include:
 Chapter 1: General Provisions: Declares that Nigeria is one indivisible and indissoluble
sovereign state; confirms the supremacy of the constitution.
 Chapter 2: Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy: (See
below).
 Chapter 3: Citizenship: Defines who a Nigerian citizen is and how to get citizenship (by
birth, registration, or naturalization).
 Chapter 4: Fundamental Human Rights: Lists the guaranteed rights of every citizen
(e.g., Right to Life, Right to Dignity, Right to Fair Hearing, Right to Freedom of
Expression, Right to Freedom of Religion).
 Chapters 5, 6, 7: The Arms of Government:
o Chapter 5: The Legislature (powers and structure of the National Assembly).
o Chapter 6: The Executive (powers and structure of the Presidency and
Governors).
o Chapter 7: The Judicature (powers and structure of the Courts).
 Other Provisions: Include rules for states, local governments, elections (INEC), and the
military.
3. Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy (Chapter 2)
 Definition: This chapter is the "soul" or "mission statement" of the constitution. It
outlines the goals and objectives that the government must work towards.
 It directs the "policy" (plans and actions) of the state.
11
 Note: Unlike the human rights in Chapter 4, these "objectives" are generally nonjusticiable
(you cannot sue the government for failing to provide them, but they are the
standards for judging government performance).
 Examples of Objectives:
1. Political Objectives: The government's motto shall be "Security and welfare of
the people." It must promote national integration and loyalty, and discourage
discrimination.
2. Economic Objectives: The government must control the national economy to
secure the maximum welfare, freedom, and happiness of every citizen. It must
ensure that resources are not concentrated in the hands of a few.
3. Social Objectives: The state shall direct its policy towards ensuring that...
 All citizens have the opportunity for education.
 There are adequate medical and health facilities for all.
 Child abuse and corruption are abolished.
4. Environmental Objectives: The state shall protect and improve the environment
(air, water, land).
5. Foreign Policy Objectives: The state shall promote African unity and
international cooperation.

2. Why is the constitution called the "supreme law"?
3. List three (3) basic provisions of the constitution (e.g., citizenship, human rights).
4. What is the purpose of the "Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State
Policy"?
5. List two (2) social or economic objectives from Chapter 2.
Assignment:

2. List three (3) rights guaranteed to you under Chapter 4.

WEEK 4: SEPARATION OF POWERS
Content: (I) Checks and balances (II) Importance.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "Separation of Powers."
2. Identify the "three arms" of government (Legislature, Executive, Judiciary).
3. Define "Checks and Balances."
4. Give two (2) examples of how one arm "checks" another.
5. Explain the main importance of separation of powers (to prevent tyranny).
Lesson Content:
14
1. Meaning of Separation of Powers
 Definition: Separation of Powers is a model of democratic government where the powers
of the state are divided among three independent and separate branches (or arms): The
Legislature, The Executive, and The Judiciary.
 The Principle: This model, promoted by the French philosopher Baron de Montesquieu,
states that:
1. The same person should not be a member of more than one arm of government.
2. One arm of government should not interfere with the work of another arm.
3. One arm of government should not perform the function of another arm.
 The Three Arms (Recap):
o Legislature (National Assembly): Makes the law.
o Executive (The President): Enforces the law.
o Judiciary (The Courts): Interprets the law.
2. Checks and Balances
 In practice, the separation of powers is not absolute. The three arms must work together.
 Definition: "Checks and Balances" is a system that overlaps the separation of powers,
giving each arm some power to monitor and limit (or "check") the powers of the other
two arms.
 Purpose: This ensures that no single arm becomes too powerful and creates a balance
(equilibrium).
 Examples of Checks and Balances in Nigeria (1999 Constitution):
1. Legislature checks the Executive:
 The Senate must confirm the President's appointments (e.g., Ministers,
Judges).
 The National Assembly must approve the national budget (power of the
purse).
 The National Assembly can impeach the President for misconduct.
2. Executive checks the Legislature:
15
 The President must assent (sign) a bill passed by the legislature before it
can become law.
 The President can veto (refuse to sign) a bill. (The legislature can override
this veto with a two-thirds vote).
3. Judiciary checks BOTH the Executive and Legislature:
 This is the most important check, called Judicial Review.
 The Supreme Court can declare a law passed by the legislature
"unconstitutional" (illegal).
 The Courts can declare an action by the President "unconstitutional."
4. Executive checks the Judiciary:
 The President appoints the judges (though they must be confirmed by the
Senate).
3. Importance of Separation of Powers and Checks & Balances
1. To Prevent Tyranny (Dictatorship): This is the main reason. It prevents the
concentration of power in one person or group. If the person who makes the law
(Legislature) is also the one who enforces it (Executive) and judges it (Judiciary), they
have total power and can become a dictator.
2. To Promote Efficiency: It allows each arm to specialize in its own function.
3. To Ensure Accountability: The arms are accountable to each other (e.g., the President is
accountable to the Legislature).
4. To Protect Human Rights: By guaranteeing the independence of the Judiciary, citizens
have a place to go (the courts) if the government violates their rights.
Evaluation:
1. What is "Separation of Powers"?
2. Who was the philosopher who promoted this idea?
3. What is "Checks and Balances"?
4. Give one (1) example of how the Legislature "checks" the Executive.
5. What is the most important reason for separating the powers of government?
16

3. Identify the major political parties in Nigeria's Fourth Republic (e.g., PDP, APC, LP,
NNPP).
4. Describe Nigeria's "multi-party system."
5. Differentiate between a one-party, two-party, and multi-party system.
18
Lesson Content:
1. Meaning of a Political Party
 Definition: A Political Party is an organized group of people who share similar
political beliefs, ideologies, and goals, and who work together to contest elections in
order to win political power and control the government.
 They are the main link between the people and the government.
 In Nigeria, political parties must be registered with the Independent National Electoral
Commission (INEC).
2. Functions of Political Parties Political parties are essential for a democracy. Their functions
include:
1. Political Recruitment: They select and recruit candidates (e.g., for President, Governor,
Senator) to run for office.
2. Contesting Elections: They organize and fund election campaigns to get their candidates
elected.
3. Political Education and Mobilization: They educate the public about important issues
(from their perspective). They hold rallies and use media to mobilize people to vote.
4. Formulation of Government: The party that wins the majority of seats forms the
government. The party leader (e.g., the President) appoints members (Ministers) to run
the Executive arm.
5. Formulation of Public Policy: Each party has a manifesto (a document of its promises
and goals). If they win, they try to turn this manifesto into government policy (new laws,
new projects).
6. Serving as an "Opposition": The parties that lose the election form the "opposition."
Their job is to check the power of the ruling party, criticize its policies, and present an
alternative, so the government remains accountable.
7. National Integration: Parties bring together people from different ethnic groups,
religions, and regions under one political "umbrella."
19
3. Party Systems A party system describes the number of political parties that have a realistic
chance of winning power.
 One-Party System:
o Only one political party is legally allowed to exist. All opposition is banned.
o This is undemocratic and found in dictatorships.
o Examples: China, North Korea.
 Two-Party System:
o Although many parties may exist, political power is dominated by two major
parties.
o Examples: USA (Democrats and Republicans), UK (Conservative and Labour).
 Multi-Party System:
o Three or more parties have a genuine chance of winning elections or forming a
coalition government (where two parties agree to rule together).
o This system gives voters more choices.
o Nigeria operates a multi-party system. In the 1999-2015 era, it was dominated
by the PDP. Since 2015, it has been dominated by the APC, but the 2023 election
showed the strength of other parties like the Labour Party (LP) and the NNPP,
confirming it is a multi-party state.

WEEK 8: ELECTION PROCESSES
Content: (I) Voter registration (II) Voting methods (III) INEC.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "Election" and "Suffrage."
2. Explain the importance of "Voter Registration."
3. Describe the basic voting method used in Nigeria (Secret Ballot).
4. Identify INEC as the body responsible for elections in Nigeria.
5. List three (3) main functions of INEC.
Lesson Content:
22
1. Definitions
 Election: A formal process in a democracy where citizens (the electorate) choose their
representatives (leaders) by voting.
 Suffrage (or Franchise): The right to vote in political elections. In Nigeria, "universal
adult suffrage" means every citizen aged 18 years and above has the right to vote.
 Electorate: All the citizens who are qualified to vote.
2. The Election Process
 A. Voter Registration:
o Meaning: This is the first and most crucial step. It is the process where eligible
citizens (18+) get their names recorded by the electoral body (INEC).
o Importance:
1. Once registered, a citizen is issued a Permanent Voter's Card (PVC),
which is the "passport" to voting.
2. It helps INEC create a clean Voter's Register (the official list of all
voters).
3. It helps prevent fraud (e.g., people voting twice or underage voting).
4. It helps in planning (knowing how many ballot papers and polling units
are needed).
 B. Voting Methods:
o Open Ballot: An old method where voters vote publicly (e.g., by raising hands or
queuing behind a candidate's photo). This is bad because it leads to intimidation
and fear.
o Secret Ballot (Used in Nigeria): This is the standard democratic method.
1. A voter's identity is confirmed (accreditation) using the PVC and (now)
the BVAS (Bimodal Voter Accreditation System).
2. The voter is given a ballot paper (a paper showing the party logos).
3. The voter goes to a private voting booth (cubicle).
4. They secretly mark their choice (usually with a thumbprint).
5. They fold the ballot paper and drop it in a public ballot box.
23
o Importance of Secret Ballot: It protects the voter's choice, prevents intimidation,
and ensures a free vote.
 C. Counting and Declaration: After voting closes, the votes are counted publicly at the
polling unit, recorded, and sent to collation centers, and eventually, INEC declares the
winner.
3. INEC (Independent National Electoral Commission)
 Definition: INEC is the independent umpire or referee responsible for conducting all
major elections (Presidential, Governorship, National/State Assembly) in Nigeria.
 "Independent" means it is not supposed to be controlled by the government or any
political party.
 Functions of INEC:
1. Conducts and supervises all elections.
2. Registers eligible voters and maintains the Voter's Register.
3. Registers political parties.
4. Educates voters on the democratic process.
5. Divides the country into constituencies (voting areas).
6. Monitors political party campaigns and finances.
7. Counts the votes and declares the winners.
Evaluation:

3. Why is voter registration important?
4. What is the "secret ballot" system?
5. What does INEC stand for, and what is its main job?
Assignment:

WEEK 9: GOOD GOVERNANCE
Content: (I) Meaning (II) Principles (III) Citizens’ role in promoting it.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "Governance" and "Good Governance."
2. List at least five (5) key principles (or characteristics) of Good Governance.
3. Explain the relationship between good governance and national development.
4. Describe three (3) roles citizens can play in promoting good governance.
Lesson Content:
1. Meaning of Governance and Good Governance
26
 Governance: The process of making and enforcing decisions within a country or
organization. It is the act of ruling.
 Good Governance: A style of governance that is efficient, effective, and just (fair). It is
the responsible use of power to manage a country's resources for the benefit of all
citizens.
 Good governance is not just about the government; it includes the participation of
citizens and civil society.
2. Principles (Characteristics) of Good Governance (Based on United Nations standards)
1. Participation: Citizens have a voice in decision-making, either directly or through their
elected representatives. (e.g., voting, peaceful protests, community meetings).
2. Rule of Law: The laws are fair, applied equally to everyone (including the government),
and enforced by an independent judiciary. (No one is above the law).
3. Transparency: The government is open about what it is doing and why. Decisions are
made publicly, not in secret. (e.g., publishing the national budget, open contract bidding).
4. Accountability: The government and public officials are answerable for their actions.
They must explain their decisions and take responsibility for failures. (e.g., facing checks
by the legislature, being voted out).
5. Responsiveness: The government listens to its citizens and responds to their needs within
a reasonable timeframe.
6. Effectiveness and Efficiency: The government gets the job done (e.g., builds the road,
provides power) without wasting time or resources (money, people).
7. Equity and Inclusiveness: All citizens, especially the most vulnerable (poor, minorities,
women), have opportunities to improve their well-being. No one is left behind.
8. Consensus-Oriented: Good governance requires mediating different interests in society
to reach a broad agreement on what is best for the whole community.
3. Citizens' Role in Promoting Good Governance Good governance is a two-way street.
Citizens cannot be passive; they must be active.
27
1. Voting and Political Participation: The most important role. Citizens must register to
vote, and then vote wisely based on merit, not on ethnic or religious sentiment.
2. Obeying the Law: Citizens must follow the same "rule of law" they expect from leaders.
(e.g., pay taxes, don't litter, don't bribe).
3. Holding Leaders Accountable:
o Asking questions at town hall meetings.
o Using freedom of speech (media, social media) to expose corruption.
o Reporting crimes and corruption to agencies (like EFCC, ICPC).
4. Civic Engagement: Joining or supporting civil society organizations (CSOs), NGOs, or
community groups that monitor government projects and advocate for public good.
Evaluation:
1. What is the difference between "governance" and "good governance"?
2. List three (3) principles of good governance.
3. What does "Transparency" mean in government?
4. What does "Accountability" mean?
5. List two (2) roles a citizen must play to promote good governance.
Assignment:

2. List and explain four (4) principles of good governance in your own words.
3. Why is "Rule of Law" important for good governance?
4. Explain three (3) ways you as a JSS3 student can promote good governance in your

5. What is the link between good governance and national development?

WEEK 10: YOUTH AND NATION-BUILDING
Content: (I) Roles of youth (II) Challenges (III) Opportunities.
Performance Objectives: By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define "Youth" (according to the Nigerian National Youth Policy).
2. Define "Nation-Building."
3. List four (4) positive roles of youth in nation-building.
4. List three (3) major challenges facing Nigerian youth.
5. Identify opportunities for youth to contribute (e.g., skills, technology, activism).
Lesson Content:
29
1. Definitions
 Youth: The Nigerian National Youth Policy (2019) defines "youth" as all persons aged
15–29 years. (JSS3 students are on the verge of entering this category). The African
Youth Charter defines it as 15-35. The youth are the most energetic, innovative, and
dynamic segment of the population.
 Nation-Building: The process of constructing or structuring a national identity using the
power of the state. It involves uniting people from different backgrounds (ethnic,
religious) into a single, cohesive, and functional nation. It means building the economy,
the political system, and social trust (unity).
2. Positive Roles of Youth in Nation-Building The youth are not just "leaders of tomorrow";
they are partners of today.
1. Economic Engine: Youths are the main workforce. They are the innovators,
entrepreneurs, and tech-savvy workers who create new businesses (like in FinTech,
music, and fashion) and drive economic growth.
2. Agents of Change and Social Activism: Young people have the energy and idealism to
challenge injustice and demand accountability. (e.g., the "Not Too Young To Run"
movement, which successfully lowered the age for running for office).
3. Promoting National Unity: When youths from different backgrounds interact (e.g., in
NYSC, universities, sports), they build bridges and break down ethnic stereotypes,
fostering a single national identity.
4. Defending the Nation: The majority of members of the Armed Forces, Police, and other
security agencies are young people.
5. Political Participation: By voting, running for office, and engaging in political discourse,
youths shape the future of the nation's leadership.
3. Challenges Facing Nigerian Youth Despite their potential, youths in Nigeria face significant
challenges that hinder their role in nation-building:
1. Unemployment and Underemployment: The biggest challenge. Millions of graduates
cannot find jobs that match their skills, leading to frustration and poverty.
30
2. Poor Quality of Education: The educational system (from basic to tertiary) often lacks
funding, infrastructure, and modern relevance, failing to equip youths with the skills
needed for the 21st-century job market.
3. Political Exclusion: Youths are often "used" by politicians for thuggery during elections
but are excluded from actual decision-making (policy-making).
4. Negative Vices: Due to frustration and poverty, many youths get involved in social
problems like cultism, drug abuse, cybercrime ("yahoo-yahoo"), and political thuggery.
5. Insecurity: Youths are both the main perpetrators and primary victims of insecurity (e.g.,
insurgency, banditry).
4. Opportunities for Youth Contribution
 Skill Acquisition: Focus on acquiring practical, high-demand skills (e.g., coding, digital
marketing, fashion design, solar installation) in addition to formal education.
 Technology (ICT): The digital economy is the biggest opportunity. Youths can create
apps, start online businesses, and solve local problems with technology.
 Entrepreneurship: Instead of waiting for a job, youths can create jobs by starting small
businesses (becoming entrepreneurs).
 Positive Activism: Using social media and community groups to advocate for good
governance, environmental protection, and social justice.

1. What are Humanitarian Issues?
 A Humanitarian Issue is a large-scale event or situation that causes widespread human
suffering and threatens the health, safety, and well-being of a community or group of
people.
 These situations often require an urgent response (humanitarian aid) in the form of food,
water, shelter, and medical care.
 Two major groups at the center of humanitarian issues are Refugees and Internally
Displaced Persons (IDPs).
2. Refugees
 Definition: A Refugee is a person who has been forced to flee their country to escape
war, violence, conflict, or persecution (being mistreated because of their race, religion,
nationality, or political opinion).
 Key Feature: A refugee crosses an international border (they leave their home country).
 International Law: Under the 1951 Refugee Convention, refugees have specific rights.
The most important is the right to asylum (protection) in the new country and the right
not to be sent back to the danger they fled from (non-refoulement).
 Global Example: A person fleeing the war in Syria and crossing into Turkey or
Germany.
 Agency: The United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) is the
global agency responsible for protecting refugees.
3. Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs)
 Definition: An Internally Displaced Person (IDP) is a person who has been forced to
flee their home for the same reasons as a refugee (war, violence, insecurity, or natural
disaster) but who has not crossed an international border.
 Key Feature: An IDP remains inside their own country.
 Example: A farmer in Borno State who flees from Boko Haram and moves to an IDP
camp in Maiduguri or Abuja. They are still in Nigeria, but they have lost their home.
41
 Legal Status: Because they are still in their own country, IDPs are not protected by
international refugee law. They must rely on their own government for protection and aid
(which is often the source of the problem).
4. Differentiating Between Refugees and IDPs This is a very important distinction. | Feature |
Refugee | Internally Displaced Person (IDP) | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | Location | Outside their home
country. | Inside their home country. | | Border Crossed| Crosses an international border. | Does
not cross an international border. | | Legal Protection | Protected by international law (UNHCR).
| Protected by their own national government. | | Example | A Nigerian who flees to Cameroon. |
A Nigerian who flees from Katsina to Kano. |
5. The IDP Crisis in Nigeria
 Causes:
1. Conflict/Insecurity: This is the main cause. (e.g., Boko Haram insurgency in the
North-East, banditry in the North-West).
2. Communal Clashes: Farmer-herder conflicts over land.
3. Natural Disasters: Severe flooding, which displaces thousands of people
annually.
 Problems Faced by IDPs:
1. Lack of Shelter: Camps are overcrowded and often made of temporary materials.
2. Food and Water Scarcity: Malnutrition is very high, especially among children.
3. No Healthcare: Diseases like cholera and malaria spread quickly in camps.
4. Loss of Education: Millions of children in IDP camps are out of school.
5. Security Risks: Camps are often attacked, and women/children face abuse.

Lesson Content:

1. Definitions
 Conflict: A serious disagreement or argument between individuals, groups, or nations.
Conflict is a natural part of human life.
 Peace: Peace is not just the absence of war or violence. It is a positive state of harmony,
justice, security, and social order. It is a state where conflicts are resolved non-violently.
 Peace Education: The process of teaching and learning the knowledge, skills, attitudes,
and values needed to build and maintain peace. It teaches people how to resolve conflicts
peacefully.
2. Peacebuilding
 Definition: Peacebuilding refers to the long-term actions and programs designed to
address the root causes of a conflict and prevent it from happening again.
 It is not just stopping the fighting. It is about rebuilding a society.
 Examples of Peacebuilding Activities:
1. Reconstruction: Rebuilding roads, schools, and hospitals destroyed in a war.
2. Reconciliation: Bringing the two warring groups together to forgive each other
and build trust (e.g., through truth commissions, community dialogues).
3. Disarmament: Taking weapons away from former fighters.
4. Economic Empowerment: Creating jobs so that youths are not tempted to return
to violence.
5. Strengthening Justice: Building fair courts and police systems.
3. Methods of Peaceful Conflict Resolution
 A. Negotiation:
o Definition: A process of direct communication between two or more parties in a
conflict, who voluntarily try to reach a mutually acceptable agreement.
o Key Feature: It involves only the conflicting parties themselves. There is no
"referee."
o Process: Both sides must be willing to compromise (give up some of their
demands) to find a "win-win" solution.
45
o Example: You and your classmate both want to use the same textbook. You
negotiate an agreement: "You use it for 30 minutes, and I will use it for 30
minutes."
 B. Mediation:
o Definition: A process where a neutral third party (a person who is not involved
in the conflict) is invited to help the conflicting parties communicate and find a
solution.
o The Mediator (The Third Party):
 Does not take sides.
 Does not make the decision.
 Their job is to facilitate (make it easier) for the two sides to talk. They are
like a "referee" or "chairman."
o Example: You and your classmate are shouting at each other. A teacher (the
mediator) comes, calms you down, listens to both sides, and helps you agree on a
solution (like the 30-minute one). The teacher doesn't force the solution but helps
you find it.
4. Negotiation vs. Mediation | Feature | Negotiation | Mediation | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | Parties
Involved | Only the conflicting parties. | The conflicting parties PLUS a neutral third party. | |
Process | Direct communication. | Facilitated communication. | | Who finds the solution? | The
parties find it themselves. | The parties find it, but with the help of the mediator. |

1. Definitions

 Global Security: The collection of actions and measures taken by nations and
international organizations (like the UN) to prevent and resolve conflicts, reduce weapons,
and protect civilians.
 Peacekeeping: The most common method of promoting global security. It involves

and security has been its role as a leading peacekeeping nation.

 History: Nigeria's peacekeeping history began almost immediately after independence,
with the UN mission in Congo in 1960 (ONUC).

WEEK 1: CIVIC CHALLENGES IN AFRICA
Content: (I) Governance (II) Corruption (III) Conflict (IV) Underdevelopment.

70
3. Analyze how "Corruption" (systemic and petty) undermines development.
4. Describe "Conflict" (ethnic, political) as both a cause and effect of other challenges.
5. Define "Underdevelopment" and link it as the consequence of poor governance,

Lesson Content:
1. What are Civic Challenges in Africa?
 Definition: Civic challenges are major, persistent problems that affect the political,
economic, and social life of citizens across the African continent.
 These challenges are not just government problems; they are problems for all citizens
("civic"). They hinder the ability of citizens to enjoy their rights, perform their duties, and
achieve national development.
 The four most critical and interconnected challenges are: Poor Governance, Corruption,
Conflict, and Underdevelopment.
2. Challenge 1: Poor Governance
 Definition: "Governance" is the process of ruling. "Poor Governance" is the failure of
leaders and institutions to manage a country's affairs effectively, fairly, and transparently.
 Manifestations of Poor Governance in Africa:
1. "Sit-Tight" Leadership: Leaders who refuse to leave power, often by changing
the constitution, rigging elections, or using force. This leads to stagnation, lack of
new ideas, and public frustration.
2. Weak Institutions: The arms of government (police, judiciary, civil service) are
often weak, underfunded, and ineffective. They serve the leaders instead of the
public.
3. Lack of Rule of Law: The law is not applied equally. The rich and powerful are
often above the law, while the poor are punished.
4. Exclusion: Many governments fail to include all ethnic groups, religions, and
regions in the decision-making process, leading to feelings of marginalization.
71
3. Challenge 2: Corruption
 Definition: The abuse of entrusted power for private gain. In Africa, this is a "pandemic"
that destroys national progress.
 Forms of Corruption:
o Grand Corruption: High-level officials (e.g., ministers, governors) stealing or
embezzling millions or billions of dollars meant for public projects (e.g., hospitals,
roads, power plants).
o Petty Corruption: Everyday bribery demanded by low-level officials (e.g., a
police officer asking for a bribe, a clerk demanding "dash" to process a file, a
teacher demanding money for grades).
 Consequences:
o Diverts public funds: Money for schools ends up in private pockets.
o Hinders foreign investment: Businesses are unwilling to invest in a place where
they have to pay bribes.
o Reduces public trust: Citizens stop believing in the government.
4. Challenge 3: Conflict
 Definition: Widespread, often violent, disagreements between groups.
 Types of Conflict in Africa:
1. Political Conflicts: Disputes over election results or power-sharing, often leading
to civil war.
2. Ethnic and Communal Conflicts: Violence between different ethnic or religious
groups, often over land, resources, or political representation.
3. Resource-Based Conflicts: Wars fought over the control of valuable natural
resources (e.g., "blood diamonds," oil).
4. Terrorism and Insurgency: (e.g., Boko Haram, Al-Shabaab).
 Consequences:
o Massive loss of life and property.
o Creates millions of Refugees and IDPs (as seen in JSS2).
72
o Destroys the economy: No one can farm or do business during a war.
o Prevents development: Money that should be used for education is spent on
buying weapons.
5. Challenge 4: Underdevelopment
 Definition: A state of low economic and social progress. It is characterized by
widespread poverty, high unemployment, poor infrastructure, and low standards of living.
 The "Vicious Cycle": Underdevelopment is the result of the other three challenges, and
it also causes them.
o Poor Governance → leads to Corruption (stealing) → leads to Underdevelopment
(no hospitals, bad roads) → leads to Poverty and frustration → leads to Conflict
(war) → which in turn destroys infrastructure and makes Poor Governance worse.
Breaking the Cycle: The solution must involve all citizens demanding good governance,
accountability, and an end to corruption.

Lesson Content:

1. What is the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights?
 Background: While the global UDHR (from JSS2) set a standard, African nations felt
the need for a treaty that reflected Africa's unique history, values, and challenges.
 Definition: The African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights (also known as the
"Banjul Charter") is the main African treaty (a binding international law) that outlines
the fundamental rights and freedoms of all African people.
 Adoption: It was adopted in 1981 by the Organization of African Unity (OAU), which
is now the African Union (AU).
2. Unique Features of the African Charter The African Charter is special and different from
the UN's UDHR in two main ways:
 Feature 1: It includes "Peoples' Rights" (Collective Rights).
o While most human rights treaties focus only on the individual (your right to
speech, your right to life), the African Charter adds collective rights that belong
to a group or people.
o Examples of Peoples' Rights:
 Article 19: The right of all peoples to equality.
 Article 20: The right to self-determination (the right of a people to
choose their own political status) and to be free from colonization.
 Article 21: The right of all peoples to freely dispose of their wealth and
natural resources (e.g., oil, minerals).
 Article 22: The right to economic, social, and cultural development.
 Article 24: The right to a "general satisfactory environment."
 Feature 2: It includes the Duties of the Individual.
o The Charter states that the enjoyment of rights comes with responsibilities. This
reflects the African value of community.
o Examples of Individual Duties (Chapter 2):
 Article 27: The duty to respect and be tolerant of others; the duty to
maintain the family.
 Article 28: The duty to treat others without discrimination.
76
 Article 29: The duty to...
 Serve the national community (e.g., work, pay taxes).
 Not to compromise the security of the state.
 Preserve and strengthen national solidarity.
 Preserve and strengthen African cultural values.
3. Enforcement: The African Commission and Court
 The Charter created two bodies to protect these rights:
1. The African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights: (Located in Banjul,
The Gambia). Its job is to promote rights (education, research) and investigate
violations.
2. The African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights: (Located in Arusha,
Tanzania). This is a continental court where individuals and NGOs can (under
certain conditions) sue their own governments for violating the Charter.
4. Challenges Despite this strong treaty, human rights are still widely abused in Africa due to the
civic challenges we learned about in Week 1 (poor governance, corruption, and conflict). Many
governments ignore the rulings of the African Court.

Lesson Content:
1. What is Civic Participation?
 Definition: Civic Participation is the active involvement of citizens in the political, social,
and economic life of their community and country.
 It is the "action" part of being a citizen.
 Passive Citizen: A citizen who only complains, does not vote, and expects the
government to do everything.
 Active Citizen: A citizen who participates in solving problems, votes, obeys laws, and
holds leaders accountable.
 Conclusion: Nigeria's future development depends entirely on moving from a culture of
passive citizenship to one of active civic participation.
2. Role of Citizens in Elections Elections are the foundation of democracy. Participation is not
just a one-day event.
 Before the Election:
o Registering to get a PVC.
o Actively researching candidates (using critical thinking).
o Attending debates and town hall meetings.
o Refusing to sell your vote (integrity).
 During the Election:
o Going out to vote (decision-making).
o Obeying all electoral rules at the polling unit.
o Serving as a non-partisan party agent or election observer (teamwork) to monitor
the vote and prevent rigging.
 After the Election:
o Accepting the results peacefully.
o Using the courts (not violence) to challenge results if there is evidence of fraud.
3. Role of Citizens in Governance (Between Elections) This is the most important part.
Democracy does not end on election day.
92
 Holding Leaders Accountable:
o Attending community meetings to ask your Local Government Chairman about
projects.
o Using "Advocacy" and "Public Speaking" to demand action.
o Using social media responsibly to highlight problems (e.g., a bad road, a corrupt
official).
 Monitoring Government:
o Tracking government spending. Civil society groups like "Tracka" and "BudgIT"
monitor government budgets and visit project sites to see if the work was actually
done (preventing corruption).
 Rule of Law: Obeying laws, paying taxes, and reporting crimes.
4. Role of Citizens in Development National development is not just the government's job.
 Economic Participation (Entrepreneurship): Starting businesses, creating jobs for
others, and paying taxes (as learned in Week 4).
 Community Participation (Volunteering): Not waiting for the government. Citizens
can work together (teamwork) to clean their own environment, set up a neighborhood
watch, or contribute money to repair a local school.
 Protecting Public Property: Treating public facilities (hospitals, schools, roads) with
care, as if they were your own—because they are (paid for with public money).
5. The Future (The JSS3 Student's Role)
 The future of Nigeria depends on your generation.
 Your role now is to:
1. Get an Education: Learn the skills (math, science, civic competence) needed to
solve problems.
2. Practice Integrity: Be the honest citizen you want to see. Refuse to cheat in
exams.
3. Be an Active Citizen: Start now in your school. Join a club, run for prefect, ask
questions, and practice teamwork.
