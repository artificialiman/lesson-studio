1. SPEECH WORK
Topic: Revision of Speech Sounds (Vowels and Consonants)
A. Introduction
Speech work (Phonology and Phonetics) is the study of how sounds are produced and used in a
language. A clear understanding of speech sounds is the foundation for correct pronunciation and
effective communication. This week, we will revise the key sounds from JSS2.
B. Vowel Sounds (Revision)
2
Vowel sounds are produced with no obstruction in the vocal tract. They are the nucleus of every
syllable. English has 20 vowel sounds, divided into monophthongs (pure vowels) and diphthongs
(glide vowels).
1. Monophthongs (Pure Vowels - 12) These sounds have a single, consistent quality.
 Front Vowels:
o /i:/ as in seat, feet, me
o /ɪ/ as in sit, hit, bin
o /e/ as in set, bed, men
o /æ/ as in sat, cat, man
 Central Vowels:
o /ɜ:/ as in bird, shirt, fur
o /ə/ (schwa) as in about, teacher, doctor (the most common, weakest sound)
o /ʌ/ as in cut, bus, sun
 Back Vowels:
o /u:/ as in shoe, blue, food
o /ʊ/ as in book, put, could
o /ɔ:/ as in caught, door, law
o /ɒ/ as in cot, hot, pot
o /ɑ:/ as in father, car, hard
2. Diphthongs (Glide Vowels - 8) These sounds are a combination of two pure vowels,
involving a glide from one to the other within the same syllable.
 /eɪ/ as in say, pain, fate
 /aɪ/ as in my, buy, high
 /ɔɪ/ as in boy, coin, toy
 /əʊ/ (UK) or /oʊ/ (US) as in go, no, road
 /aʊ/ as in now, house, cow
 /ɪə/ as in here, near, fear
 /eə/ as in hair, air, there
3
 /ʊə/ as in poor, tour, sure
C. Consonant Sounds (Revision)
Consonant sounds are produced with some obstruction or blockage of airflow in the vocal tract.
We will focus on the sounds that often cause confusion.
 The 'th' sounds (Dental Fricatives):
o /θ/ (Voiceless): Air passes between the tongue and top teeth. No vocal cord
vibration.
 Examples: thin, think, math, path, thunder
o /ð/ (Voiced): Same mouth position, but the vocal cords vibrate.
 Examples: this, that, mother, father, breathe
 The 'sh' and 'j' sounds (Palato-Alveolar):
o /ʃ/ (Voiceless): The "sh" sound.
 Examples: shoe, ship, wash, nation, machine
o /ʒ/ (Voiced): The "zh" sound, often found in the middle of words.
 Examples: pleasure, treasure, measure, television, beige
o /tʃ/ (Voiceless): The "ch" sound.
 Examples: church, chair, watch, catch
o /dʒ/ (Voiced): The "j" or "soft g" sound.
 Examples: judge, jam, giant, age, bridge

2. GRAMMAR
Topic: Revision of the Eight Parts of Speech
A. Introduction
The parts of speech are categories that words are grouped into based on their function in a
sentence. Understanding them is the key to building and analyzing sentences.
1. Noun
 Definition: A word that names a person (Sade), animal (goat), place (Lagos), thing
(book), or idea (love).
 Types:
o Proper: Specific names (capitalized). E.g., JSS3, Mr. Ade, Nigeria.
o Common: General names. E.g., school, teacher, country.
o Abstract: Ideas, feelings, qualities (cannot be touched). E.g., honesty, fear,
courage.
o Concrete: Things that can be touched. E.g., desk, water, smoke.
o Collective: A name for a group. E.g., flock, crowd, committee, government.
o Countable: Can be counted. E.g., three chairs, many boys.
o Uncountable: Cannot be counted (no plural). E.g., rice, information, advice.
 Functions: Subject, Object, Complement.
2. Pronoun
 Definition: A word used in place of a noun to avoid repetition.
 Types:
o Personal: I, you, he, she, it, we, they, me, him, her, us, them.
o Possessive: My, your, his, her, its, our, their, mine, yours, hers.
o Reflexive: Myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves,
themselves.
o Demonstrative: This, that, these, those.
6
o Interrogative: Who, what, which, where, when, whose.
o Relative: Who, which, that (introduces a clause).
3. Verb
 Definition: A word that shows an action (run, jump) or a state of being (is, are, feel). It
is the most important word in a sentence.
 Types:
o Transitive: Requires a direct object. E.g., She wrote <u>a letter</u>.
o Intransitive: Does not require an object. E.g., He slept.
o Linking: Connects the. subject to a noun or adjective. E.g., He is <u>a
doctor</u>. She seems <u>happy</u>.
o Auxiliary (Helping): Helps the main verb.
 Primary: be, have, do. E.g., I am running. He has eaten.
 Modal: can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must. E.g., You
must study.
4. Adjective
 Definition: A word that modifies (describes or limits) a noun or pronoun. It answers:
What kind? How many? Which one?
 Examples: a tall building, three goats, the blue sky, a beautiful girl.
 Degrees: Positive (tall), Comparative (taller), Superlative (tallest).
5. Adverb
 Definition: A word that modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. It answers:
How? When? Where? How often? To what extent?
 Types & Examples:
o Manner: He ran quickly.
o Time: We will leave tomorrow.
o Place: She looked around.
o Frequency: I never see him.
7
o Degree: It is very hot.
6. Preposition
 Definition: A word that shows the relationship between a noun/pronoun (its object) and
another word in the sentence.
 Examples: The book is on the table. He went to the store. She is in the room. He sat
under the tree.
7. Conjunction
 Definition: A word that joins words, phrases, or clauses.
 Types:
o Coordinating (FANBOYS): For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So. (Joins equal parts).
o Subordinating: because, while, although, if, when, since. (Joins a dependent
clause to an independent one).
o Correlative: either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also.
8. Interjection
 Definition: An exclamatory word that shows strong emotion and is grammatically
separate from the sentence.
 Examples: Ouch!, Wow!, Alas!, Oh no!, Hurray!

3. READING & COMPREHENSION
Topic: Scanning for Main Points (and its difference from Skimming)
9
A. Introduction
Good readers use different strategies for different tasks. You do not read a novel the same way
you read a phone directory. Scanning and Skimming are two essential "speed reading"
strategies.
 Clarification: The topic mentions "Scanning for main points." This is a common
confusion.
o The technique for finding main points or the general idea (gist) is SKIMMING.
o The technique for finding specific details (like a name, date, or number) is
SCANNING.
o This lesson will focus on SCANNING, as it is a distinct skill.
B. What is Scanning?
Scanning is the process of moving your eyes quickly over a text to find a specific piece of
information. You are not reading every word.
Analogy: Imagine you are looking for your friend's name in a list of 100 students. You don't
read every name; you let your eyes "scan" for the specific name.
C. How to Scan Effectively
1. Know Your Target: Be very clear on what you are looking for before you start. Is it a
name? A date? A number? A keyword?
2. Use Keywords: Focus only on the keywords related to your target.
3. Move Your Eyes Quickly: Do not read in a straight line. Move your eyes in a zig-zag or
"S" pattern down the page.
4. Ignore Everything Else: Your goal is to find one thing. You must ignore all other
information, even if it seems interesting.
5. Stop and Read: Once your eye catches the keyword or information you want, stop
scanning and read the sentence around it to confirm it's what you need.

4. COMPOSITION
Topic: Informal Letter – Letter to My Best Friend on My Plan for the Academic Session
A. Introduction
An informal letter, or personal letter, is a letter you write to someone you know well, such as a
friend, sibling, or parent. The tone is casual and conversational, like you are talking to them.
B. Features and Format of an Informal Letter
An informal letter has a specific, though relaxed, format.
1. Writer's Address:
o Written at the top-right corner.
o Do not write your name here.
o Example:
Government Secondary School, P.M.B. 102, Abuja, F.C.T.
2. Date:
o Written just below your address.
o Example: 6th September, 2025.
3. Salutation (Greeting):
o Written on the left side, after skipping a line.
o It is casual and ends with a comma.
o Example: Dear Kunle, or Dearest Titi,
4. Body of the Letter:
o This is the main message, organized into paragraphs.
o Paragraph 1 (Introduction): Start with greetings, ask about your friend's wellbeing,
and state the purpose of your letter.
o Paragraph 2-4 (Main Points): Discuss the main topic. In this case, your plans
for the new JSS3 session.
13
o Paragraph 5 (Conclusion): Conclude your letter, send greetings to their family,
and say you hope to hear from them.
5. Complimentary Close:
o Written at the bottom, on the right side. It is followed by a comma.
o Example: Yours sincerely, or Your friend, or Best wishes,
6. First Name:
o Written just below the complimentary close.
o Example: David (or your first name only).

5. LITERATURE IN ENGLISH
Topic: Introduction to Fiction & Non-Fiction
A. Introduction
Literature is the broad term for written works. All literature can be divided into two main
categories: Fiction and Non-Fiction.
B. Fiction
 Definition: Fiction is any creative work, chiefly in a narrative form, that describes events
and people that are not real. It is based on the imagination of the author.
 Purpose: To entertain, to explore ideas, to comment on the human condition through
imaginary stories.
 Keywords: Imaginary, "made-up," created.
 Examples:
o Novels: Things Fall Apart by Chinua Achebe
o Short Stories: "The Lottery" by Shirley Jackson
o Plays (Drama): The Lion and the Jewel by Wole Soyinka
o Myths, Legends, Fables, Fairy Tales.
C. Non-Fiction
 Definition: Non-fiction is any text or creative work that is based on facts, real events,
and real people. The author claims to be writing the truth.
 Purpose: To inform, to educate, to persuade, to explain, to describe.
 Keywords: Factual, "real," informational, verifiable.
D. Key Differences (Fiction vs. Non-Fiction)
Feature FICTION NON-FICTION
Basis Imagination Fact and Reality
Author's Intent To entertain, explore ideas To inform, educate, persuade
16
Characters Imaginary (even if based on real people) Real People
Events Made-up Verifiable, historical
Truth Explores emotional truth ("what if") Claims to be factually true
E. Examples and Types of Non-Fiction
This is a very broad category. Here are some of the most common types:
1. Biography:
o A factual story of a real person's life written by someone else.
o Example: A book about the life of Nelson Mandela, written by a historian.
2. Autobiography:
o A factual story of a real person's life written by that person themselves. (Auto =
self).
o Example: Long Walk to Freedom by Nelson Mandela; Ake: The Years of
Childhood by Wole Soyinka.
3. Memoir:
o Similar to an autobiography, but it focuses on one specific period, event, or theme
in the author's life, not their entire life story.
o Example: Becoming by Michelle Obama (focuses heavily on her time in the
White House).
4. Textbooks:
o Your New General Mathematics or Integrated Science books are non-fiction.
Their purpose is to educate.
5. Essays:
o A short written work on a specific subject (e.g., an argumentative essay on "The
Importance of Education").
6. Journalism / News Reports:
o Articles in newspapers or on websites that report on real, current events.
7. Diaries and Journals:
o A personal, day-to-day account of a person's life.
o Example: The Diary of a Young Girl by Anne Frank.
17
8. Speeches:
o Written transcripts of famous public addresses (e.g., Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I
Have a Dream" speech).

1. SPEECH WORK
Topic: The Schwa Sound /ə/
A. Introduction
The schwa /ə/ is the most common vowel sound in the English language. It is a very short,
"lazy," unstressed vowel sound. It is the sound you hear in:
 about
 computer
 doctor
 pencil
The schwa sound is never in a stressed syllable. This is why it's often called a "weak" vowel. Its
symbol /ə/ looks like an upside-down 'e'.
19
B. Identifying the Schwa
The most confusing part of the schwa is that it can be represented by any vowel letter (a, e, i, o,
u). You must listen for the sound, not look at the spelling.
 'a' can be /ə/:
o about, ago, arise, banana, salad
 'e' can be /ə/:
o telephone, problem, synthesis, mother
 'i' can be /ə/:
o pencil, easily, cousin
 'o' can be /ə/:
o computer, polite, lemon, condition
 'u' can be /ə/:
o support, success, album, focus

2. GRAMMAR
Topic: Expressing/Describing Emotions (Verb/Adjective + Preposition)
A. Introduction
In English, we often use specific prepositions to connect verbs or adjectives to the object of our
feelings. Using the correct preposition is essential for sounding natural. These combinations are
called collocations.
B. Common Emotion Collocations
Here is a list of common adjectives and verbs used to express emotions, followed by the
prepositions they require.
 about (used for a situation, topic, or event)
o worried about: I am worried about the exam.
o sad about: He was sad about the bad news.
o happy about: Are you happy about your new class?
o excited about: The children are excited about the holiday.
o angry about: She is angry about the broken window.
o nervous about: I am nervous about my speech.
 of (used for the source of the fear or pride)
o afraid of: My little brother is afraid of the dark.
o scared of: Are you scared of snakes?
o proud of: The principal is proud of the JSS3 students.
o tired of: I am tired of listening to his excuses.
o ashamed of: He was ashamed of his bad behaviour.
 with / at (often used when the object is a person)
21
o pleased with: The teacher is pleased with my work.
o disappointed with: I am disappointed with your performance.
o angry with / at: My father was angry with me for coming home late.
o satisfied with: Are you satisfied with your results?
 by / at (used for the thing that causes a sudden feeling)
o surprised by / at: We were surprised by his sudden arrival.
o shocked by / at: The world was shocked by the news.
o amazed by / at: She was amazed at the beautiful painting.

Topic: Skimming for Specific Information
A. Clarification: Skimming vs. Scanning
In Week 1, we learned Scanning, which is searching for specific details (like a name or date).
The scheme for this week says "Skimming for specific information." This is slightly confusing.
The true definition of Skimming is reading quickly to get the general idea or gist of a passage.
We will focus on the true definition of SKIMMING.
B. What is Skimming?
Skimming is a high-speed reading technique used to get a general overview of a text. You are
not reading every word, but looking for the main ideas.
Analogy: Skimming is like watching a 2-minute movie trailer. You don't see the whole movie,
but you get a very good idea of what the story is about.
C. How to Skim Effectively
1. Read the Title and Subheadings: These tell you the main topic.
2. Read the Introduction: The first paragraph usually introduces the main idea.
3. Read the First Sentence of Other Paragraphs: This is the topic sentence, and it often
summarizes the paragraph's main point.
4. Read the Conclusion: The last paragraph often summarizes the entire passage.
5. Look for Key Words: Let your eyes drift over the text, looking for bold words, names,
dates, and keywords.

The Importance of Bees

Topic: Poetry Analysis
A. Introduction
26
Poetry is a form of literature that uses the sound, rhythm, and meaning of language to create
vivid images and express deep emotions. Unlike prose, poetry is often written in a compact form,
using lines and stanzas.
B. Basic Elements of a Poem
To analyze a poem, we look at its building blocks:
1. Stanza: A stanza is a group of lines that form a unit in a poem. It is like a "paragraph" in
prose.
o Couplet: A stanza with 2 lines.
o Tercet: A stanza with 3 lines.
o Quatrain: A stanza with 4 lines (the most common).
2. Rhyme: The repetition of similar sounds at the end of lines.
o Rhyme Scheme: The pattern of rhyme in a stanza. We use letters (A, B, C, D) to
mark the rhyme scheme.
C. Poetic Devices (Figures of Speech)
Poets use figures of speech to make their writing more powerful and descriptive.
1. Simile: A comparison between two different things using the words "like" or "as".
o Example: He is as tall as a giant. The moon shines like a diamond.
2. Metaphor: A direct comparison that states one thing is another thing (without using
"like" or "as").
o Example: He is a giant. The moon is a diamond.
3. Personification: Giving human qualities, feelings, or actions to an inanimate (non-living)
object.
o Example: The sun smiled at us. The angry wind shouted through the trees.

 Intonation: The rise and fall of the voice when speaking (the "melody").

2. GRAMMAR
Topic: Adverbs of Frequency
A. Introduction
An adverb modifies a verb, adjective, or another adverb. An Adverb of Frequency is a specific
type of adverb that answers the question: "How often?"
B. The Scale of Frequency
We can place adverbs of frequency on a scale from 100% (all the time) to 0% (never).
 100%: Always (e.g., I always brush my teeth at night.)
 90%: Usually / Generally (e.g., I usually walk to school.)
 70%: Often / Frequently (e.g., We often play football.)
 50%: Sometimes (e.g., I sometimes eat rice for breakfast.)
 30%: Occasionally (e.g., We occasionally visit our grandmother.)
31
 10%: Seldom / Rarely (e.g., He rarely speaks in class.)
 0%: Never (e.g., I have never been to London.)
C. Placement in a Sentence (The Rules)
The position of the adverb is very important.
Rule 1: Adverbs of frequency go BEFORE the main verb.
 Subject + Adverb + Main Verb
 Example:
o She never <u>eats</u> meat. (NOT: She eats never meat.)
o They often <u>watch</u> television.
o I sometimes <u>forget</u> my pen.
Rule 2: Adverbs of frequency go AFTER the verb "to be" (am, is, are, was, were).
 Subject + Verb 'to be' + Adverb
 Example:
o He is <u>always</u> late. (NOT: He always is late.)
o The students are <u>usually</u> quiet.
o I was <u>never</u> good at sports.

Topic: The Consonants /ʒ/ and /dʒ/
A. Introduction
This lesson focuses on two similar, voiced consonant sounds that often confuse learners:
 /ʒ/ (Voiced Palato-alveolar Fricative): This is the "zh" sound. It is a continuous,
buzzing sound, like the 's' in pleasure.
 /dʒ/ (Voiced Palato-alveolar Affricate): This is the "j" or "soft g" sound. It is a stop
sound that is released with a "zh" sound. It is not continuous.
Key Difference: /dʒ/ starts with a complete stop (like the /d/ sound), while /ʒ/ is a continuous,
hissing sound.
B. Articulatory Description & Spelling Patterns
39
1. The Sound /ʒ/ (as in leisure)
 How to make it: The tongue is held near the roof of the mouth (palate), and voiced air is
pushed through the narrow gap, creating friction (a "zh" hiss).
 Common Spellings:
o 's' + 'ion': vision, television, confusion, decision
o 's' + 'ure': pleasure, measure, treasure, leisure
o 'ge' (from French): garage, beige, prestige, massage
2. The Sound /dʒ/ (as in ledger)
 How to make it: The tongue tip first touches the ridge behind the teeth (like 'd'), stopping
the air. It is then released with friction (like 'ʒ'). It's a "d + zh" sound combined: /dʒ/.
 Common Spellings:
o 'j': June, jam, joy, judge, major
o 'g' (before e, i, y): giant, Germany, gym, gentle, age
o 'dge': ledger, bridge, edge, badge

2. GRAMMAR
Topic: Changing Positive Statements to Negative (using "not")
A. Introduction
A positive statement (or affirmative statement) says that something is true.
 Example: The boy is tall. She likes rice.
A negative statement says that something is not true. In English, we usually form this by adding
the word "not".
 Example: The boy is not tall. She does not like rice.
41
B. The Rules for Using "not"
The placement of "not" depends on the verb in the sentence.
Rule 1: Sentences with an Auxiliary (Helping) Verb This is the easiest rule. Auxiliary verbs
include:
 'to be': am, is, are, was, were
 'to have': has, have, had
 Modals: can, could, will, would, shall, should, must, may, might
Rule: Place "not" AFTER the auxiliary verb.
 Positive: He is sleeping.
o Negative: He is not sleeping. (or isn't)
 Positive: They have finished.
o Negative: They have not finished. (or haven't)
 Positive: You must touch it.
o Negative: You must not touch it. (or mustn't)
Rule 2: Sentences with NO Auxiliary Verb (Simple Present & Simple Past) This is the most
important rule. If the sentence only has a main verb (like play, eats, went, saw), we must add an
auxiliary verb to help. We add: do, does, or did.
 Simple Present (with I, You, We, They): Add do not (don't)
o Positive: They like school.
o Negative: They do not (don't) like school.
 Simple Present (with He, She, It): Add does not (doesn't)
o Positive: He likes school.
o Negative: He does not (doesn't) like school. (Note: the 's' on likes is removed).
 Simple Past (All subjects): Add did not (didn't)
o Positive: She went to the market.
42
o Negative: She did not (didn't) go to the market. (Note: the past tense went
changes back to the present tense go).

4. COMPOSITION
Topic: Expository Essay – How to Care for a Motor Vehicle
A. Introduction
An Expository Essay is a piece of writing that "exposes" (explains), describes, or informs about
a topic. It is factual, logical, and organized. It is not a story (narrative) and not an argument
(argumentative).
The topic "How to..." requires you to write a process essay. You must explain the steps for
doing something in a clear, logical order.
B. Features of an Expository "How-to" Essay
1. Clear Topic: The purpose is obvious from the start.
2. Logical Order: Steps are presented chronologically (first, next, finally).
3. Formal Tone: Use clear and direct language. Avoid slang ("...and stuff").
4. Transitional Words: Use words to guide the reader: First, Second, Next, Then, After that,
Furthermore, Finally.
5. Factual Content: The information should be accurate and helpful.
C. Planning the Essay: "How to Care for a Motor Vehicle"
 Introduction:
o Hook: Start with a general statement (e.g., "A car is one of the most valuable
possessions a person can have.")
o Background: Explain that it needs care to last long and be safe.
47
o Thesis Statement (Main Idea): State the main points you will discuss. (e.g.,
"Properly caring for a motor vehicle involves three essential areas: checking the
fluids, maintaining the tires, and keeping it clean.")
 Body Paragraph 1: Checking Fluids
o Topic Sentence: "First, the most important part of car care is checking the
fluids."
o Details: Explain how to check engine oil (dipstick), why it's important
(lubrication), and mention other fluids like coolant and brake fluid.
 Body Paragraph 2: Maintaining Tires
o Topic Sentence: "Second, a driver must never ignore the tires."
o Details: Explain checking tire pressure (why it's important: fuel, safety) and
checking the tread (grip, safety).
 Body Paragraph 3: Keeping it Clean
o Topic Sentence: "Finally, regular cleaning is vital for the car's longevity."
o Details: Explain washing the exterior (prevents rust/corrosion) and cleaning the
interior (healthy, pleasant).
 Conclusion:
o Restate Thesis: Summarize your main points (e.G., "In conclusion, by paying
attention to the fluids, tires, and cleanliness...")
o Final Thought: End with the benefit. (e.g., "...a car owner can ensure their
vehicle remains safe, reliable, and valuable for many years.")

5. LITERATURE IN ENGLISH
Topic: Prose – In-depth Analysis of Characterization, Theme, and Plot
A. Introduction
In Week 3, we introduced Plot, Character, and Theme. This week, we go deeper. These elements
are the keys to unlocking the meaning of your recommended prose text.
B. Deeper Look 1: PLOT & CONFLICT
 Plot: The sequence of events.
 Conflict: The engine of the plot. Conflict is the problem that the characters must face.
Without conflict, there is no story.
 Types of Conflict:
o Internal Conflict: Character vs. Self (a character struggling with a difficult
decision, fear, or a weakness in their own mind).
o External Conflict:
 Character vs. Character (The hero vs. the villain).
 Character vs. Nature (A character lost in a storm, or facing a wild animal).
 Character vs. Society (A character fighting against the rules, laws, or
traditions of their town).
C. Deeper Look 2: CHARACTERIZATION
 Characterization: The way an author creates and develops a character.
49
 Review:
o Protagonist: Main character (hero).
o Antagonist: Character who opposes the protagonist (villain).
 New Concepts:
o Direct Characterization: The author tells you the character's personality.
 Example: "Tunde was a very lazy and selfish boy."
o Indirect Characterization: The author shows you the character's personality
through their...
 Speech (What they say)
 Thoughts (What they think)
 Effect on others (How others react to them)
 Actions (What they do)
 Looks (How they dress or look)
 Example: "Tunde lay on the sofa watching TV, ignoring his mother as she
struggled to carry the heavy bag of water." (This shows us he is lazy and
selfish, without using those words).
 Types of Characters:
o Round Character: Complex, multi-sided, feels real (like the protagonist).
o Flat Character: Simple, one-sided (e.g., a "bad guy" who is just evil).
o Dynamic Character: A character who changes their personality or attitude by
the end of the story.
o Static Character: A character who stays the same from beginning to end.
D. Deeper Look 3: THEME
 Theme: The central message, idea, or truth about life that the story reveals.
 Theme vs. Topic:
o A Topic is one word (e.g., "Love," "War," "Greed").
o A Theme is a full sentence about the topic (e.g., "True love can overcome any
obstacle," "War destroys everyone," "Greed leads to a person's downfall").

2. GRAMMAR
Topic: Adjectives & Adverbs Expressing Willingness/Unwillingness
A. Introduction
Willingness is a state of being prepared, happy, or ready to do something. Unwillingness is a
state of being reluctant, hesitant, or refusing to do something.
We can express these ideas using:
 Modals: will, would, won't, wouldn't
54
 Adjectives: eager, willing, reluctant, unwilling
 Adverbs: readily, gladly, grudgingly, reluctantly
B. Expressing Willingness
1. Using Modals: The modal verb will (or would in the past/polite) shows readiness.
 Example: "Don't worry, I will help you with the bags."
 Example: "Would you help me, please?" (Asking about willingness)
2. Using Adjectives (Structure: to be + adjective + to-infinitive)
 willing to: He is willing to work late.
 eager to: The students are eager to learn.
 happy to: I am happy to help.
 prepared to: The school is prepared to fund the project.
 ready to: We are ready to start.
3. Using Adverbs (Structure: modal + adverb + verb or verb + adverb)
 gladly / cheerfully: I will gladly drive you. She cheerfully accepted the task.
 readily: He readily shared his notes. (meaning: willingly and without hesitation)
C. Expressing Unwillingness
1. Using Modals: The negative modals won't (will not) and wouldn't (would not) are the
strongest ways to show refusal.
 Example: "He is stubborn. He won't listen to advice."
 Example: "I asked him for help, but he wouldn't even look at me."
2. Using Adjectives (Structure: to be + adjective + to-infinitive)
 unwilling to: The man is unwilling to pay for the damage.
 reluctant to: She was reluctant to tell the teacher.
55
 hesitant to: They are hesitant to join the club.
3. Using Adverbs (Structure: verb + adverb)
 reluctantly: He reluctantly cleaned his room.
 grudgingly: She grudgingly admitted she was wrong. (meaning: angrily and reluctantly)

3. READING & COMPREHENSION

A. Introduction: What is a Deduction?
56
A deduction (or inference) is a conclusion you reach by using evidence and reasoning. In
reading, it means "reading between the lines."
It is the answer to the question: "What is the author showing me, without telling me directly?"
Deduction Formula: What the Text SAYS (Clue) + What I already KNOW (Background
Knowledge) = What I can DEDUCE (Inference)
B. Deduction vs. Literal Comprehension
 Literal Question: What colour was the girl's dress?
o Text: "The girl wore a red dress."
o Answer: Red. (The answer is stated directly.)
 Deductive Question: What was the weather like?
o Text: "The girl's umbrella was useless against the howling wind, and her clothes
were soaked to the skin before she even reached the bus stop."
o Answer: It was raining heavily and very windy. (The text never says "rain" or
"windy," but you can deduce it from "soaked" and "howling wind.")

5. LITERATURE IN ENGLISH
Topic: Drama (Analysis of Recommended Text)
A. Introduction
Drama is a form of literature designed to be performed by actors on a stage. It is different from
Prose (novels) because the story is told almost entirely through dialogue (what characters say)
and action (what characters do).
The written text of a play is called a script.
B. Key Elements of Drama
1. Plot:
o The sequence of events. In drama, the plot is structured into Acts (major divisions,
like chapters) and Scenes (smaller divisions within an Act).
o The plot is still driven by Conflict (Internal or External).
2. Characterization:
o In a novel, the author can tell you what a character is thinking (direct
characterization).
o In a play, you learn about characters indirectly through:
 Their Dialogue (what they say and how they say it).
 Their Actions (what they do).
61
 What other characters say about them.
3. Setting:
o The time and place of the story.
o In a script, this is described in the Stage Directions (the notes written in italics,
often in parentheses).
o Example: (The stage is dark. A single light on an old wooden chair. It is raining.)
4. Diction:
o The author's choice of words. Diction is very important in drama.
o Diction reveals a character's:
 Education: (e.g., using big words vs. simple words).
 Social Status: (e.g., speaking in formal language vs. slang).
 Origin: (e.g., using a specific dialect or accent).
 Emotional State: (e.g., speaking in short, sharp, angry sentences).
5. Rhyme Scheme (Note):
o "Rhyme Scheme" (like AABB, ABAB) is a key element of Poetry, not drama.
o Most modern plays (like your recommended text) are written in Prose (normal
sentences).
o However, some very old plays (like those by Shakespeare) are written in Verse
(poetic lines). Even then, they don't always rhyme. This term is more important
for poetry analysis.
C. Application (To Recommended Text)
(For the Teacher: Apply these concepts to the JSS3 recommended play, e.g., "The Successors" or
"The Trials of Brother Jero")
 Plot: What is the main conflict in our play?
 Setting: Where does Scene 1, Act 1 take place? How do we know from the stage
directions?
 Characterization: How do we know that Character X is the antagonist? (What does he
say or do that shows us?)
62
 Diction: Look at the way Character Y speaks. What does his choice of words tell us
about his education?

a fricative sound.

the /t/ sound).
2. Then, the air is released slowly with the /ʃ/ sound.

Topic: Adverbs of Place and Manner
66
A. Introduction
An Adverb is a word that modifies (describes) a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. This week,
we focus on two important types: Adverbs of Manner and Adverbs of Place.
B. Adverbs of Manner
 Function: Answers the question "How?" or "In what way?"
 Description: They describe how an action is performed.
 Formation: Many are formed by adding -ly to an adjective (e.g., slow
$\rightarrow$ slowly).
 Examples:
o slowly, quickly, happily, sadly, loudly, quietly, angrily, carefully
o Irregular: Some adverbs don't end in -ly.
 well (The adjective is "good." He did a good job. He did the job well.)
 fast (The adjective is also "fast." He is a fast runner. He runs fast.)
 hard (The adjective is also "hard." He works hard.)
 Sentence Placement: Usually placed after the verb or after the object.
o Example 1: He spoke loudly.
o Example 2: She closed the door gently.
C. Adverbs of Place
 Function: Answers the question "Where?"
 Description: They tell us the location of an action.
 Examples:
o here, there, everywhere, nowhere, somewhere
o inside, outside, upstairs, downstairs, abroad, underground
o Adverbial Phrases of Place: Sometimes a prepositional phrase functions as an
adverb of place.
 Example: He is sitting in the room. She went to the market.
 Sentence Placement: Almost always placed after the verb or after the object.
o Example 1: Please come inside.
67
o Example 2: I left the keys on the table.
D. Order of Adverbs
When Adverbs of Manner and Place are in the same sentence, there is a preferred order: Manner,
Place, Time (MPT). The Adverb of Manner (How?) comes before the Adverb of Place
(Where?).
 Correct: He sat quietly (Manner) in the corner (Place).
 Incorrect: He sat in the corner quietly. (This is awkward).
 Correct: She sang beautifully (Manner) on the stage (Place).
 Incorrect: She sang on the stage beautifully.

4. COMPOSITION
Topic: Debate – "Corruption is worse than Armed Robbery"
A. Introduction
A Debate is a formal, structured argument between two opposing sides on a specific topic,
known as a motion or proposition. It is not a quarrel or a shouting match; it is a contest of ideas,
logic, and persuasion.
 The Motion: "Corruption is worse than Armed Robbery."
 The Proposition (Affirmative): This side agrees with the motion. Their job is to prove
that corruption is worse.
 The Opposition (Negative): This side disagrees with the motion. Their job is to prove
that armed robbery is worse (or that corruption is not as bad).
B. The Structure of a Debate Point (A.E.E.C.)
A strong debate point is not just a statement. It must be built logically. A good model is A.E.E.C.
 Assertion: State your point (your "headline").
o e.g., "My first point is that corruption is a silent killer, while armed robbery is a
localized crime."
 Explanation: Explain why this is true.
o e.g., "This means that while an armed robber can only attack one family at a time,
a corrupt official who steals money meant for a hospital can cause the deaths of
thousands of people who can't get medicine."
 Example: Give a clear, powerful example.
71
o e.g., "When funds for a major highway are stolen, the result is a bad road. The
accidents on that road kill far more people in one year than a single robber ever
could."
 Conclusion: Link your point back to the motion.
o e.g., "Therefore, it is clear that the massive, hidden, and widespread damage of
corruption is far worse than the immediate, visible crime of armed robbery."

5. LITERATURE IN ENGLISH
Topic: Drama – Costumes and Props / Rhyme Schemes
A. Introduction
In drama, the story is not just told with words (dialogue) but also with visual elements. The
Stage Directions in a script tell the director and actors what the scene looks like and what to use.
Two of the most important visual elements are costumes and props.
B. Costumes
 Definition: Costumes are the clothes, accessories, and even makeup worn by an actor to
portray a specific character.
 Purpose: Costumes tell the audience essential information about the character before
they even speak.
 Costumes reveal:
1. Time Period: A character wearing a 1960s "Afro" and "bell-bottom" trousers
looks different from one in a modern suit.
2. Social Status: A king wears a crown and velvet; a servant wears simple, roughspun
clothes.
3. Occupation: A character in a white coat and stethoscope is a doctor. A character
with a uniform and helmet is a police officer.
73
4. Personality: A character who is sad and mourning might wear all black. A
vibrant, happy character might wear bright yellow.
5. Situation: A character in torn, dirty clothes is clearly in trouble.
C. Props (Properties)
 Definition: A prop (short for "property") is any movable object that an actor uses or
interacts with on stage. (Note: The fixed background, like the walls or a large sofa, is the
Set.)
 Examples: A teacup, a book, a letter, a knife, a walking stick, a telephone, a briefcase.
 Purpose:
1. To advance the plot: A letter might contain crucial news. A hidden knife might
be used in a fight. A ring might prove someone's identity.
2. To reveal character: A character who is always carrying a "holy book" is shown
to be religious. A character who is always polishing a gun is shown to be violent.
D. Rhyme Schemes (Revisiting the Topic)
 Clarification: As we have noted before, "Rhyme Scheme" (the pattern of rhymes at the
end of lines, like AABB or ABAB) is a primary element of POETRY, not drama.
 When is it used in Drama? Rhyme schemes only appear in a specific type of drama
called Verse Drama or Poetic Drama.
 Example: Many plays by William Shakespeare are written in poetic verse.
o e.g., "My only love sprung from my only hate! (A)
o Too early seen unknown, and known too late!" (A)
o This is a rhyming couplet (AA). It is used to add emphasis.
 Your Recommended Text: Most modern recommended texts (like The Successors or
Trials of Brother Jero) are written in Prose (normal, everyday language). They do not
have a rhyme scheme.

Topic: Contrast Between Consonants /ʃ/ and /tʃ/
A. Introduction
This lesson focuses on two voiceless consonants that are often confused:
 /ʃ/ (Voiceless Palato-alveolar Fricative): The "sh" sound. It is a continuous
hissing/hushing sound.
o Examples: shoe, wash, pressure, machine
 /tʃ/ (Voiceless Palato-alveolar Affricate): The "ch" sound. It is a stop sound that is
released with a "sh" sound. It is not continuous.
o Examples: chair, watch, catch, nature
Key Difference: The /tʃ/ sound begins with a complete stop of air (like /t/), while the /ʃ/ sound is
a continuous release of air.
76
B. Articulatory Description
 To make /ʃ/: The blade (middle-front) of the tongue is raised towards the hard palate.
The lips are often slightly rounded. Air is pushed through the narrow gap, creating a
continuous "shhhhh" sound.
 To make /tʃ/: This is an affricate.
1. First, the tongue tip touches the alveolar ridge (just behind the teeth), completely
stopping the air (like the /t/ sound).
2. Then, the air is released forcefully as a /ʃ/ sound.
o It is a single, sharp sound: /t/ + /ʃ/ = /tʃ/ (like a train's "choo-choo").

4. COMPOSITION
Topic: Descriptive Essay – My Favourite Subject
A. Introduction
A Descriptive Essay is a piece of writing that paints a picture with words. Its main purpose is to
make the reader see, hear, smell, taste, and feel what you are describing. It is not just telling the
reader; it is showing them.
82
 Telling (Boring): "My favourite subject is Mathematics. It is very interesting. I like
solving problems."
 Showing (Descriptive): "My favourite subject, Mathematics, is a thrilling puzzle. I love
the sharp, clean look of an equation on a fresh page, the satisfying 'click' in my brain
when a difficult problem finally makes sense, and the rush of triumph as I pencil in the
final, correct answer."
B. The Key: Using Sensory Details (The 5 Senses)
To write a great descriptive essay, you must appeal to the five senses:
 SIGHT (What do you see?):
o Subject: Mathematics $\rightarrow$ The white chalk on the green board, the
perfect circle drawn with a compass, the rows of numbers.
o Subject: English $\rightarrow$ The thick, old novels, the sharp red ink of the
teacher's corrections, the different countries in a story.
 SOUND (What do you hear?):
o Subject: Mathematics $\rightarrow$ The silence of deep concentration, the taptap-
tap of the calculator, the teacher's voice explaining a theorem.
o Subject: English $\rightarrow$ The musical rhythm of a poem, the powerful voice
of a character in a play, the sound of different accents.
 FEEL/TOUCH (What do you feel?):
o Subject: Mathematics $\rightarrow$ The smooth, cool surface of a protractor, the
anxiety of a difficult test, the relief of finding the answer.
o Subject: English $\rightarrow$ The rough pages of an old book, the sadness for a
character, the excitement of a plot twist.
 SMELL/TASTE (Harder, but possible):
o Subject: Home Economics $\rightarrow$ The sweet smell of baking cakes, the
spicy taste of a well-cooked stew.
o Subject: Agric Science $\rightarrow$ The earthy smell of fresh soil, the sharp tang
of fertilizer.
83
C. Planning the Essay: "My Favourite Subject" (Example: English)
 Introduction:
o Hook: Start with a question or a vivid statement. "While some students love the
cold logic of numbers, I have always been captivated by the power of words."
o Background: State your favourite subject and why, generally. "My favourite
subject is English Language and Literature."
o Thesis Statement: "It is my favourite because it allows me to explore new worlds,
understand deep emotions, and master the art of communication."
 Body Paragraph 1: (Sensory Details of the Class)
o Topic Sentence: "Walking into my English class feels different from any other."
o Details: Describe the sight of the tall bookshelf in the corner, the smell of old
paper and new books, the sound of the teacher reading a poem aloud with a rising
and falling voice.
 Body Paragraph 2: (The Feeling of Reading)
o Topic Sentence: "The greatest joy of English is disappearing into a story."
o Details: Describe the feeling of excitement during a plot twist, the sight of the

 Body Paragraph 3: (The Power of Writing)
o Topic Sentence: "Furthermore, English gives me a powerful voice."
o Details: Describe the feeling of satisfaction when you write a perfect sentence,

 Conclusion:
o Restate Thesis: "English is more than just a subject; it's a journey into worlds of
imagination, emotion, and expression."
o Final Thought: "It is the one class where I not only learn about the world but

Topic: The Consonants /w/ and /j/ (Semi-vowels)
A. Introduction
This lesson focuses on two unique voiced consonants known as semi-vowels or glides. They are
called "semi-vowels" because they are produced with very little obstruction, almost like vowels,
but they function as consonants in words.
 /w/ (Voiced Labio-velar Glide): The "w" sound, as in wet, away, queen.
 /j/ (Voiced Palatal Glide): The "y" sound, as in yes, use, onion.
NOTE: The symbol /j/ does not represent the "j" sound in "jam." That sound is /dʒ/. The symbol
/j/ always represents the "y" sound.
B. Articulatory Description
88
 To make /w/:
1. Start by rounding your lips tightly (like you are about to say /u:/ as in "who").
2. Your tongue is high at the back (near the velum).
3. Quickly glide away from this position to the vowel sound that follows.
o Examples: wet (glide from /w/ to /e/), win, wall.
 To make /j/:
0. Start by raising the front/middle of your tongue high against the hard palate (like
you are about to say /i:/ as in "see").
1. Your lips are spread (not rounded).
2. Quickly glide away from this position to the vowel sound that follows.
o Examples: yes (glide from /j/ to /e/), yard, yell.

2. GRAMMAR
Topic: Adverbs of Cause/Reason (Adverbial Clauses)
A. Introduction
The scheme refers to these as "Adverbs of Cause/Reason." While traditional adverbs can show
this (e.g., "He consequently left"), the examples so that and in order to function as
subordinating conjunctions that introduce adverbial clauses of purpose or reason.
These phrases and clauses answer the question: "Why?"
90
 Question: Why did you study hard?
 Answer (Reason/Purpose):
o because I wanted to pass.
o in order to pass.
o so that I could pass.
B. Common Phrases and Conjunctions
1. because / since / as (Show the Cause/Reason) These give the direct reason something
happened.
 Example: He failed because he did not read.
 Example: Since you are here, you can help.
 Example: As it was raining, we stayed indoors.
2. in order to / so as to (Show Purpose - Infinitive) These introduce the purpose of an action.
They are followed by a base verb (e.g., pass, eat, see).
 Structure: Action + in order to + Base Verb
 Example: He studied hard in order to pass the exam.
 Example: She woke up early in order to catch the bus.
 Example: We must eat so as to live.
3. so that / in order that (Show Purpose - Clause) These also introduce purpose, but they are
followed by a full clause (a subject + a modal verb like can, could, will, would).
 Structure: Action + so that + Subject + Modal + Verb
 Example: He studied hard so that he could pass the exam.
 Example: She woke up early so that she would catch the bus.
 Example: Give me the money so that I can buy the book.
C. Combining Sentences
 Sentences: 1. I am saving money. 2. I want to buy a bicycle.
91
 Combined (Reason): I am saving money because I want to buy a bicycle.
 Combined (Purpose): I am saving money in order to buy a bicycle.
 Combined (Purpose): I am saving money so that I can buy a bicycle.

3. READING & COMPREHENSION
Topic: Identifying Topic Sentences in Passages
A. Introduction
A topic sentence is the single most important sentence in a paragraph. It states the main idea or
central point of that paragraph. All other sentences in the paragraph (the "supporting sentences")
must explain, describe, or give examples of the topic sentence.
Finding the topic sentence is the key to understanding a text quickly.
92
B. Where to Find the Topic Sentence
The topic sentence can be in one of three places:
1. At the Beginning (Most Common) The author states the main idea first and then gives
examples.
(Topic Sentence) $\rightarrow$ The problem of waste in our major cities is becoming a
chronic emergency. As urban populations grow, the volume of refuse is staggering. In many
cities, plastic bags and bottles clog the drainage systems. This leads to severe flooding.
2. At the End (Less Common) The author gives several examples first, building up to a final
conclusion (the main idea).
As populations grow, the volume of refuse is staggering. Plastic bags and bottles clog drainage
systems, leading to severe flooding. Uncollected piles of refuse attract rats and spread diseases
like Lassa fever. (Topic Sentence) $\rightarrow$ Clearly, the problem of waste in our major
cities is a chronic emergency.
3. In the Middle (Least Common) The author starts with an introductory sentence, states the
main idea, and then gives examples.
Many people are wondering what our biggest urban problem is. (Topic Sentence)
$\rightarrow$ The problem of waste in our major cities is becoming a chronic emergency.
For example, plastic bags and bottles clog drainage systems, leading to severe flooding.

 The Opposition (Negative): Argues that Stealing is worse, or that CT is not as bad as it

o E(xample): A man who has his car stolen loses transportation. A child who is

o C(onclusion): Therefore, the theft of a human life and future is infinitely worse

1. A(ssertion): Child trafficking is a terrible but specific crime, while stealing, in the form

Topic: The Consonants /t/ and /θ/
A. Introduction
This lesson focuses on two voiceless consonants that are often confused, leading to
pronunciation errors. The difference is based on where you place your tongue.
 /t/ (Voiceless Alveolar Stop): This is the sound for "t" in tin, tick, and pot.
 /θ/ (Voiceless Dental Fricative): This is the "th" sound in thin, thick, and path.
B. Articulatory Description (How to Make the Sounds)
1. To make the /t/ sound:
104
 Tongue: The tip of your tongue touches the alveolar ridge (the hard ridge just behind
your top teeth).
 Air: You stop the air completely for a moment, then release it in a small puff.
 Vocal Cords: Do not vibrate (voiceless).
 Practice: Say "t-t-t-t-t." Feel your tongue tapping behind your teeth.
2. To make the /θ/ sound:
 Tongue: The tip of your tongue touches the bottom of your top front teeth, or is placed
just between your top and bottom teeth.
 Air: You push air continuously through the narrow gap between your tongue and teeth.
This creates a hissing, fricative sound.
 Vocal Cords: Do not vibrate (voiceless).
 Practice: Say "th-th-th-th" (as in think). Feel the air hissing over your tongue and against
your teeth.
Key Difference: /t/ is a stop sound (it stops). /θ/ is a fricative (it's continuous, like /s/ or /f/).

2. GRAMMAR
Topic: Revision of Parts of Speech
A. Introduction
The Parts of Speech are the categories we give to words based on their function in a sentence. A
word's part of speech depends on how it is used. There are eight parts of speech.
B. The Eight Parts of Speech
1. Noun:
 Definition: Names a person (Bello), place (Lagos), thing (book), or idea (love).
 Functions: Can be the subject, object, or complement of a sentence.
 Types: Proper (Nigeria), Common (country), Abstract (honesty), Concrete (table),
Collective (flock).
 Example: Ade kicked the ball in the park.
2. Pronoun:
 Definition: Replaces a noun to avoid repetition.
 Types: Personal (I, he, she, it, us, them), Possessive (my, his, our, theirs), Relative (who,
which, that).
 Example: Ade kicked it. He is a good player.
3. Verb:
 Definition: Shows action (run, jump) or a state of being (is, are, feel, seem). It is the
heart of the sentence.
 Types: Transitive (needs an object - He kicked the ball), Intransitive (no object - He
slept).
 Example: The students wrote the test and felt happy.
4. Adjective:
107
 Definition: Modifies (describes or limits) a noun or pronoun. It answers: What kind?
How many? Which one?
 Example: The tall boy bought three new books. She is smart.
5. Adverb:
 Definition: Modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. It answers: How? When?
Where? How often? To what extent?
 Example: He ran quickly (modifies ran). It is very hot (modifies hot).
6. Preposition:
 Definition: Shows the relationship between a noun/pronoun and another word in the
sentence (often showing location or time).
 Example: The book is on the table, under the chair, near the window. He arrived at 5
p.m.
7. Conjunction:
 Definition: Joins words, phrases, or clauses.
 Types:
o Coordinating (FANBOYS): For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So.
o Subordinating: because, while, although, if, when.
 Example: I like rice and beans, but I won't eat until the class ends.
8. Interjection:
 Definition: An exclamatory word that shows strong, sudden emotion. It is grammatically
separate from the sentence.
 Example: Wow! That is a great score. Ouch! You stepped on my foot.

Topic: Reading for Critical Evaluation
A. Introduction
In JSS1 and JSS2, you learned to read for comprehension (understanding what the text says). In

Critical Evaluation is the process of thinking deeply about a text. You do not just accept the

B. What is Critical Evaluation?
Critical evaluation involves:
1. Distinguishing Fact from Opinion:
o Fact: A statement that can be proven true or false. Example: "Nigeria is a country

o Opinion: A statement of belief, feeling, or judgment. It cannot be proven true or
false. Example: "Nigeria is the best country in the world."
2. Identifying the Author's Purpose:
o Why did the author write this?
o To Inform: (e.g., a textbook, a news report).
o To Persuade: (e.g., a debate, an advertisement, an editorial).
o To Entertain: (e.g., a novel, a comic).
3. Detecting Bias:
o Is the author presenting a one-sided view? Are they using emotionally loaded
words to make you feel a certain way?
o Example (Biased): "That foolish company is destroying our pure, beautiful
forests."
o Example (Unbiased): "The company is cutting down trees in the forest."

5. LITERATURE IN ENGLISH
Topic: Review of Features of Folktales; Discussion of Selected Folktales
A. Introduction
A folktale is a story that has been passed down from one generation to another through oral
tradition (word of mouth) before being written down. Folktales are a key part of orature (oral
literature) and are found in every culture.
B. Key Features of Folktales
1. Oral Tradition: They were originally told, not read. This is why they are often simple,
repetitive, and use memorable language.
2. Formulaic Opening/Closing: They often start and end with a set formula.
o Opening: "Once upon a time..." or "A long time ago..."
o Closing: "...and they lived happily ever after." or "This is my story..."
3. Stock Characters: The characters are often simple and represent one main quality. They
are rarely complex.
o The Trickster: A clever, cunning animal or person who uses their wits to trick
others (e.g., the Tortoise, Anansi the Spider, the Hare).
o The Hero: A brave (often young) person who goes on a quest.
o The Villain: A simple "bad" character (e.g., the wicked stepmother, the greedy
king, the hyena).
4. Magic and Supernatural Elements: Events happen that are not possible in real life (e.g.,
animals talk, magic pots, enchanted forests, ghosts, transformations).
5. A Clear Moral or Lesson: Folktales are meant to teach a lesson about good and bad
behaviour, or to explain why something is the way it is (e.g., "Why the Tortoise's Shell is
Cracked"). The moral is the most important part.
6. Simple Plot: The story is usually straightforward (Beginning, Middle, End) and easy to
follow and remember.
C. Discussion of Selected Folktales
116
(The teacher will select a well-known African folktale, such as "The Tortoise and the Hare" or
"How the Tortoise Got His Cracked Shell.")
Discussion Questions to Apply to the Folktale:
1. Opening: How does this story begin? Does it use a formula?
2. Characters: Who is the trickster in this story? What character trait does he represent?
(e.g., greed, cunning, wisdom).
3. Supernatural: What events happen in this story that cannot happen in real life? (e.g.,
"Animals are having a feast in the sky.")
4. Plot: What problem did the main character face? How did he/she solve it?
5. Moral: What is the moral or lesson this story is trying to teach us? (e.g., "Don't be
greedy," "Pride comes before a fall," "Listen to your elders.")

1. SPEECH WORK
Topic: Consonant Contrast /t/ and /s/
A. Introduction
This lesson focuses on two voiceless consonants that share the same place of articulation (the
alveolar ridge) but differ in their manner of articulation.
 /t/ (Voiceless Alveolar Stop): This is the sound in tailor, tin, and pit.
 /s/ (Voiceless Alveolar Fricative): This is the sound in sailor, sin, and pass.
The confusion often arises from the similar tongue position, but the way the air is released is
completely different.
B. Articulatory Description (How to Make the Sounds)
1. To make the /t/ sound:
118
 Manner: Stop (or Plosive)
 Tongue: The tip of your tongue presses firmly against the alveolar ridge (the hard ridge
just behind your top teeth).
 Air: The airflow is completely stopped for a moment.
 Release: The air is released in a single, sharp puff: "t... t... t..."
2. To make the /s/ sound:
 Manner: Fricative
 Tongue: The tip of your tongue is held close to the alveolar ridge, but it does not touch
it.
 Air: The airflow is not stopped. It is forced through the tiny, narrow gap.
 Release: The air is released continuously, creating a hissing sound: "ssssssss..."
Key Difference: /t/ is a short tap that stops the air. /s/ is a long hiss that squeezes the air.

Topic: Prepositions Expressing Relations with People (with, for, against)
A. Introduction
Prepositions are words that show the relationship between a noun (or pronoun) and other words

o Example: "Our school is playing a match with your school."

o Example: "Are you for or against this idea?" (Do you support it?)

o Example: "He has deep feelings for her."

Topic: Listening for Implied Meanings
A. Introduction
In communication, people often say one thing but mean another. The implied meaning is the
true meaning that is suggested, not stated directly.
When we are listening, we don't just listen to the words; we listen to the tone of voice. Tone of
Voice: The emotion, pitch, and volume a speaker uses. It tells us their true feelings (e.g., anger,
sadness, sarcasm, excitement).
Implied Meaning = Words + Tone of Voice
B. Scenarios: Stated Meaning vs. Implied Meaning

o Implied Meaning: "I am just asking about your plans."
 Tone 2 (Disapproving): Teacher says it with stress on the word "that," sounding shocked.
o Implied Meaning: "Your clothes are inappropriate/dirty/strange, and I don't want

cords vibrate). It is the sound in pleasure, vision, and garage.
How to check for voicing: Place your fingers lightly on your throat (your voice box).

 Vocal Cords: Do not vibrate.
 Example words: shoe, wash, Chicago, nation, mission, pressure, sure.

 Lips: The lip position is identical to /ʃ/.

 Vocal Cords: You must vibrate your vocal cords.

 Example words: pleasure, measure, treasure, vision, decision, television, beige, garage,

2. GRAMMAR
Topic: The Simple Sentence
A. Introduction
A sentence is a group of words that makes complete sense. It must contain a subject and a finite
verb. A clause is a group of words containing a subject and a verb. There are three main types of
sentences: Simple, Compound, and Complex. This week, we focus on the Simple Sentence.
B. Defining the Simple Sentence
A simple sentence consists of only one independent clause. An independent clause is a group
of words that can stand on its own and make complete sense. The core of a simple sentence is
one Subject (S) and one Finite Verb (V).
 Example: The boy (S) cried (V).
 Example: She (S) is (V) a doctor.
 Example: Birds (S) fly (V).
C. Simple Sentences with Compound Parts
This is the most important concept to understand: A simple sentence can have more than one
subject or more than one verb, but it is still a simple sentence as long as it is one single clause.
1. Simple Sentence (SS) with Compound Subject (S + S):
o Formula: S + S + V
o Example: Bisi and Tunde (S+S) are reading (V).
o (This is one clause. The subject is "Bisi and Tunde".)
2. Simple Sentence (SS) with Compound Verb (V + V):
o Formula: S + V + V
o Example: The man (S) laughed and jumped (V+V).
o (This is one clause. The verb is "laughed and jumped".)
3. Simple Sentence (SS) with Compound Subject and Verb:
133
o Formula: S + S + V + V
o Example: The boys and girls (S+S) sang and danced (V+V).
o (This is still a simple sentence because it is one independent clause.)
D. Simple Sentence Patterns
All simple sentences follow a few basic patterns:
1. S + V (Subject + Verb):
o Example: The baby cried.
2. S + V + O (Subject + Verb + Object):
o Example: The girl kicked the ball.
3. S + V + C (Subject + Verb + Complement): (The complement renames or describes the
subject)
o Example: He is a teacher. (teacher = He)
o Example: She seems happy. (happy = She)
4. S + V + A (Subject + Verb + Adverbial): (The adverbial tells where or when)
o Example: They went to the market.
E. What a Simple Sentence is NOT:
 Compound Sentence: Two independent clauses joined by FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but,
or, yet, so).
o Example: [The boy cried], but [the girl laughed]. (Two clauses)
 Complex Sentence: One independent clause and one or more dependent clauses (joined
by because, when, if, although, etc.).
o Example: [The boy cried] because [he was hungry]. (One independent, one
dependent)

2. GRAMMAR
Topic: The Compound Sentence
A. Introduction
In Week 3, we studied the Simple Sentence, which has only one independent clause (one
subject-verb unit).
 Example (Simple): The boy ran.
 Example (Simple, compound subject): The boy and the girl ran.
This week, we study the Compound Sentence. A compound sentence is made of two or more
independent clauses joined together. It has at least two separate subject-verb units.
 Independent Clause 1: The boy sang. (This is a full sentence).
 Independent Clause 2: The girl danced. (This is a full sentence).
 Compound Sentence: The boy sang, **and** the girl danced.
B. How to Join Independent Clauses
You must use the correct "glue" to join two independent clauses.
Method 1: Coordinating Conjunctions (FANBOYS) This is the most common method. The
"FANBOYS" are seven conjunctions.
 For (shows reason)
 And (adds information)
 Nor (adds a negative)
 But (shows contrast)
 Or (shows a choice)
 Yet (shows contrast, like 'but')
 So (shows a result)
147
Punctuation Rule: You must use a comma (,) before the FANBOYS conjunction. Formula:
[Independent Clause 1] , [FANBOYS] [Independent Clause 2]
 And: He is a good student, and he is also a talented artist.
 But: I studied hard, but I failed the test.
 Or: You can go to the market, or you can stay home.
 So: She was tired, so she went to bed.
 For: I went to sleep, for I was very tired. (Note: 'for' is formal, like 'because').
Method 2: The Semicolon ( ; ) You can use a semicolon to join two independent clauses that
are very closely related.
 Example: The man is a doctor; his wife is a lawyer.
Method 3: The Semicolon + Conjunctive Adverb
 Example: He studied hard; **however,** he failed the test.
C. Simple vs. Compound (A common confusion)
 Simple Sentence (with compound verb): Bisi sang **and** danced.
o (One subject, "Bisi," does two things. This is ONE clause).
 Compound Sentence: Bisi sang, **and** Tunde danced.
o (Two subjects, "Bisi" and "Tunde," do two different things. This is TWO clauses).

3. READING & LISTENING COMPREHENSION
Topic: Listening to Questions in Order to Answer Them
A. Introduction
This lesson focuses on a critical listening skill: understanding a question before you answer it.
The way a question is asked (its structure and intonation) tells you exactly what kind of answer is
expected.
If you misunderstand the question, you will give the wrong answer, even if you know the
information.
B. Types of Questions (and How to Answer Them)
Type 1: Yes/No Questions
 How to recognize: They begin with an auxiliary verb (e.g., Is, Are, Was, Do, Did, Have,
Can, Will).
 Intonation: They have a rising tone (↗) at the end.
 Expected Answer: "Yes" or "No," followed by a short explanation.
 Example Question: "Did you eat breakfast this morning? (↗)"
 Wrong Answer: "I ate rice."
 Correct Answer: "Yes, I did. I ate rice." or "No, I did not."
Type 2: "Wh-" Questions (Information Questions)
 How to recognize: They begin with an interrogative word (e.g., What, Where, When,
Who, Why, How).
 Intonation: They have a falling tone (↘) at the end.
 Expected Answer: A specific piece of information.
 Question: "What did you eat for breakfast? (↘)"
 Wrong Answer: "Yes, I did."
 Correct Answer: "I ate rice."
150
Breakdown of "Wh-" Words:
 What? (Asks for a thing or idea): "What is that?"
 Who? (Asks for a person): "Who is the principal?"
 Where? (Asks for a place): "Where is your book?"
 When? (Asks for a time): "When are you leaving?"
 Why? (Asks for a reason): "Why were you late?"
 How? (Asks for a manner/process): "How does this work?"
Type 3: Alternative Questions (Choice Questions)
 How to recognize: They contain the word "or" and give a choice.
 Intonation: The tone rises on the first choice and falls on the second.
 Expected Answer: One of the choices.
 Example Question: "Do you want tea (↗) or coffee (↘)?"
 Wrong Answer: "Yes."
 Correct Answer: "I would like coffee, please."

o Key Element: Atmosphere (Mood). The setting helps create the feeling of the
story (e.g., a dark, rainy night creates a tense, scary atmosphere).

156
o Definition: The central idea, moral, or lesson about life that the author wants to
share.
o Key Element: Theme is a sentence, not one word.
 Topic: "Greed"
 Theme: "Greed leads to a person's downfall."

1. SPEECH WORK
Topic: The Nasal Sounds /m/, /n/, and /ŋ/
A. Introduction
This lesson focuses on the three nasal consonants in English. They are called "nasal" because
when we produce them, the airflow is blocked from leaving the mouth and is released through
the nose instead. All three are voiced, meaning your vocal cords vibrate.
 /m/ (Voiced Bilabial Nasal): The sound in mentor, hammer, sum.
 /n/ (Voiced Alveolar Nasal): The sound in notty, know, sun.
 /ŋ/ (Voiced Velar Nasal): The "ng" sound, as in fling, sing, bank.
B. Articulatory Description (How to Make the Sounds)
The only difference between these three sounds is the place where the air is blocked in the mouth.
159
1. To make the /m/ sound:
o Place: Bilabial (using both lips).
o Action: Press your lips firmly together, blocking the air from leaving the mouth.
o Air: Release the voiced air through your nose.
o Practice: Say "m-m-m-m" and feel the vibration on your lips.
o Examples: man, more, lamb, swim, hammer.
2. To make the /n/ sound:
o Place: Alveolar (using the tongue-tip).
o Action: Press the tip of your tongue against the alveolar ridge (the hard ridge just
behind your top teeth).
o Air: Release the voiced air through your nose.
o Practice: Say "n-n-n-n" and feel the vibration on your tongue tip.
o Examples: now, never, manner, know, knotty, sun.
3. To make the /ŋ/ sound (the 'ng' sound):
o Place: Velar (using the back of the tongue).
o Action: Raise the back of your tongue to press against the velum (the soft palate,
at the back roof of your mouth).
o Air: Release the voiced air through your nose.
o Practice: Say "sing... sing... sing" and hold the final "ng" sound: "si-ŋ-ŋ-ŋ". Feel
the sound vibrating at the back of your mouth, not the front.
o Spelling Rule 1: Always found with the letters ng.
 Examples: sing, long, fling, wrong, hanging.
o Spelling Rule 2: Often found when n comes before k or g.
 Examples: bank (/bæŋk/), think (/θɪŋk/), uncle (/ˈʌŋkl/), anger
(/ˈæŋgə(r)/).

Topic: Punctuation Marks – Uses of Capital Letters
A. Introduction
Capital letters (also called "majuscules" or "upper case") are an essential punctuation mark.
Their main purpose is to show the beginning of a sentence or to identify a proper, specific
name. Using them incorrectly can cause confusion.
B. The Major Rules for Capitalization
1. The First Word of a Sentence:
o This is the most important rule. Every new sentence must begin with a capital
letter.
o Example: The boy is here. Where is he going?
2. Proper Nouns (Specific Names):
o People: Capitalize specific names of people.
 Example: Ade, Musa, Mr. Okonjo, Bello.
o Places: Specific cities, states, countries, continents, rivers, and mountains.
 Example: Ibadan, Lagos State, Nigeria, Africa, River Niger.
o Organizations: Specific schools, companies, teams.
 Example: Government Secondary School, United Nations, Google.
3. Proper Adjectives:
o Adjectives that are formed from proper nouns.
o Example: I am a Nigerian. I speak English. I like African music.
4. Days, Months, and Holidays (But not Seasons):
o Correct: Monday, January, December, Christmas, Eid el-Kabir.
o Incorrect: Do not capitalize seasons: summer, winter, harmattan, rainy season.
5. The Pronoun "I":
o The single-letter pronoun "I" is always capitalized, no matter where it is in a
sentence.
o Example: He said that I must go.

6. Titles of People (When used with their name):
o Capitalize titles like Dr., Mrs., Mr., King, President, Principal when they come
before a name.
o Example: Principal Bello arrived. We spoke to Dr. Smith.

7. Titles of Works (Books, Movies, Poems):
o Capitalize the first word, the last word, and all "important" words (nouns, verbs,
adjectives).
o Example: Things Fall Apart
o Example: The Lion and the Jewel (Note the small 'a' and 't').
8. Acronyms and Initialisms:
o Words formed from the first letters of other words.
o Example: JSS (Junior Secondary School), ECOWAS, WAEC, CNN.

o If the quote is a full sentence.

A. Introduction

 Sarcastic Tone: Low, slow, unenthusiastic.
 Interpretation: The speaker is not happy about the test.
3. Word Choice (Diction): What words does the speaker use?
o Neutral Words: "The government's new policy..."
o Biased Words (Negative): "The government's disastrous, foolish, job-killing
policy..."
o Biased Words (Positive): "The government's brilliant, life-saving, heroic
policy..."

Topic: Drama – Theme, Setting, Characterization in Recommended Drama Text
A. Introduction
This lesson is a focused review of the three most important elements of your recommended

village, a king's palace, a city) of the play.

sad or tense).

o Protagonist: The main character, who the audience follows.

o Dynamic Character: A character who changes or learns a lesson by the end.

A. Introduction
This lesson addresses a complex but important vowel contrast. The scheme of work provides

1. /æ/ (Short 'a'): The vowel in cat, hat, man. It is a short front vowel.
2. /ɑː/ (Long 'ah'): The vowel in father, car, arm. It is a long, open, back vowel. The

3. /ɜ:/ (Long 'er'): The vowel in bird, nurse, earn. It is a long, central vowel. The scheme's

B. Articulatory Description (How to Make the Sounds)

 Tongue: Your tongue is flat and in the center of your mouth.
 Lips: Your lips are relaxed and in a neutral shape (unrounded).
 Sound: It is a long, steady sound from the middle of your throat: "errrrr."

Topic: Reflexive and Relative Pronouns
A. PART 1: REFLEXIVE PRONOUNS
1. Definition: A Reflexive Pronoun is a pronoun that ends in -self (singular) or -selves (plural).
It is used when the subject and the object of a verb are the same person or thing.
2. List of Reflexive Pronouns:
 Singular: myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself
 Plural: ourselves, yourselves (for more than one 'you'), themselves
3. Functions:
 Function 1: As an Object (Reflexive Use)
o This is used when the subject performs an action to itself.
o Example: I saw myself in the mirror. (Subject = I, Object = myself)
o Example: He accidentally cut himself.
o Example: The cat is washing itself.
o Example: They fooled themselves.
o Common Error: Incorrect: "Please call my wife and myself." Correct: "Please
call my wife and me." (Use "myself" only if "I" is the subject).
 Function 2: As an Emphatic Pronoun (Intensive Use)
o This is used to add emphasis to a noun or pronoun. It means "that person/thing
and nobody else."
o Example: The principal himself came to our class. (This emphasizes "the
principal.")
o Example: I will do it myself. (This means "I will do it personally, without help.")
B. PART 2: RELATIVE PRONOUNS
176
1. Definition: A Relative Pronoun is a pronoun that introduces a dependent clause (called a
relative clause) and connects it to a noun that came before it. It gives us more information about
that noun.
2. List and Usage:
 who: Refers to people (as the subject).
o Example: That is the man who stole the car. (The relative clause "who stole the
car" describes "the man.")
 whom: Refers to people (as the object). It is formal and often replaced by "who."
o Example: That is the man whom the police arrested. (The police arrested him.)
 whose: Refers to possession (for people, animals, or things).
o Example: He is the boy whose book was stolen.
 which: Refers to animals or things.
o Example: This is the book which I bought yesterday.
o Example: The car, which is red, belongs to my father.
 that: Refers to people, animals, or things. (It is less formal than who or which).
o Example: He is the man that lives next door.
o Example: This is the book that I bought.

2. GRAMMAR
Topic: Personal Pronouns – Rules of Usage (Case)
A. Introduction
Personal Pronouns are words that replace nouns referring to people or things (e.g., I, me, he,
him, they, them). A common error is using the wrong form (or case) of a pronoun. The case of a
pronoun changes based on its grammatical job in the sentence.
There are three main cases: Subjective, Objective, and Possessive.
B. Rule 1: The Subjective Case (Nominative)
 Pronouns: I, you, he, she, it, we, they
 Rule: Use the subjective case when the pronoun is the SUBJECT of the verb (the person
or thing doing the action).
 Examples:
o He is a teacher. (Who is a teacher? He is.)
o They went to the market. (Who went? They did.)
 Common Error (Compound Subjects):
o Incorrect: "My friend and me are in the same class."
o Correction: To check, remove the other person: "Me am in the same class."
(Wrong). "I am in the same class." (Right).
o Correct: "My friend and I are in the same class."
C. Rule 2: The Objective Case (Accusative)
 Pronouns: me, you, him, her, it, us, them
 Rule (a): Use the objective case when the pronoun is the OBJECT of the verb (the
person or thing receiving the action).
o Example: The principal saw him. (The principal saw who? Him.)
o Example: The dog followed us.
191
 Rule (b): Use the objective case after a PREPOSITION (e.g., to, for, with, between, at,
on).
o Example: The gift is for her.
o Example: Please give the book to me.
 Common Error (Compound Objects):
o Incorrect: "Between you and I, this is a secret."
o Correct: "Between you and me, this is a secret." (Because "between" is a
preposition).
o Incorrect: "The teacher punished Tunde and I."
o Correct: "The teacher punished Tunde and me." (Tunde and me are the objects).
D. Rule 3: The Possessive Case
This case shows ownership. It has two forms:
1. Possessive Adjectives:
o Words: my, your, his, her, its, our, their
o Rule: These words MUST be followed by a noun.
o Example: That is my book. This is our class.
2. Possessive Pronouns:
o Words: mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs
o Rule: These words stand ALONE. They replace the noun.
o Example: That book is mine. The class is ours.
 Common Error (Apostrophes):
o Incorrect: The book is our's. The dog wagged it's tail.
o Correct: The book is ours. The dog wagged its tail. (Note: It's = "It is").

Topic: Poetry – Language in Poetry; Construct Sentences with Irony and Hyperbole
A. Introduction
Poetry uses language in a much more compressed and powerful way than prose. Poets use

200

not commit, crime.)
o Example: "The man who invented the 'Stop' sign was killed in a car accident."
3. Dramatic Irony: (Mostly in plays/stories)

3. READING & LISTENING COMPREHENSION
Topic: Reading to Summarize Paragraphs
A. Introduction

 Skimming (to find the main idea).

sentences, using your own words.

Topic: Differentiating Demonstrative Pronouns and Demonstrative Adjectives
A. Introduction
This lesson revises the four demonstrative words: this, that, these, and those. These words are
used to "point out" or "demonstrate" specific nouns. They can function as either pronouns or
adjectives (determiners), and it is crucial to know the difference.
B. Review: The Four Demonstratives
1. Proximity (Distance):
o Near: this, these
o Far: that, those

o Singular: this, that
o Plural: these, those
NEAR (Here) FAR (There)
Singular (one) this that
Plural (many) these those
C. The Core Difference: Function
The word's function depends entirely on its job in the sentence.
Function 1: Demonstrative Pronoun
 Job: It replaces a noun. It stands alone as the subject or object.
 Formula: Demonstrative + Verb (e.g., This is...) OR Verb + Demonstrative (e.g., I want

 Examples:
o "This is my book." (Here, "This" is the subject. It replaces "This thing.")

 Formula: Demonstrative + Noun
 Examples:
o "This book is mine." (Here, "This" modifies the noun "book.")
o "That smell is wonderful." (Here, "That" modifies the noun "smell.")
o "I will buy these shoes." (Here, "these" modifies the noun "shoes.")
o "Those friends are yours." (Here, "Those" modifies the noun "friends.")

As a Pronoun (Stands alone) As an Adjective (Comes before a noun)

That is your house. That house is yours.
These are new. These shoes are new.
Those are rotten. Those mangoes are rotten.

4. COMPOSITION
Topic: Expository Writing – "Population Explosion"
A. Introduction
An Expository Essay is a formal piece of writing that explains, informs, and describes a topic.
It is not a story (narrative) or an argument (debate), but a factual analysis. The goal is to make a
complex topic easy for the reader to understand.
Topic: "Population Explosion" (the rapid, sudden, and massive increase in the human
population).
B. Key Features of an Expository Essay
1. Clear Thesis Statement: A sentence in the introduction that states the main idea of the
entire essay.
2. Logical Structure: Organized into paragraphs, each with a clear topic sentence.
3. Factual Evidence: Uses facts, examples, and logical reasoning (not personal, emotional
opinions).
4. Formal Tone: No slang. The language is serious and academic.
5. Clear Transitions: Uses words like First, Furthermore, However, In conclusion.
C. Planning the Essay: "Population Explosion"
 Title: Population Explosion: Causes and Consequences
 Introduction:
o Hook: Start with a powerful general statement. (e.g., "The human population on
Earth is growing at an alarming rate, faster than at any other time in history.")
o Definition: Define "population explosion." (e.g., "This rapid and dramatic
increase in the number of people is known as population explosion.")
o Thesis Statement: State the main points your essay will cover. (e.g., "This
dangerous trend is caused by several key factors and has devastating effects on
our planet, economy, and society.")
226
 Body Paragraph 1: The Causes
o Topic Sentence: "There are two main causes of this rapid population growth."
o Point 1 (Decreased Death Rate): Explain that advances in modern medicine,
better sanitation, and increased food production mean people are living longer
(lower infant mortality, higher life expectancy).
o Point 2 (High Birth Rate): Explain that in many cultures, there is a preference
for large families, or a lack of access to education and family planning.
 Body Paragraph 2: The Effects on Environment & Society
o Topic Sentence: "The most immediate consequence of this explosion is the
immense strain on our environment and social services."
o Point 1 (Resources): More people demand more resources, leading to
deforestation (cutting down trees for housing), water shortages, and pollution.
o Point 2 (Services): There is too much pressure on hospitals, schools, and housing,
leading to overcrowded classrooms and slums.
 Body Paragraph 3: The Effects on the Economy
o Topic Sentence: "Furthermore, a nation's economy is often the greatest victim of
population explosion."
o Point 1 (Unemployment): There are more people than available jobs, leading to
massive youth unemployment.
o Point 2 (Poverty): This high unemployment leads to increased poverty and a rise
in crime rates as people become desperate.
 Conclusion:
o Summary: "In conclusion, population explosion is a serious global crisis caused
by falling death rates and high birth rates."
o Final Thought: "It leads to environmental damage, a strain on services, and
widespread poverty. Therefore, it is essential for governments to promote
sustainable education and family planning to secure a stable future for humanity."

A. Introduction
In English speech, there are 20 vowel sounds. These are divided into Monophthongs (12) and
Diphthongs (8). This lesson revises the 12 monophthongs.
A Monophthong (from Greek mono, meaning "one," and phthong, meaning "sound") is a pure
vowel sound. The sound has a single, unchanging quality. Your tongue and lips remain in one
position while making the sound. This is different from a diphthong, where your tongue "glides"
from one position to another (e.g., /aɪ/ in "my").
The 12 monophthongs are divided into short vowels and long vowels.
B. In-Depth Review: The 12 Monophthongs
243
1. The Short Vowels (7)
1. /ɪ/ (Short 'i'): As in hit, sit, ship, bin.
o Tongue: High and front. Lips: Spread. Sound: Quick.
2. /e/ (Short 'e'): As in head, set, bed, men.
o Tongue: Mid-front. Lips: Spread. Sound: Quick.
3. /æ/ (Short 'a'): As in cat, hat, bad, man.
o Tongue: Low and front. Lips: Spread wide. Sound: Quick.
4. /ɒ/ (Short 'o'): As in hot, pot, got, not.
o Tongue: Low and back. Lips: Loosely rounded. Sound: Quick.
5. /ʊ/ (Short 'oo'): As in put, book, could, should.
o Tongue: High and back. Lips: Rounded. Sound: Quick.
6. /ʌ/ (Short 'u'): As in cut, bus, sun, money.
o Tongue: Low and central. Lips: Neutral/Unrounded. Sound: Quick.
7. /ə/ (The Schwa): The weak, unstressed sound. As in about, teacher, pencil.

2. GRAMMAR (ENGLISH STRUCTURE)
Topic: Review of Nouns and Pronouns
A. Introduction
This lesson revises the first two parts of speech, which are the fundamental "naming words" that
anchor a sentence: Nouns and Pronouns.
B. In-Depth Review of Nouns
A Noun is a word that names a person, animal, place, thing, or idea.
1. Types of Nouns:
 Proper Nouns: Specific names of people, places, or organizations. They are always
capitalized.
o Examples: Bello, Lagos, Nigeria, Monday, Google.
 Common Nouns: General names for a class of people, places, or things.
o Examples: boy, city, country, day, company.
 Abstract Nouns: Names of ideas, qualities, feelings, or concepts. You cannot see or
touch them.
o Examples: love, honesty, fear, bravery, time, wisdom.
 Concrete Nouns: Names of things you can experience with your five senses (see, touch,
smell, hear, taste).
o Examples: table, smoke, music, perfume, book.
 Collective Nouns: A singular name for a group of individuals.
o Examples: flock (of sheep), army (of soldiers), committee, family, team.
 Countable Nouns: Nouns that can be counted and have a plural form.
o Examples: one book, two books; one man, two men.
 Uncountable (Mass) Nouns: Nouns that cannot be counted and usually have no plural
form.
o Examples: water, rice, information, advice, luggage, furniture.
246
2. Functions of Nouns in a Sentence:
 Subject of a Verb: The person/thing doing the action.
o Example: The teacher arrived.
 Object of a Verb: The person/thing receiving the action.
o Example: I saw the teacher.
 Subject Complement: Renames the subject after a linking verb (is, are, was, seem,
become).
o Example: Mr. Ade is a teacher. (Mr. Ade = a teacher)
 Object of a Preposition: Follows a preposition (in, on, at, for, with).
o Example: I gave the book to the teacher.
C. In-Depth Review of Pronouns
A Pronoun is a word used in place of a noun to avoid repetition.
1. Personal Pronouns (Subjective & Objective Case):
 Subjective Case: Used as the subject of a verb. (I, you, he, she, it, we, they)
o Example: She went to the market.
 Objective Case: Used as the object of a verb or preposition. (me, you, him, her, it, us,
them)
o Example: The teacher saw her. He gave the book to me.
 Rule: When you have a compound subject, always use the subjective case.
o Incorrect: "My friend and me are in JSS3."
o Correct: "My friend and I are in JSS3." (Test: "I am in JSS3." not "Me am in
JSS3.")
2. Possessive Pronouns: (Show ownership)
 Possessive Adjectives: Come before a noun. (my, your, his, her, its, our, their)
o Example: That is my book.
247
 Possessive Pronouns: Stand alone and replace the noun. (mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours,
theirs)
o Example: That book is mine.
 Rule: it's (with an apostrophe) means "it is." its (no apostrophe) is the possessive
pronoun.
3. Reflexive & Emphatic Pronouns: (myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves,
yourselves, themselves)
 Reflexive: The subject performs an action on itself. The pronoun is the object.
o Example: He cut himself.
 Emphatic: Used for emphasis only. The sentence still makes sense without it.
o Example: The principal himself came to our class.
4. Relative Pronouns: (Introduce a relative clause to describe a noun)
 who/whom: For people. ("The man who came...")
 which: For things/animals. ("The book which I read...")
 whose: For possession. ("The boy whose bag was stolen...")
 that: For people or things.

A. Introduction
This lesson revises two important essay types. While they are often mixed, they have different

 A Narrative Essay TELLS A STORY. Its main goal is to show a sequence of events

 A Descriptive Essay PAINTS A PICTURE. Its main goal is to make the reader

 Beginning (Exposition): Introduce characters and setting.
 Middle (Rising Action & Climax): The main problem or event happens.

3. Setting: The time and place.

 Key Element (The "Picture"):
1. Sensory Details (The 5 Senses): This is the most important part.
 Sight: What did it look like? (e.g., "the bright red, peeling paint")

 Sound: What did it sound like? (e.g., "the high-pitched, endless scraping")
 Smell: What did it smell like? (e.g., "the sharp, tangy smell of salt and

 Touch/Feel: What did it feel like? (e.g., "the rough, cracked surface")
 Taste: (If applicable) (e.g., "the bitter, metallic taste of the medicine")
 Language: Uses strong verbs and vivid adjectives (e.g., not "a nice car," but "a gleaming,
royal-blue sports car").
 Example Prompt: "My Favourite Place," "My Favourite Subject," "The Market on a

 Telling: "The man was angry."

B. In-Depth Review of Literary Terms
1. Plot (Revision):
 The sequence of events.
 Exposition (Start), Rising Action (Tension builds), Climax (Peak of tension), Falling

2. Characterization (Major vs. Minor):
 Major Characters: The story revolves around them.
o Protagonist: The main character, who the audience roots for.
o Antagonist: The character or force that opposes the protagonist.
 Minor Characters: They support the story but are not the main focus. They are there to
help or hinder the protagonist, or to reveal more about the major characters.
 Review:
o Dynamic: A character who changes significantly (in personality or belief) by the
end.
o Static: A character who does not change.
o Round: A complex, believable character with many traits.
o Flat: A simple, one-dimensional character (e.g., the "bully").

 The central message, moral, or idea about life that the author wants to convey.
256
 Theme is a full sentence, not one word.

o Theme: "The author shows that greed ultimately leads to self-destruction."
4. Tragic and Comic Elements (Genre):

faces a great struggle. The story ends in disaster (often death) for the protagonist, usually
because of their own Tragic Flaw (a weakness like pride, ambition, or jealousy).
o Example: A king who is so proud he refuses to listen to advice, and as a result,
loses his kingdom and his life.
 Comedy: A story with a happy, or at least a positive, ending. It is not always "funny,"
but it is not tragic. The protagonist overcomes the obstacles. In classic comedy, the story
often ends with a celebration, like a wedding.
o Example: A story about two people who misunderstand each other, get into
arguments, but finally resolve their differences and become friends or get married.

 Definition: The author's or playwright's specific choice of words.
 Purpose: Diction is the tool used to build everything else.
o Formal Diction: Using complex, serious, and professional language. (e.g., "We
must endeavour to ascertain the truth.")
o Informal Diction (Colloquial): Using casual, everyday language or slang. (e.g.,
"We have to find out what's going on.")
 Function in Literature:
o Reveals Character: An educated professor speaks with formal diction, while a
market seller might use colloquial diction.
o Sets Tone/Mood: Using dark, heavy, and sad words (e.g., "gloomy," "despair,"
"shattered") creates a tragic or sad mood.

1. SPEECH WORK: REVIEW OF DIPHTHONGS
Introduction: Speech sounds are broadly divided into vowels and consonants. Vowels are
sounds produced with no obstruction of air from the lungs. Vowels can be monophthongs (pure,
single vowel sounds, like /i:/ in 'see') or diphthongs (gliding vowel sounds).
What is a Diphthong? A diphthong is a single vowel sound that is formed by the combination
of two different vowel sounds. It starts with one vowel sound and "glides" into another within
the same syllable. There are eight common diphthongs in English.
The Eight English Diphthongs:
1. /eɪ/ (as in "say")
o Description: Starts from /e/ (like in 'bet') and glides towards /ɪ/ (like in 'sit').
o Examples: pain, rain, date, late, wait, say, great, weight, eight
2. /aɪ/ (as in "my")
o Description: Starts from /a/ (an open-front sound) and glides towards /ɪ/.
o Examples: my, fly, like, time, high, night, buy, eye, height
3. /ɔɪ/ (as in "boy")
o Description: Starts from /ɔ:/ (like in 'sore') and glides towards /ɪ/.
o Examples: boy, toy, joy, noise, voice, coin, oil, annoy
4. /aʊ/ (as in "now")
o Description: Starts from /a/ (an open-front sound) and glides towards /ʊ/ (like in
'put').
o Examples: now, how, cow, brown, down, out, shout, loud, mouth
5. /əʊ/ (as in "go") - (Note: Also written as /oʊ/ in American English)
o Description: Starts from the central vowel /ə/ (schwa) and glides towards /ʊ/.
o Examples: go, no, so, home, stone, road, boat, coat, though
6. /ɪə/ (as in "near")
o Description: Starts from /ɪ/ (like in 'sit') and glides towards the central vowel /ə/.
o Examples: near, here, deer, clear, fear, beer, weird, pierce

7. /eə/ (as in "hair")
o Description: Starts from /e/ (like in 'bet') and glides towards the central vowel /ə/.
o Examples: hair, air, chair, fair, pair, there, where, bear, share
8. /ʊə/ (as in "pure")
o Description: Starts from /ʊ/ (like in 'put') and glides towards the central vowel /ə/.
o Examples: pure, cure, tour, poor, sure, mature, during

2. GRAMMAR: REVIEW OF VERBS AND ADVERBS
A. Verbs
Definition: A verb is a word or group of words that expresses an action (e.g., run, eat, think) or a
state of being (e.g., is, are, was, feel, seem). It is the most important part of a sentence.
Types of Verbs:
1. Main Verbs (Lexical Verbs): These carry the main meaning of the sentence.
o Transitive Verbs: Require a direct object to complete their meaning. (e.g., "She
wrote a letter." The object is "a letter".)
o Intransitive Verbs: Do not require an object. (e.g., "He slept." "The bird sings.")
2. Auxiliary Verbs (Helping Verbs): These help the main verb to form tenses, moods, or
voices.
o Primary Auxiliaries: be, have, do (e.g., "He is running." "She has eaten." "Do
you understand?")
o Modal Auxiliaries: Express ability, permission, possibility, necessity, etc. (e.g.,
can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, must)
Verb Forms:
1. Finite Verbs: Show tense (past/present) and agree with a subject. (e.g., "He walks."
"They walked.")
2. Non-Finite Verbs: Do not show tense and do not agree with a subject.
o Infinitives: The base form, often with 'to'. (e.g., "I want to eat.")
o Gerunds: The '-ing' form of a verb used as a noun. (e.g., "Swimming is good
exercise.")
o Participles: Used as adjectives or to form tenses.
 Present Participle: (-ing) "The running boy fell."
 Past Participle: (-ed, -en) "The broken chair is mine."
261
B. Adverbs
Definition: An adverb is a word that modifies (describes or gives more information about) a
verb, an adjective, or another adverb. It answers questions like How? When? Where? How often?
To what extent?
Types of Adverbs:
1. Adverbs of Manner: (How?) e.g., slowly, quickly, carefully, well, badly
o "He drove carefully."
2. Adverbs of Place: (Where?) e.g., here, there, everywhere, outside, in
o "Please wait here."
3. Adverbs of Time: (When?) e.g., now, then, today, yesterday, soon
o "She will arrive soon."
4. Adverbs of Frequency: (How often?) e.g., always, often, sometimes, never, rarely
o "They often visit us."
5. Adverbs of Degree: (To what extent?) e.g., very, extremely, quite, too, almost
o "The food is very hot." (Modifies adjective 'hot')
o "He ran too quickly." (Modifies adverb 'quickly')
Position of Adverbs: The position of an adverb in a sentence can vary, but common patterns
include:
 Manner: Usually after the verb or object. ("He speaks English well.")
 Frequency: Usually before the main verb but after 'be'. ("He rarely complains." "He is
rarely late.")
 Time: Usually at the beginning or end of a clause. ("Today, we are going out." "We are
going out today.")

4. COMPOSITION: REVIEW OF ARGUMENTATIVE & EXPOSITORY ESSAYS
A. Argumentative Essay
Definition: An argumentative essay is a type of writing that aims to persuade the reader to
accept a particular point of view (your "argument" or "thesis"). It uses logic, evidence, and
reasoning to support this viewpoint and may also address opposing viewpoints.
Key Features:
 A Clear Thesis Statement: A single sentence in the introduction that states your main
argument. (e.g., "Mobile phones should be banned in secondary schools because they are
a distraction, promote cheating, and hinder social interaction.")
 Logical Arguments: Each body paragraph presents a single, clear reason supporting
your thesis.
 Evidence: You must support your arguments with facts, examples, statistics, or expert
opinions.
 Counter-Argument (Optional but strong): Acknowledging and refuting the opposing
view shows you have considered all sides.
 Formal Tone: Use formal language. Avoid slang or casual expressions.
 Strong Conclusion: Summarise your points and restate your thesis in a compelling way.
Structure:
1. Introduction: Grab the reader's attention, provide brief background, and state your thesis.
2. Body Paragraphs (2-3):
o Start with a topic sentence (your reason).
o Explain your reason.
o Provide evidence/examples.
3. (Optional) Counter-Argument Paragraph: State the opposing view and then show why
it is weak or wrong.
4. Conclusion: Restate your thesis, summarise your main points, and leave the reader with

B. Expository Essay
Definition: An expository essay is a type of writing that aims to explain, describe, define, or
inform (to "expose") a topic. It is not about arguing a point but about presenting information
clearly and objectively.
Key Features:
 Clear Topic: The essay focuses on one central topic.
 Factual Information: The content is based on facts, not personal opinions or emotions.
 Logical Organisation: Information is presented in a clear, easy-to-follow order.
 Objective Tone: The writer's feelings are kept out of the essay.

5. LITERATURE: REVIEW OF LITERARY TERMS IN POETRY
Introduction: Poets use special tools called literary devices (or figurative language) to make
their poems more vivid, meaningful, and memorable.
Key Literary Terms:
1. Simile: A direct comparison between two unlike things, using the words "like" or "as".
o Example: "He runs as fast as the wind." "Her smile was like sunshine."
2. Metaphor: An indirect or implied comparison between two unlike things, stating that
one thing is the other. It does not use "like" or "as".
o Example: "He is a lion in battle." "Her smile is sunshine."
3. Personification: Giving human qualities, feelings, or actions to inanimate objects,
animals, or ideas.
o Example: "The wind whispered through the trees." "My alarm clock shouted at
me."
4. Alliteration: The repetition of the same consonant sound at the beginning of words that
are close together.
o Example: "She sells seashells by the seashore." (Note: 'c' in 'cells' would not be
alliteration here as it makes an /s/ sound).
5. Assonance: The repetition of vowel sounds within words that are close together.
o Example: "The rain in Spain stays mainly on the plain." (Repetition of the /eɪ/
sound).
6. Irony: A contrast between what is said and what is meant, or between what is expected
and what actually happens.
o Verbal Irony: Saying the opposite of what you mean (sarcasm is a form of this).
(e.g., Looking at a messy room and saying, "You are so tidy!")
o Situational Irony: When the opposite of what is expected happens. (e.g., A fire
station burns down.)
o Dramatic Irony: When the audience knows something that a character in the
story does not.
7. Onomatopoeia: The use of words that imitate the sound they describe.
268
o Example: buzz, hiss, clang, moo, splash, boom
8. Rhyme: The repetition of similar sounds at the end of lines in a poem.
o Example: "The cat in the hat / Sat on the mat."
o Rhyme Scheme: The pattern of rhymes, labelled with letters (e.g., AABB,
ABAB).
9. Rhythm: The pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line of poetry, creating a
"beat".

A. Adjectives
Definition: An adjective is a word that describes or modifies a noun or a pronoun. It provides
more information about its qualities, characteristics, or state.
 Example: "The tall girl." (modifies 'girl') "He is smart." (modifies 'He')
Types & Order of Adjectives: When using multiple adjectives, they often follow a specific

Comparison of Adjectives: Adjectives have three forms:
1. Positive: The base form. (e.g., big, beautiful)
2. Comparative: Compares two things. (e.g., bigger, more beautiful)
3. Superlative: Compares three or more things. (e.g., biggest, most beautiful)
B. Conjunctions
Definition: A conjunction is a "joining" word. It connects words, phrases, or clauses.
 Example: "You and I." (connects words) "In the house or in the garden." (connects
phrases) "He is poor, but he is honest." (connects clauses)
Types of Conjunctions:
1. Coordinating: Connects two equal or independent clauses.
o FANBOYS: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So

2. Subordinating: Connects a dependent (subordinate) clause to an independent (main)
clause.
o Examples: because, although, if, when, while, since, after, before, unless
o "He left because he was angry." ('because he was angry' cannot stand alone)
3. Correlative: Pairs of conjunctions that work together.
o Examples: either...or, neither...nor, both...and, not only...but also
o "You can have either the rice or the beans."
C. Prepositions
Definition: A preposition is a word (or group of words) that shows the relationship between a
noun or pronoun and another word in the sentence. It often shows location, time, or direction.
 Example: "The book is on the table." (shows location) "He arrived at 9 pm." (shows time)
"She walked towards the school." (shows direction)
Common Prepositions:
 in, on, at, for, from, with, by, under, over, above, to, towards, through, since, during
D. Interjections

is often followed by an exclamation mark.

 "Wow! That is a beautiful car."
